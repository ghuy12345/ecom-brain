# Retention Flows and Segmentation

## What it is
Retention is the work of getting customers to come back quickly and profitably. Its real value is not "email revenue" but a bigger allowable customer acquisition cost: if each new cohort earns more gross profit in its first 90 days than the cohort before it, you can bid more for traffic and still out-earn competitors. At most brands only a small minority of first-time buyers ever order again, so the gap between a good and a poor retention system is large.

Measure it in lifetime gross profit (LTGP) over a fixed window (30, 90 or 180 days), not lifetime revenue. The ratio math that turns LTGP into a CAC target lives in [[LTGP to CAC Ratio and the CAC Death Spiral]]; this note covers the system that raises LTGP in the first place.

## When to use it
- You can already acquire customers at or near break-even and want room to scale.
- Repeat purchase is plausible (consumables, replenishment, a range with natural cross-sells).
- Your email platform reports a healthy "share of revenue", yet profit is not moving.

## How (steps)
1. **Check the retention hierarchy from the bottom up.** Owned media only amplifies what sits beneath it. In order: product quality (use survey or NPS data, not your own opinion), product portfolio (is there anything to buy next?), acquisition quality (very cheap marketplace buyers and deep-discount bargain hunters rarely return), and customer experience (how you handle problems matters more than whether they happen). Only then email and SMS.
2. **Find your constraint.** Three jobs drive retention: acquiring customers, converting first-time buyers into second-time buyers, and growing revenue from your best 20%. For most brands below eight figures a year, the first-to-second order step is the bottleneck and the strongest predictor of 90-day LTGP.
3. **Split every flow by customer type before anything else.** New customers get the most persuasive version (trust, price, brand introduction). Returning customers skip the introduction and get lighter incentives. Loyal or subscribed customers get the gentlest version, ideally with no discounts. Only after this split should you test cart value or product category branches.
4. **Prefer flows for personalisation and campaigns for reach.** Behaviour-triggered flows run around the clock and are where tailoring pays off. Campaigns should put excellent content in front of the widest possible audience; heavily personalised campaigns rarely repay the labour.
5. **Plan sends on an 80/20 split.** Roughly 80% of sends build attention (education, entertainment, social proof) and 20% chase a specific goal such as clearing excess stock. Only a few percent of a list is ready to buy on any day; reach sends keep the rest warm for sales, launches and restocks.
6. **Expand the engaged audience.** Define "engaged" against your buying cycle (for example opened or clicked within about six months, added to cart or ordered within a slightly longer window) and aim for at least half the list. Divide the unengaged contacts into random batches and include one batch in each engaged send; repeat monthly for several months, then mail the full list at peak season. Roughly 20-30% of each sampled batch tends to open and a share re-engage; a large retailer that had only ever mailed a fraction of the profiles it paid for beat its previous peak by about 40% this way.
7. **Segment with RFM against your own base.** Group customers into champions, loyal, recent, at-risk and needs-attention. Use it for re-engaging lapsed champions, for win-backs triggered by each customer's own churn pattern rather than a flat "120 days since purchase", and for a second-order push to recent buyers who are not yet loyal.
8. **Test against the constraint.** Test offers first, then flow structure and timing, then messaging. Subject-line tests rarely move profit.

## Example
Say two pet-supplement brands both acquire customers at a $40 CAC and earn $100 gross profit on the first order. One lifts 90-day LTGP to $130 through a strong second-order flow; the other reaches $112. The first can let CAC rise to $45, buy more customers than the second and still make more profit per cohort. Retention became an acquisition advantage.

## Common mistakes
- Treating "% returning customers" as a loyalty metric; it climbs as a brand ages and when new-customer acquisition weakens.
- Optimising for the email platform's attributed revenue instead of contribution margin. Use total owned-media share only as a health check.
- Computing LTGP with a flat assumed margin, or booking refunds against CAC instead of LTGP.
- Spending heavily on paid ads to existing customers. It can be incremental for fashion brands with weekly drops; hold it to a much tighter CPA target and budget it from frequency, audience size and CPM.
- Ignoring discount-acquired cohorts: they often look great in ad metrics but return far less at full price. Check cohorts by discount code (see [[Cohort LTV Modelling]]).
- Pushing VIP revenue with discounts when VIPs are not the constraint.
