# Giveaway Email Sequence

## What it is
A short, fixed run of emails that drives past customers into a survey giveaway, announces the winners and then thanks everyone else with a discount. The giveaway exists to collect honest customer feedback; the emails make it feel real and personal, and the final founder email turns the goodwill into revenue. This cadence is a sensible default rather than a proven optimum, so adapt it, but keep its logic.

## When to use it
- You have decided a survey giveaway is worth running and have built the sweepstake, the entry page and a segmented list of past purchasers (see [[Customer Survey Giveaways - When and How to Run One]]).
- You want a repeatable sequence you can build in a few hours.

## How (steps)
1. **Schedule five sends around a seven-day giveaway.**
   - Day 1: announcement, to past purchasers.
   - Day 3: reminder, to past purchasers.
   - Day 7: last chance, to past purchasers.
   - Day 8: giveaway closes, no send.
   - Day 9 (or day 8 if ready): winner announcement, to entrants only.
   - About a week after the winners email: the founder's thank-you with a discount code, to entrants minus winners.
   Any weekday works as a start. SMS alongside email should bring more entries faster, but test it rather than assume.
2. **Audit your existing emails.** Open recent campaigns and the best-converting flows, especially the welcome email, to judge palette, fonts and how much room you have to be creative.
3. **Use one structure for every designed email:** one designed hero graphic at the top with editable text underneath. For each email, swap only the hero graphic, the button colour, the link and the key numbers (how many prizes, what they are). Duplicate a template rather than rebuilding, and compress images before uploading.
4. **Match the brand.** Match your store's existing button colour and corner radius. Save the button as a reusable block so one link change updates every email; treat recurring text blocks the same way, but detach heading blocks once placed so each email can differ.
5. **Write each email for its job.**
   - Announcement: the email that matters most; feature the products involved, lead with the main prize, and stress that entry takes under a minute. With complex prize ladders, mention only the headline prize (for example "win up to a set amount of store credit").
   - Reminder: make it look clearly different so people do not skip it; this is where the complete prize ladder goes.
   - Last chance: the shortest, urgency only.
   - Winner announcement: echo the look of the announcement so people recognise what they entered a week earlier; leave names blank until the draw.
   - Founder email: plain text, no design. The message is simple: you did not win this time, thank you for your time and honesty, here is a code.
6. **Set up the founder email carefully.** Put the winners on their own list so you can exclude them. Send to entrants, exclude winners, turn off any "skip recently emailed" setting so nobody is dropped, and use the founder's first name as the sender. Create a single-use code per customer with no minimum spend, choose a percentage your margin supports, and set the code live a full day before the send. Test it in a real checkout and check the maths against the cart total.
7. **QA every email on mobile.** Stale prize numbers often survive in the mobile view. Check links, bold the code, remove stray spaces and send yourself a preview.

## Example
Say a candle brand runs a seven-day survey giveaway with three store-credit prizes. Monday's email shows the collection with "Share your thoughts in under a minute for a chance to win" and the top prize. Wednesday's reminder uses a different hero and lists all three prizes. Sunday's last-chance email is two lines. Tuesday's winner email mirrors Monday's design. A week later a plain-text note from the founder offers non-winners a one-time code, which the team activated the day before.

## Common mistakes
- Sending the winner announcement to every past purchaser rather than only entrants.
- Setting the code's start date to the day of the send; early clickers hit an invalid code.
- Stacking a large code with a site-wide promotion without checking margin, or forgetting an expiry date.
- Updating the desktop version but not the mobile one.
- Designing every email from scratch instead of swapping a hero image.
