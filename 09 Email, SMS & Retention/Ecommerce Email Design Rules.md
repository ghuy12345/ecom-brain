# Ecommerce Email Design Rules

## What it is
A repeatable set of layout, visual and conversion rules for promotional and lifecycle emails. Most ecommerce emails are opened on a phone, skimmed in seconds and judged before anyone scrolls. These rules make every email readable on mobile, focused on a single action and consistent with the brand, so you spend your effort on the message rather than reinventing the layout each time.

## When to use it
- Designing any campaign: launch, restock, sale, editorial, loyalty.
- Building or refreshing flows: welcome, cart recovery, post-purchase and win-back.
- Reviewing a designer's or agency's email before it ships.

## How (steps)
1. **Write a short brief before designing.** Confirm the campaign type, brand voice, the single hero message, the primary call to action and where it links, any secondary content, the audience segment, the send context (real urgency or scarcity) and the hero image. Do not make up products, prices or claims to fill space.
2. **Stack full-width bands in this order.** An optional preheader strip; a modest centred logo bar; a hero with a large headline, a one- or two-sentence subhead and one button; a single benefit section (pick one: bundle, comparison table, step-by-step usage, ingredient list or product grid); a softer secondary section with its own button; a closing band with the final push; and a dark footer with wordmark, a small grid of utility links, social icons, legal text and unsubscribe.
3. **Respect the technical limits.** Keep content to about 600px in a single column. The only side-by-side elements should be two-up product grids and two-column comparisons, and each has to collapse into one column on phones. Check every email at phone width. Body text around 16px (never below 14px, legal text no smaller than 11px) and tap targets at least 44px tall. Use table-based, inline-styled HTML with fallback fonts and coded buttons rather than modern CSS layout. Keep important text live rather than baked into images, and check dark mode so logos and text do not vanish.
4. **Keep the visual system tight.** Two or three background colours rotated by section, at most one photographic background, cards on a contrasting band with no deeper nesting. One or two typefaces: a bold display face for headlines, a clean sans for body text at a comfortable line height, never justified. Small uppercase labels above section headlines help scanning. Generous section padding. One button shape and one button colour for the whole email. Real product or lifestyle photography only.
5. **Apply the conversion rules.** One message and one destination per email, with the button repeated two or three times using the same wording. Logo, headline, subhead and button sit in the first screen on mobile. Open on the offer or the benefit rather than the brand story, and allow no more than about two sentences of body before a button. If there is a discount, show it in the hero and echo it in the subject line. Mention genuine scarcity once, near the last button, and add one line of risk reversal there (guarantee, returns, shipping threshold). Use one specific attributed quote or one hard number as proof, and lists with numbers or icons so readers can count the benefits.
6. **Treat subject line and preheader as part of the design.** Aim for a subject of roughly 50 characters and a preheader of 40 to 90 characters that adds to the subject instead of repeating it. Use personalisation tokens only when they add something, always with a fallback.
7. **Edit the copy.** Write in the second person and present tense with an active voice, and keep buttons verb-led at two to four words. Swap adjectives for numbers and specifics, and cut hype words.

## Example
A skincare brand restocking a bestseller might send: subject "Back in stock: the night serum", preheader "Last batch sold out in days, so bundles go first". The hero headline states the restock, the benefit section shows three bundle cards each with a photo, a short "what's inside" list and its own button, and the closing band notes that stock is limited. For an educational send, the same brand would swap in a contrarian headline, a two-column comparison of its formula against typical serums, a four-step routine grid and a lifestyle photo before the closing button. A soft-sell variant uses a full-width photo, a curated two-by-two product grid and a "not sure which to pick? Just reply" block.

## Common mistakes
- Body layouts with more columns than a two-up grid, which collapse badly on phones.
- Two competing hero buttons or more than one button colour.
- Walls of star ratings or logos instead of one real, attributed quote.
- Gradients behind body text, text shadows, nested boxes and three or more fonts.
- Tiny text, placeholder copy left in, or claims nobody verified.
- Fake countdown timers or invented scarcity.
