# Subscription Programs - Intro Offers to Recurring Revenue

## What it is
A subscription turns a one-off purchase into a recurring cycle. Done well, subscribers can generate several times the gross profit of one-time buyers over the following three to six months, which lets you pay more to acquire them. The structure has three parts: an introductory first order that gets the customer in, a quick move onto a regular refill cycle, and a set of KPIs and policies that keep the program profitable and fair.

## When to use it
Subscriptions are an amplifier, not a starting point. Check readiness first:
- The product is consumable, people like it (reviews and repeat orders confirm it) and quality is verified, ideally lab-tested for ingestibles. Unhappy subscribers become chargebacks.
- First orders are already profitable and you have found bundles and angles that work.
- Delivery is fast in your main markets. Shipping that takes ten days or more drives churn, so set up local fulfilment first.
- Cash flow can absorb a first-order loss or lower front-end conversion.

Do not launch a new supplement as subscription-first unless it is genuinely unique. Children's products often struggle: kids outgrow them and parents resist adding another recurring task. The exceptions fix a serious, emotional problem the parent badly wants solved.

## How (steps)
1. **Design the intro offer.** Treat the opening order as a cost of acquiring the customer: buy two get one, buy three get three, or a free gift. Free product usually beats a price discount: it costs you COGS rather than revenue, avoids confusing rebill prices, and does not anchor the customer on a half-price monthly cost or attract the most price-sensitive buyers.
2. **Make the first quantity make sense.** A larger first shipment should have a product reason, such as a loading phase in month one for a supplement where that helps.
3. **Move to a 28-30 day cycle quickly** and make clear that gifts apply to the first order only.
4. **Set the ongoing discount.** Ongoing subscriber discounts usually sit between 20% and 30%. Think about raising list price before adding the subscription discount so margin survives. Some brands go as far as roughly half off the first order, which only works when lifetime value is proven.
5. **Decide where the subscription appears.** Lead your ads with the product's value, not with "subscribe". On the page, a front-end subscription option tends to win more subscribers than an upsell after checkout. If cash is tight, skip the front-end dip: build cash first and offer subscription after purchase to gather data. Split-test the subscription widget; a subscription seldom lifts conversion without an outstanding offer, and offers that look too good can read as a scam.
6. **Run separate KPIs.** One-time buyers should be profitable on the first order. Subscribers can be acquired at break-even or below when cohort LTV supports it. Either point copies of winning ads at a subscription page that has its own targets or treat subscriptions as a secondary objective, and count subscriptions in your store backend rather than in ad-platform reports.
7. **Accept a measured dip.** Front-end conversion may fall (say from 3% to 2%) when subscription is introduced; keep it only if lifetime value more than compensates, and never bet the whole business on it.
8. **Keep it honest.** State the rebill amount and the number of deliveries at checkout, remind customers ahead of the first renewal and give them an easy way to change quantity or cancel, and disclose that gifts are first-order only.
9. **Review cohorts.** Track LTV, churn and drop-off by cohort and tune for the best mix of recurring revenue, LTV and COGS.

## Example
Say a greens-powder brand has profitable one-time orders and steady reorders. It launches a front-end option: subscribe and get the first month as a two-pack plus a free shaker, then a monthly single pack at 20% off a slightly raised list price. Ads stay focused on the product's benefit. Front-end conversion drops a little, but subscribers are tracked on their own break-even target, and the 90-day cohort shows enough extra gross profit to justify the dip. Gift and rebill terms are stated at checkout, and a reminder goes out before the first renewal.

## Common mistakes
- Adding a subscription before the product, offer and angles are proven.
- Judging subscribers and one-time buyers against a single shared target.
- Renaming the subscription with a softer label; it changes nothing and can feel evasive.
- Attaching a subscription requirement to a trial-style offer and killing purchases; test the offer without the requirement too.
- Weak digital membership perks as the main value; community or a strong product reason works better.
- Hiding rebill terms or making cancellation hard (see [[Product Integrity and Claims That Outgrow Reality]]).
