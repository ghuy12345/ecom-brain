# Advertorial Landing Pages

## What it is
An advertorial is a pre-sale page styled as editorial content: a feature article, a news-style report or a first-person account. It sits between the ad and the offer page, and the reader experiences it as something they discovered rather than a sales pitch. It works because the article format lowers defences, the narrative builds investment before any product appears, the problem-agitate-solve arc (state the problem, sharpen the pain, reveal the answer) makes the solution feel like relief, and the reader feels understood in a way no product page manages.

The unique mechanism holds the whole page together. The hidden cause of the problem (why it lingers, why earlier fixes failed) belongs in the agitation. The reason your solution works belongs in the product section. Leave the mechanism out and you are left with a nice story that ends in an unrelated sales link.

## When to use it
Strong fit: cold traffic that needs teaching; complicated problems with skeptical buyers (health, supplements, skincare with a genuine mechanism); purchases that demand high trust; jaded markets where people have tried everything; problem-aware readers who have stopped believing anything works.

Weak fit: quick visual purchases like clothing or decor (send them to the product page); very price-driven markets, where a short listicle closes faster; shoppers already comparing options (use a comparison page); and pages that must ship within a day or two. Against a listicle, an advertorial takes longer, leans harder on story and handles complex mechanisms better.

## How (steps)
1. **Write an editorial headline.** Options include a discovery, a first-person story, an insider exposé, a hidden truth, a contrarian take or a warning. A cold reader should pause because it seems to describe them. First-person stories often suit personal problems such as health or appearance; exposé angles often suit people who feel let down by mainstream advice. Test styles head to head.
2. **Hook in three sentences.** Open with a concrete scene, an unexpected fact, a bold claim that cuts against common belief, or the thoughts already running in the reader's head.
3. **Describe the problem.** Use the customer's own words, show how it intrudes on ordinary days, acknowledge how it feels and mention the fixes they have tried. Write like a friend who has lived it, not like a clinician or a marketer.
4. **Agitate.** Paint the next year if nothing changes, point out that most people quietly put up with it, connect it to how they see themselves, and name an outside culprit that made the problem worse than it used to be.
5. **Open a knowledge gap (optional, strong).** Reveal what every past attempt overlooked: a group of people who never get the problem, a finding the industry ignored, something professionals are not trained on. This is where the problem's mechanism lands.
6. **Make the shift.** Bring in the product as the way to act on the cause the reader now accepts, for example: after learning the real cause, the narrator went looking for something that dealt with it directly.
7. **Explain product and mechanism.** Plain words, benefits before specs, a clear contrast with past attempts, then back to the reader's story.
8. **Weave proof through the narrative.** Short case stories, quotes that echo the reader's own phrasing, brief before/after accounts, expert backing. Keep it credible and restrained.
9. **Answer objections inside the story.** Voice the doubt ("this probably sounds familiar...") and respond. Defuse price by setting it against the money already spent on things that failed. Save the FAQ for leftovers.
10. **Invite rather than push.** Calls to action such as "see if it suits you" or "check availability", with the guarantee mentioned. Put them after the story, after the strongest proof and under the main case study. Avoid oversized "buy now" buttons.

**Voice:** first person, contractions, hard specifics (how many months, how much money), feelings plus physical detail. No corporate tone, no passive voice.

## Example
Hypothetical: a sleep-supplement advertorial opens "For two years I woke at 3 a.m. every night, until a pharmacist explained what my tablets were missing." It covers the foggy afternoons, then the slow cost to work and mood, casts late-night screen light as the villain, reveals that the common form of the mineral absorbs poorly, and only then presents the product as the better-absorbed form. Stories from other early-wakers follow, then a gentle "see if it's available near you" button.

## Common mistakes
- Swerving abruptly from story to sales pitch.
- Dropping the mechanism.
- Ad-speak: hype words, "clinically proven" with no citation, fuzzy customer counts. Use real figures and claims you can verify.
- Spending more words on the product than on the reader's experience.
- Leaving out the villain, which is often the strongest trust builder.
- A headline that reads like an ad.
- A flat emotional line. The reader should travel from frustration, to hope, to conviction, to action.

Final check: would a reader take it for a real article?
