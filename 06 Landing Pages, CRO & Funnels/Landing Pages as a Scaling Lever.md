# Landing Pages as a Scaling Lever

## What it is
The post-click experience is one of the most under-used levers in paid social. Many large DTC brands route a big portion of paid clicks, often a third to well over half, to purpose-built landing pages instead of product pages or the homepage. The reason is simple: an ad makes a specific promise, and a page built to continue that promise converts the click better than a general store page.

The effect is not a small conversion tweak. When a proven ad is pointed at a congruent page, ROAS can improve enough that the platform is willing to give that ad several times the budget. A merely average ad can end up taking most of the account's budget. Treat a good page as a way to unlock scale, not as a design exercise.

## When to use it
Pages and ads are both levers, but they come in order, and where you are in that order decides which one gets your effort.

**Ads first while you are still searching.** If you do not yet have an angle that spends consistently, effort is better spent finding winning ads. A congruent page typically adds something like 10-30% to performance; a new winning ad can move results several-fold; a better offer can do even more. In this phase, produce more ads and send them to a simple product page. That also keeps the test clean: if the ad fails on a plain page, you know the ad is the problem. Do not build or test dedicated landing pages yet.

**Pages second, as a scaling unlock once an angle is proven.** When an angle is already taking real budget and starting to plateau, a dedicated page is one of the cheapest ways to get more out of it. You are improving an asset that is already proven, so the upside is concentrated where spend already flows.

In practice: new angles go to the simple product page; once an angle proves itself, build a page for it, starting cheap by copying a general page and rewriting it for the angle (often under an hour); save slow, elaborate builds like advertorials for angles that keep spending week after week.

## How (steps)
1. **Confirm you have a winner.** Pick an ad with consistent spend at or near target (see [[Choosing Which Ads Deserve a Landing Page]]).
2. **Rule out the ad as the problem.** An impressive hook rate is not proof of a good ad, since an attention-grabbing opening can draw the wrong people. Check that spend, CTR and conversion all support it.
3. **Build the page around that ad's promise and angle.** Messaging matters more than design: speak to the pain in the customer's own words (see [[Ad-to-Page Congruency]]).
4. **Send the same ad to the new page** and scale if it beats the old destination (see [[Testing Landing Pages Through Ad Spend]]).
5. **Keep a page cadence.** One person can usually produce about one page a week; a small team two to four. Iterations of existing high-spend pages are cheap and worth doing.
6. **Do not default to pages when you stall.** Big jumps usually come from fresh creative and stronger offers. Pages are decisive mainly when the current page is clearly poor, such as a cluttered affiliate-style page replaced with a clean one.

## Example
Hypothetical: a sleep-aid brand has one angle ("for people who wake at 3 a.m.") spending steadily at target into its product page, plus a dozen weaker ads. The product page lists eight benefits, with waking at night sixth. The team duplicates its evergreen page, rewrites the headline, story, reviews and FAQ around night waking, and sends the same ad there. ROAS rises enough that the platform lets the ad take roughly three times its previous daily budget. Meanwhile the weaker ads stay on the evergreen page until one of them earns its own build.

## Common mistakes
- Building or testing dedicated pages before you have an angle that spends.
- Treating hook rate as proof that an ad deserves a page.
- Polishing page design while the message is still wrong.
- Assuming every plateau is a landing page problem.
- Crediting a page for a jump that coincided with a promotion; concurrent changes confound the read.
