# What CRO Is and Is Not

## What it is
Conversion rate optimisation (CRO) is the work of improving the path from click to purchase so the business can take more traffic profitably. The name misleads: the goal is not a higher store conversion rate, it is more sales at a scale you can sustain. Those two often move in opposite directions. When you push more cold traffic into a store, the blended conversion rate usually drops even while revenue and profit grow. If you judged that move by conversion rate, you would call a good decision a failure.

Most of the gains come from the heavy levers: what you sell, how you package the deal, what you say and what you show. Button colours, badge placements and other micro-tweaks only pay back when a store already processes a very large number of orders, because tiny percentage gains need huge volume to be worth anything (or even to be measurable).

There are three broad ways to do CRO:

1. **No testing.** You ship what you believe is the best version. Fast and cheap, but you get no signal. When such changes flop, a build error is the usual culprit, although now and then the idea itself was poor.
2. **Classic split testing.** Two or more versions of a page run side by side with traffic divided between them. You need statistical significance (confidence the gap is not chance) and power (the result is likely to hold). That takes hundreds to thousands of orders per variant, at least a couple of weeks per test, and a team to design, build and QA each test. It suits large stores, roughly those doing several hundred orders a day.
3. **Ad-focused CRO.** You create pages and offers for particular ads and measure them by ROAS or CPA inside the ad account. You do not wait for significance; if the return is good you scale immediately. This works at every size.

## When to use it
- When you have a working ad and want it to take more budget. A page built for that ad's promise often lifts returns more than any on-page tweak.
- When you are deciding where to spend testing effort. Start with the levers that can move results by multiples, not by fractions of a percent.
- Not yet, if your store is bare-bones. A beginner mostly needs a site that works and makes sense; get that before you think about tests.

## How (steps)
1. **Pick the right scoreboard.** Judge changes by revenue or profit per visitor and by account-level ROAS or CPA, not store conversion rate (see [[Revenue and Profit Per Session]]).
2. **Ask the purpose question.** For any tactic, ask whether it helps the business scale. If not, skip it.
3. **Understand the customer first.** Knowing what the buyer needs and doubts makes every journey fix obvious.
4. **Work the big levers in order.** Clarity, copy, offer, proof, friction (see [[The CRO Priority Order - Clarity, Copy, Offer, Proof, Friction]]).
5. **Choose your testing approach by volume.** Below a few hundred orders a day, test pages through your ads ([[Testing Landing Pages Through Ad Spend]]). Above that, classic split tests become viable ([[Reading Split-Test Results - Sample Size and Power]]).
6. **Move fast.** If a single landing page takes one or two months to launch, you are moving too slowly. Ship many pages quickly and let the best become the main destination.
7. **Budget for QA.** Checking that every version actually works is a hidden but large cost in every approach.

## Example
Say a pet-supplement brand sends all its ads to the homepage at a 3% conversion rate. It builds a dedicated page for its best ad and starts sending colder audiences to it. Store conversion rate falls to 2.2% because the traffic is colder, but ROAS on that ad climbs from 1.5 to 2.4 and spend doubles. By the conversion-rate scoreboard this looks worse; by the business scoreboard it is a clear win.

A second case shows why order counts matter. An A/A test (two identical pages) can easily show a 10% "lift" and a visible AOV gap after a hundred orders per side. Nothing changed; it is noise. Small samples behave like a few coin flips that happen to land mostly heads.

## Common mistakes
- Making store conversion rate the target and the yardstick for tests.
- Chasing tricks before the product, offer, message and content are sorted.
- Running classic split tests on a store with too few orders to ever reach a reliable answer.
- Believing projected "extra profit" from a testing dashboard that never appears in your bank balance. Rely on results you can see at the account level.
- Expecting ad-based tests to detect small differences. They are reliable only for big, unmistakable lifts, because ROAS on a fresh page bounces around a lot.
