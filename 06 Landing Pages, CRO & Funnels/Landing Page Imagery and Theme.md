# Landing Page Imagery and Theme

## What it is
Two linked ideas about how a landing page looks. **Imagery** (photos, GIFs, video) exists to reinforce the message, not to fill space; each asset should prove the claim next to it. **Theme** (fonts, colour roles, button styles, spacing) exists to keep the page consistent, so nobody is left puzzling over mismatched heading sizes or button styles. Neither is about beauty. A plain page that thoughtfully answers the reader's questions outperforms a beautiful page with nothing to say, because buyers weigh the message far above the styling.

## When to use it
- Setting up a page builder for a new brand (theme first).
- Choosing visuals for each section of a new page.
- When a page looks "busy" and someone wants to add more decoration.

## How (steps)
**Imagery**
1. **Lock messaging first.** Then choose imagery to sharpen it, and expect imagery to change some copy; edit the two together.
2. **Source in this order:** the brand's own authentic content; new photos you shoot or request; edits of existing photos in a simple design tool (background removed, composited, labelled); AI-generated images, which are now good enough for landing pages; generic stock last, and ideally never.
3. **Pair each claim with a visual that proves it.** For "real ingredients", a shot where individual ingredients are visible beats a plain bowl. For "zero added sugar", show the naturally sweet ingredients. For "10 g protein", a clean callout. For "ready in minutes", a short sequence of the steps.
4. **Show, don't tell.** Dig through the brand's content library for unused video; a prep clip cut into a GIF often proves a point better than any photo.
5. **Keep a set consistent.** Same framing and the same label style across related images matters more than perfection.
6. **Build a rhythm,** alternating motion, layouts and photo styles so neighbouring sections differ.
7. **For the hero,** a cut-out product photo placed on a simple textured backdrop works well, with the message, not the product, as the main selling point.

**Theme**
1. Fonts come first, followed by the logo and a square site icon.
2. Define colour **roles**, not literal colours: which brand colour plays "dark section background", which plays "light section background", text colours, one accent.
3. Define the main and secondary button styles once (colours, border, radius, padding, font).
4. Publish the theme (twice if the builder caches) and check that templates inherit it.
5. Fix by hand what the theme cannot, such as a white logo variant on dark sections.

## Example
Hypothetical: a ready-meal brand's first draft used a stock photo of a smiling family and gradients behind every heading. The rebuild swaps the stock image for the brand's own kitchen footage turned into a GIF, gives each of six benefit blocks a photo that proves that specific benefit with the same circular label style, and removes the gradients. The page looks simpler and reads faster.

## Common mistakes
- Adding images because a section "needs one".
- Stock photos with no connection to the claim beside them.
- Pictures copied from search engines; savvy shoppers spot them (browsing search results for inspiration is fine).
- Spending time on gradients, arrows and effects that do not lift conversion.
- Inconsistent text colours and heading sizes that make the brand look sloppy.
- Fiddling with exact padding and corner radius by eye.
- Treating the theme as the finished page; it only gives you a rough template.
- Fabricated founder imagery or stories. If there is no real story, do not invent one.
