# The CRO Priority Order - Clarity, Copy, Offer, Proof, Friction

## What it is
A working order for improving a store or landing page once you are measuring the right number. Most so-called CRO jumps to interface tweaks. This order starts with what the buyer understands and believes, and treats design as the thing that reinforces it. Conveniently, the same order also describes a good page from top to bottom.

The number to watch is revenue per visitor (conversion rate x AOV), and contribution per visitor (revenue per visitor x gross margin). Conversion rate alone is an output of many off-page inputs (category, price, traffic temperature, how much the ad already educated) and is easy to game: switch off cold ads and it jumps overnight while revenue collapses. Never compare it across brands. Use it as a diagnostic.

It still matters, though. CAC equals spend divided by (sessions x conversion rate), so a better conversion rate directly lowers acquisition cost, and it costs decisions rather than money. Just do not expect to double it often.

## When to use it
- When you inherit a store or page and need to know what to fix first.
- When a team has a long list of ideas and needs a ranking.
- After any rebuild, as a review checklist.

## How (steps)
Rank every idea with an ICE score: impact, confidence and ease, each out of 10, multiplied. Work from the top down, but within this sequence:

1. **Clarity and hierarchy.** On a phone (where most visitors are) the first screen has to tell people what the product is, why it beats alternatives, why to act today and why to believe you. Arrange elements by how much they persuade: the core promise, then proof, then price, then the button. Keep size and quantity selectors out of the first view or push buyers to the larger bundle. Add a scroll cue. Fix page speed; it is one of the easiest wins. Clarity fixes are quick, low-risk and seldom need testing.
2. **Continuity.** When the persona, offer or angle in the ad changes, the page should change too. One generic page for ten angles means each visitor finds their problem listed as one benefit among many. Specific pages beat broad ones (see [[Ad-to-Page Congruency]]).
3. **Copy.** Make the copy about the reader rather than the item. Challenge each line with "so what?". Open with the result and use the feature as the supporting reason. Position yourself as the only option for this buyer, using reviews and research to find claims competitors cannot make. Push table-stakes points (free shipping, good service) small and low. Near the end, handle objections to the cheaper or familiar alternative, and fill the FAQ with genuine objections rather than policy boilerplate.
4. **Offer.** Increase what the offer feels worth while keeping its cost flat; the difference is your margin. Reframing (a routine bundle, a seasonal set) can beat a straight discount. Gifts with purchase, bundles and well-set free-shipping thresholds lift AOV (see [[Post-Purchase Upsells and AOV Levers]]).
5. **Proof.** Getting from zero to roughly 80-100 reviews matters a lot; beyond that the returns fade. Sort photo and video reviews first. Match proof to the specific claim rather than piling on generic praise (see [[Proof Types and Placement on Pages]]). Footer badge walls do little; a guarantee reverses risk but does not differentiate.
6. **Friction.** Most carts are abandoned, many by people just browsing. The fixable causes: unexpected fees, slow shipping, lack of trust, compulsory accounts, long forms, vague returns and a final price revealed too late. Default to guest checkout, put express pay at the top, cut fields, state the returns policy plainly and run abandonment flows.

Whatever your size, run a heatmap or session-recording tool.

## Example
Say a skincare brand tests five angles in ads, all pointing at one product page. Clarity check: the first mobile view shows a size dropdown and a long brand paragraph. Continuity check: the "adult acne" ad lands on a page listing acne fifth of eight benefits. Fixes in ICE order: move the value proposition, rating and price above the fold; build a separate page for the acne angle; rewrite the headline from ingredients to outcome; filter reviews to acne results. Only then does the team look at the bundle offer and checkout.

## Common mistakes
- Rating a page purely on conversion rate.
- Headlines that lead with features, and pages that open on table stakes.
- Many different ads funnelled into one generic page.
- Badge walls instead of proof that matches the claim.
- Split-testing small changes with far too few orders (see [[Reading Split-Test Results - Sample Size and Power]]).
- Pushing conversion with claims the product cannot deliver. It shows up later as returns, chargebacks and weak repeat purchase.
