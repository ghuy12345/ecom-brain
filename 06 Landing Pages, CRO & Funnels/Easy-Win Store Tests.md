# Easy-Win Store Tests

## What it is
A backlog of store changes that are cheap to make and often pay back, ordered roughly from table stakes to higher leverage. Some are simply fixes you should make; others are worth split-testing because adding them can also break a page.

## When to use it
- On a new or neglected store before more ambitious tests.
- When traffic is too low for big tests but you still want steady improvements.
- After an audit has surfaced obvious gaps.

## How (steps)
1. **Policies.** Shipping, returns and refund pages should exist, be clear and be specific to your store, not a generic template. People who read policy pages have purchase intent, so these pages are conversion surface. Many jurisdictions require them by law, so confirm the rules where you sell.
2. **Homepage first screen.** Hero, headline and where reviews appear carry outsized weight. Test homepage headlines continually; they are one of the cheapest lifts available.
3. **Product image carousel.** Pull information buried lower on the page up into the images: a size or scale shot, the product opened, in use, on a plain background, a short demo, the product in context.
4. **Research-backed FAQs** on product pages and the homepage (method below). Split-test the launch, because a new block can break layouts.
5. **Mixed reviews.** Publish three- and four-star reviews, even a two-star with a good public reply. Filter on tone, not star rating. Mixed reviews make the five-star ones believable. A dedicated reviews app that requests reviews by email usually beats a weak native review system; personalise the request and consider a small incentive where allowed.
6. **Size guides per product.** One store-wide sizing table helps nobody buying, say, socks. Remove the logistical doubt.
7. **Advertise the guarantee you already give.** If you refund unhappy customers anyway, say so plainly on the page, and keep honouring it.
8. **Shipping tests.** Test free-shipping threshold and flat rate as separate variants against the control, changing one thing per variant (details below).

**FAQ method**
- Choose questions whose answers would change a purchase decision; the test is persuasion, not how often it is asked.
- Ask about ten people to browse the store and tell you what they would want to know, or what information would make them buy.
- Always include: the guarantee, shipping time and cost, and the refund or exchange policy.
- Add one deliberate brand question ("What makes us different?").
- Use deep, specific questions (materials, heating time, cleaning, chemicals); they signal quality. Useful starting questions cover the mechanism, ease of use, the reason the product exists, time to results, durability, who it suits, the reasoning behind the price, and why it beats the alternative.
- Customer survey answers supply the real wording for price and differentiation questions.

**Shipping test design**
- Hypothetical: the control charges $7.50 with free shipping from $250. Variant A lowers only the free-shipping line, to $199. Variant B lowers only the flat fee, to $5.
- Shoppers see rate names, so keep them identical in every variant; only the amounts should differ.
- Check that cart banners mentioning the threshold update, since test tools may not edit them.
- QA each variant with an empty cart, add a qualifying product and confirm the rate at checkout.
- With three arms sharing the traffic and roughly 300 orders needed per arm, expect to wait for 900 or more orders.
- Shipping tests usually need a higher tool tier with profit tracking.

## Example
Hypothetical: a camping-gear store adds an FAQ to its tent page using questions gathered from ten friends: "Can I pitch it alone?", "What happens in heavy rain?", "Which floor surfaces does it suit?" It also publishes a three-star review complaining about the carry bag, with a public reply offering a replacement. Both go live as a split test, and the variant wins on revenue per visitor.

## Common mistakes
- Expecting large lifts from FAQs everywhere. Big lifts are most likely on stores that have no FAQ at all.
- Generic FAQs that restate policy instead of answering buying questions.
- Hiding all critical reviews.
- Forgetting to QA after developers edit the site.
- Naming shipping rates with internal test wording.
