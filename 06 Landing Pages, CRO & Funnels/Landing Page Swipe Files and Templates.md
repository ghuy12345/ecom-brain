# Landing Page Swipe Files and Templates

## What it is
A swipe file is a saved collection of real landing pages that brands send paid traffic to, kept for structure and idea reference. Ad-spy tools that show destination pages (not just ads), and page libraries bundled with some page builders, both reveal which pages large advertisers actually run. You only need one of the two.

The purpose is understanding, not copying. No layout wins by itself; what you put inside the listicle or advertorial is what sells. Pouring your information into someone else's layout will not produce a winner on its own.

Two patterns stand out when you study enough pages. First, brands at scale often run several pages for one product, a **page per argument**: a discount page, a reasons-to-switch page, a numbered-reasons page, a page fronted by an expert, a page for women only or for one specific body area. Each matches a different ad argument. Second, the best template is the one with evidence behind it: the page carrying the largest share of a comparable brand's traffic.

## When to use it
- Before building a first landing page for a brand.
- When choosing a template for a new angle.
- When you need section ideas for a page that has plateaued.

## How (steps)
1. **Find pages.** Look up a brand or theme in your ad-spy tool or page library. For each brand note its ad count, top ads and how traffic spreads across its pages. Filter libraries by page type, browse by brand, or search directly. Save or follow brands worth tracking.
2. **Review every page a brand runs, not just one.** Count them and record the audience or argument behind each.
3. **Include off-niche brands** purely for structure and how they explain a product.
4. **Capture properly.** Open each page in a new tab (a VPN avoids country redirects). Switch the browser's developer tools to a phone view, reload, scroll through so every section loads, expand accordions, then take a full-page screenshot with an extension. Cut tall captures into roughly four pieces to keep them legible, and place them in order on a design board, grouped per page. Always store the page address beside the capture.
5. **Annotate.** Write down the section sequence and the logic behind it, then note blocks you could adapt for your own brand.
6. **Collect offers too:** first-order discounts, bundles with gifts, free-shipping thresholds.
7. **Pick a template by evidence.** Start from your locked angle. Find the comparable brand's top page by traffic share with a traffic-analytics tool, and check what its longest-running ads link to. Map how its sections and copy progress.
8. **Check authenticity.** Make sure the layout rests on something genuinely true for your brand. A founder or shared-struggle layout needs a real story behind it.
9. **Adapt, do not force.** Skip template blocks that do not fit and optimise the ones you keep.

## Example
Hypothetical: a pet joint-supplement brand studies a competitor that runs five pages for one product: a senior-dog story, a vet-endorsed page, a before/after page, an offer page and a large-breed page. Traffic data shows the senior-dog story carries the biggest share, a non-obvious winner. The brand adapts the story structure because its founder genuinely started the company after her old dog's mobility declined, and adds an ingredient block borrowed from an off-niche food page: what it is, why it is included, what it does for the dog.

## Common mistakes
- Copying pages word for word.
- Treating a tool's traffic-share numbers as exact without knowing its data source.
- Believing a page library captures a brand's full history of pages.
- Picking a template because it looks nice rather than because it carries traffic.
- Using a story template without a true story behind it.
- Working from one enormous screenshot that blurs when you zoom.
