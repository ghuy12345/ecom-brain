# Alternative Pre-Sale Page Types

## What it is
Beyond advertorials and listicles, five pre-sale formats are worth keeping in your testing rotation. Each earns belief in a different way:

| Type | Typical awareness | Trust lever | Fits best |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder story | Problem- or solution-aware | A personal connection | Premium or mission-driven brands with a genuine origin |
| Expert authority | Problem- or solution-aware | Outside credibility | Health, supplement, wellness and skincare brands |
| Comparison | Solution- or product-aware | Clear differentiation | Busy categories with well-known rivals |
| Customer review | Solution- or product-aware | Weight of social proof | Products with hundreds of detailed reviews |
| Science/mechanism | Problem- or solution-aware | Logic and evidence | Skeptical, well-informed buyers |

## When to use it
- When a listicle or advertorial has stalled and you want a different trust lever to test against it.
- When the brand owns a specific asset (a true origin story, a credentialed expert, a deep review base, a real mechanism) that one of these formats puts front and centre.

## How (steps)
**Founder story.** Open on the moment the founder hit the problem, then the struggle (what they tried, what flopped, what it cost them), the breakthrough, the purpose behind the brand, the product, stories from customers who went through the same change, and a warm invitation to buy. The founder's own change is the centre of the page; the product is simply what made it possible. Use real details and failures. The founder should resemble the buyer and appear in photos or video; faceless founder pages tend to fall flat. Only use this format if the story is true; if the brand began purely as a business opportunity, pick another.

**Expert authority.** Present the expert and their qualifications, the pattern they keep noticing with patients or clients, the problem in their professional framing, why the usual fixes fall short, what they advise, the product, supporting evidence, customer results and the call to action. The expert has to exist and be checkable; a made-up authority ruins trust and invites legal trouble. Choose an expert the audience already trusts (a natural-health practitioner, a physician, a dermatologist, a physiotherapist, a dietitian, a researcher). Let them lead with what they have observed rather than a sales line. Their explanation of the cause and of why the product helps is the most trusted copy you can put on a page. Add a headshot, a highlighted quote and any published work; video is especially strong.

**Comparison.** Lead with the idea that products in the category differ more than people think, show where typical options fall short, lay out the buying criteria (which you choose), then the comparison table, a closer look at the biggest differences, stories from people who switched, the offer and the call to action. Choose criteria you honestly win on, include your mechanism as a row nobody else can tick, and add cost per use if it favours you. Comparing against "most brands" rather than naming rivals keeps legal risk down. Never misstate what a named competitor does, and be concrete about what they are missing.

**Customer review.** Open with a big proof number, the overall rating and exact review count, three to five detailed transformations, reviews sorted by problem or buyer type, a short mechanism note, a filterable wall of further reviews, the offer and the call to action. You need on the order of a hundred strong reviews to start, several hundred to do it well. Give each featured review a first name, location, photo, concrete result and timeframe. Keep a realistic spread of four- and five-star reviews with the occasional three-star; flawless sets look manufactured.

**Science/mechanism.** Start with a finding that changes how the reader sees the problem, explain the biology or physics behind it and why it is widely misunderstood, walk through the mechanism, present the research, then the product, expert endorsement, results and the call to action. Only cite studies you have personally checked. Swap jargon for analogies, and spell out the logic step by step: the evidence says X matters, the product supplies X, so it works. Diagrams make it stronger.

## Example
Hypothetical: a dog joint-supplement brand already runs a listicle as its control. It tests a vet-led page (opening with what the vet keeps seeing in older Labradors) and a review page sorted by breed size. The review page wins among owners of large breeds, so the brand keeps both and sends large-breed ads to the review page.

## Common mistakes
- Founder stories that do not mirror the buyer's experience, drift into company history, hide all vulnerability or show no founder at all.
- Bringing in the product before the reader cares.
- Inflated or unverifiable credentials.
- Comparison tables padded with trivial or invented advantages.
- Review pages so polished they look curated.
- Science pages quoting studies nobody on the team has read.
