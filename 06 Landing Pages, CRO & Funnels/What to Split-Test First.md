# What to Split-Test First

## What it is
An order of operations for landing page tests. Two rules sit underneath it. **Only test what everyone sees:** a small share of visitors scroll deep, so polishing a section near the bottom rarely moves anything. **Change one big, obvious thing at a time:** if you change five things and the variant loses, you learn nothing.

Testing is a continuous system, not an event. Ship many versions quickly, let data decide and iterate without ego. A good-enough page launched this week beats a perfect one launched next month.

## When to use it
- When a page is live, converting and getting enough traffic to read a test.
- When a team has a list of test ideas and needs an order.
- When you are tempted to test button colours.

## How (steps)
1. **Start with the three first swings, ranked by effort and impact:**
   - **Hero vs order form above the fold.** Copy the page, hide the hero, put the order form first and split visitors evenly. It is the cheapest test to build; skip it if the layout relies on the hero.
   - **Anything else above the fold:** the headline, how the product is named, the main image and the gallery. With strong headline research behind you, start with the headline.
   - **The offer,** the biggest potential impact: replace the order form with a buy-one-get-one, a bundle or a pick-your-own pack. Create a custom product if the catalogue cannot express the offer. Starting with the offer is fine if you prefer.
2. **Then work down by leverage:** headlines, then item structure and order (on listicles), then visuals, then offer and CTA details. Refining buttons is wasted effort until the headline performs. Reported effects help rank the queue: a numbered headline beat a vague one by about 37%; power words lifted CTR about 31%; action-led button text beat a plain "Buy Now" by 20-35%; before/after images lifted conversion about 64% per item in beauty; avatar-specific pages beat generic ones by 58%; a 70/30 education-to-promotion mix beat promo-heavy pages by 68%; loss framing beat gain framing by 40-60%; better sub-headlines alone added 41% engagement; and mobile optimisation added 37% on social traffic.
3. **Headline batches.** Write 10-15 variants across fear, curiosity, problem, story/before-after, revelation and authority styles. Test two or three at a time: CTR at the ad level, conversion at the page level, measured separately.
4. **No-code tests worth running early:** a headline test; rearranging product tiles; how large the product appears in the hero; a clean order form with the add-to-cart button in the first screen (usability research backs keeping it above the fold); a larger, clearer price, with about 18px cited as the sweet spot.
5. **Keep the first screen light on words.** Dense paragraphs at the top cost sales, and menus or announcement bars should not take over the opening view. On homepages, offer guided browsing (shop by use, type or style), not just a product list.
6. **Launch ad-driven page tests cleanly:** keep them in your existing campaign so they share learning, put the same pixel on the new page, optimise for purchases, and when pitting page types against each other give each its own ad set with the same audience and judge on ROAS rather than conversion rate alone (details in [[Testing Landing Pages Through Ad Spend]]).
7. **Keep a test log:** name, variable, variant A and B, start date, result for each, winner, notes.

## Example
Hypothetical: a snack brand's page converts, but nobody picks the curated starter packs. First test: move the buy box above the hero; it wins modestly. Second test: a credential-style headline ("Made by a gut-health dietitian") against a bold category claim ("The first cereal your gut will thank you for"). Third test: swap the starter packs for pick-your-own packs of 3, 6 and 12, placing the 12-pack in the centre as the highlighted choice, shifting the goal from conversion to AOV now that the page converts.

## Common mistakes
- Testing below-the-fold details while the headline is unproven.
- Changing several elements at once.
- Assuming another brand's published results will repeat on your audience.
- Accepting precise pixel or usability numbers you have never verified yourself.
- Polishing for looks; improvement is measured in conversion and revenue.
- Reading a result before it has enough data (see [[Reading Split-Test Results - Sample Size and Power]]).
