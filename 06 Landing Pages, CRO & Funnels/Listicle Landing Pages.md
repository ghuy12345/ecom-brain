# Listicle Landing Pages

## What it is
A listicle is a bridge page built as a numbered list ("7 reasons...", "5 things...") sitting between the ad and the offer page. It positions the brand as an educator first and a seller second, and handles most of the persuasion before anyone sees the product page.

Why it works better than a product page for cold traffic:
- People scan, jumping from sub-headline to sub-headline, so they stay longer (in one case time on page went from under a minute to over three).
- A number signals concrete value and a clear end point; a numbered headline reportedly beat a vague one by 37%.
- After reasons one and two, curiosity pulls the reader toward three.
- It reads as information, so defences stay low.
- Each item gives a natural point to convert.
- The visible structure feels organised and professional.
- Lists get passed around, with social shares reported up to 340% higher.

Reported results for listicles include conversion rising from about 1.1% to about 6.2%, and lifts of 127-500% over a standard product page.

It is also the standard way to move education upstream. If a product is high-friction or needs explaining, a listicle (often with a strong analogy) does that work before the offer page, instead of cramming it into the product page.

## When to use it
Strongest with cold visitors and benefits people can easily picture; products that need a reason to believe even though they are simple to explain; problem- and solution-aware audiences; considered purchases, roughly $75 AOV and up; and whenever build speed matters.

Not ideal for impulse or visual purchases like fashion (product page), very complex problems that need a deep narrative (advertorial), or fully unaware audiences.

## How (steps)
1. **Answer three questions first.** What mainly drives this buyer: fear, aspiration, fixing a problem or status? What is the biggest objection? Which proof type will persuade this audience?
2. **Draft the headline and all sub-headlines before the body.** When testing headlines, vary the count first, then power words, then question versus statement, then problem versus solution framing, then length (see [[Headline Formulas - From Claims and Desires]]).
3. **Lead paragraph, 3-5 sentences.** Establish relevance, name the overall issue, build credibility, preview value without giving it away. Open with a striking statistic, a relatable scene, an expert introduction or a shared frustration.
4. **Five to nine items, seven as a starting point.** Give every item the same skeleton: a sub-headline, the main claim, a short note on how it works, proof, a tie-back to the product, an optional testimonial and a visual.
5. **Order the items.** For lower-priced products put the strongest reason first; for higher-priced products build from least to most impressive.
6. **Keep roughly 70% education, 30% promotion.** The first items give pure value; the last one leads naturally into the product.
7. **Write transitions** that reference the previous point and escalate ("even better..."); good transitions are reported to lift completion by up to 40%.
8. **Place CTAs.** Small ones inside items, a stronger one after about the third item, the strongest at the end with a guarantee and any genuine time limit. Prefer journey wording ("check availability", "start your...") over "buy now".
9. **Put proof next to each claim,** favouring specific, third-party and visual proof. Pile several proof types onto the claim that matters most.
10. **Build visual rhythm.** Image, then reason headline, then two or three lines; bold key phrases; vary paragraph length; alternate section backgrounds.

**Niche adaptations:**
- Skincare and beauty: problem-solution or mistake-correction frames; a before/after with every item (reported +64% conversion); cover short- and long-term results.
- Supplements: deficiency or symptom frames; rely heavily on authority, because nobody can watch the product act; lightly edited customer video.
- Home products: hidden-problem and cost-saving frames; a demo clip per item (reported +54% engagement).
- Fitness: mistake-correction and results frames with specific metrics and timeframes.
- Tech: problem-solution and efficiency frames; logical proof and comparison tables (reported +35% conversion).

## Example
Hypothetical: a posture-corrector brand runs "6 reasons desk workers are switching from stretching apps". Item one names the hidden cause (muscles that switch off after hours of sitting) with a short diagram; item two shows a before/after photo pair; item three adds a physiotherapist quote and the mid-page CTA; items four and five cover comfort and price per day; item six introduces the bundle with a 60-day guarantee.

## Common mistakes
- Generic items a competitor could paste their name onto.
- A clumsy jump from education to pitch.
- Thin proof, or text-only items with no visuals.
- Inconsistent structure between items.
- Too salesy, or one listicle for every avatar instead of one per avatar.
- Weak sub-headlines. Read only the sub-headlines; they should tell a story alone.
- No clear final CTA, or poor mobile layout.
