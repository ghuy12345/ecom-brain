# Sales and Offer Page Structure

## What it is
The sales or offer page is where a warmed-up visitor decides. In a full funnel it comes after the pre-sale page and just before checkout. It succeeds only because the visitor already understands the problem and believes in the solution. Its one task is to make the deal hard to refuse and clear away the last worries about price and risk, with as few steps to purchase as possible.

The split of work with the pre-sale page: the pre-sale page names and deepens the problem, introduces the mechanism, earns trust through story and handles the biggest objection. The offer page gives only a quick recap of problem and mechanism, then concentrates on the deal itself, plenty of social proof, honest urgency, the remaining objections and a very visible guarantee.

## When to use it
Point these visitors here: readers arriving from a pre-sale page, email lists, retargeting pools, warm social and customer audiences, and anyone already product-aware or ready to buy. Cold traffic should not land here directly unless the brand is already a household name; strangers have not had their beliefs shifted, and a list of features means little to someone who has not accepted why they need it.

## How (steps)
1. **Hero.** A headline built around the deal rather than the specs; images showing precisely what arrives in the box; one proof signal such as a star rating, review count or customer count; the main button. Keep brand history and long explanations out of this area.
2. **Image gallery.** Several angles, the product being used, everything in the bundle including gifts, something for scale, labelled close-ups of key features.
3. **Offer box (the block that matters most).** Name of the product or bundle; everything included (product, extras, guarantee); the original price crossed out as an anchor; the sale price; any gifts; the risk reversal; quantity tiers where buying more saves more and higher tiers unlock gifts; a big action button; payment logos. Tiers are one of the most reliable ways to lift AOV.
4. **Differentiator strip.** Three to five icons, each with a short line on what sets you apart.
5. **Social proof.** Short customer videos in their own words, before/afters that state age and duration, written reviews with photos, a rating breakdown, press logos. Feature customers who look like your buyer.
6. **How it works.** No more than three visual steps, as a quick reminder rather than a rerun of the pre-sale argument.
7. **Comparison (optional).** Include it only if you win decisively; a lukewarm comparison does damage.
8. **FAQ.** The doubts still standing, most important first: how soon results appear, whether it is safe in particular situations, what happens if it fails, how to use it, delivery, how to cancel a subscription, how it differs from other options.
9. **Guarantee.** At least 30 days, ideally 60-90. A money-back promise sounds more solid than a vague satisfaction promise. Make it concrete (a stated result by a stated date or a full refund), simple to claim and easy to spot. Together with the proof, it should address the five risks buyers weigh: wasting time, feeling foolish, losing money, too much effort, and what other people will think.
10. **Closing call to action.** Show the tiers again, a one-line guarantee, the button and any genuine deadline.

**Urgency has to be true.** A countdown is only honest if the price really changes when it hits zero; fake deadlines damage trust for good. Real constraints, such as limited production batches, are fair to mention.

**What to test:** whether savings read better in dollars or percent (dollar amounts usually win on higher-priced items, roughly above $50, and percentages below); tiers versus a single option; the type of bonus (a cheap but closely related bonus often beats a costly unrelated one); guarantee length and wording (longer usually lifts conversion without a meaningful rise in refunds); button wording (a specific action beats a bare "buy now").

## Example
Hypothetical: a cold-brew coffee brand's offer page leads with "3 bags + a free tumbler, 25% off this week" above a photo of exactly that. The offer box shows one, three and six bags with the middle tier tagged "most popular", a 60-day money-back guarantee and express checkout buttons. Underneath come customer videos, three how-it-works steps, an FAQ that opens with "will it taste bitter?" and a final repeat of the tiers.

## Common mistakes
- Leading with brand story or specs instead of the deal.
- Sending cold traffic straight to the offer.
- Countdown timers that reset, or invented scarcity.
- A vague guarantee buried in small print.
- Rewriting the whole pre-sale argument instead of closing.
- A mobile layout where the main button is hidden below the fold.
