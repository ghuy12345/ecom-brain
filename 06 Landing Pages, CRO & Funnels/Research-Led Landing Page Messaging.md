# Research-Led Landing Page Messaging

## What it is
A discipline for writing landing page copy: nothing goes on the page unless you can point to where it came from. Valid sources are customer research, actual reviews, competitor research and the founder's real story. If a benefit you want to claim has no source, you research it first, even if that only means a quick search.

The second half of the discipline is that research never really closes. A page can be selling nicely and still have plenty of room to improve. Live data, new reviews and support conversations keep raising questions the page does not answer yet.

## When to use it
- Writing a new landing page from a template.
- When copy will not come. That is usually a sign you need more research, not more effort at the keyboard.
- After launch, on a regular rhythm, to plan the next page variant.

## How (steps)
1. **Lock the angle first,** then gather research for it (see [[Setting Up the Research Document]]).
2. **Rank benefits by how much the buyer cares,** leading them step by step. Cut or move any template section that adds nothing; templates are a starting point, not a rulebook.
3. **Source every claim.** For each benefit block, note the review, survey answer or finding it rests on. Prefer the strongest true phrasing ("zero added sugar" is stronger than "no added sugar" if accurate).
4. **Choose reviews deliberately.** In customer surveys, around 75% of buyers name reviews as what drove their purchase, so reviews can do much of the selling. Choose a different review for each kind of buyer (someone buying a gift, someone with a diagnosed condition, a skeptic of mainstream options, someone who arrived from an ad). Put one that echoes the main headline near the top and one that mirrors the traffic source. Highlight the most powerful line in each. Order them on purpose.
5. **Let reviews rewrite copy.** If customers use the product in an unexpected way, add that use to the copy and quote them.
6. **Build offers for different buyers** found in research: a starter option, a larger "most preferred" option to lift AOV, and a gift option if competitors show gifting demand.
7. **Settle the message before choosing images,** then let the images sharpen it. Build and check everything in a phone-sized view.
8. **Ship once it is good enough.** Research must not become a reason to delay launch.
9. **Keep researching after launch.** Sources of new questions: fresh reviews, new offers, pain points raised by support or the team, and purchase data (if many buyers pick one product, show its details on the page).
10. **Dig deeper into a working angle rather than broadening.** Return to the same sources (short-video platforms, forums, competitor pages) with a tighter question, imagining yourself in the customer's position.
11. **Re-read your own copy after a few days.** Distance is a research method; weaknesses become obvious.
12. **Test each change against the live version** so continued improvement stays low-risk.

## Example
Hypothetical: a ready-to-eat soup brand builds a page around gut comfort. Every benefit block cites its source: "high fibre" from the label, "zero added sugar" verified against the recipe, "easy when you can't face cooking" lifted from three reviews. After launch, support tickets reveal people asking about preservatives, which the page never mentions, and order data shows one flavour dominating. The next variant adds a short "no preservatives, here's why it's shelf-stable" section and the best-seller's label, and a second test rebuilds the same knowledge as an advertorial.

## Common mistakes
- Claims with no source.
- Generic "love it!" reviews instead of ones chosen for a buyer type.
- Leaving in template blocks that have nothing to do with the angle.
- Treating the launched page as finished.
- Using research as a reason to delay shipping.
