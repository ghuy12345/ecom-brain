# Revenue and Profit Per Session

## What it is
**Revenue per session (RPS)** is AOV multiplied by conversion rate. A page or offer only counts as better if that product goes up; lifting one side while the other drops by more is a loss. **Profit per session (PPS)** is RPS multiplied by gross margin. RPS on its own can fool you, because a thinner-margin offer can bring in more revenue and less profit. Few operators track PPS, which makes it an advantage for those who do.

Rough RPS bands for US traffic (most other markets run lower):
- **Below $1:** something is seriously off; you would need extremely cheap clicks to make money.
- **$1-2:** within reach; look for improvements.
- **Over $2:** a $1 click can pay off.
- **$2.50-3:** a healthy place to be.

## When to use it
- Comparing two offers, two pages or two price points.
- Working out whether a weak funnel is an ad problem or a page problem.
- Deciding whether a discount earns back the margin it gives away.

## How (steps)
1. **Take your numbers from the ad platform, not the store dashboard.** Create custom columns: conversion rate as purchases over landing page views (fall back to link clicks if page views look wrong), and AOV as purchase value over purchases.
2. **Model every offer in a spreadsheet.** For the current offer and each challenger list price, units, product cost (round shipping estimates up), payment fees (roughly 3% plus a few cents), margin, breakeven ROAS and the CPA you can afford. Get exact shipping quotes for one, two and three or more units from suppliers; if shipping barely rises with quantity, bigger bundles widen your margin on their own.
3. **Guard your margin.** Aim to stay above about 60% gross margin and treat 50% as a floor. If you are squeezed below it, add value through the copy and the bundle rather than cutting price again.
4. **Test in a sensible sequence:** bundles, then price, then buy-one-get-one, then multi-buy percentage discounts, then shipping thresholds. Start with wide gaps and then narrow (try $59.99 against $69.99; if the cheaper one wins, test $64.99 next).
5. **Preselect the multi-unit option** on consumable products so most buyers simply accept your recommendation.
6. **Give every test at least a week,** and revisit offers on a schedule, because offers left alone lose their pull.
7. **Set RPS against CPC.** When RPS sits comfortably above what you pay per click, there is room to scale. When RPS is healthy but clicks are expensive, the ads are attracting costly or low-intent visitors, and the page is not the culprit.

## Example
All numbers here are hypothetical, to show the arithmetic.

- **Price cut:** going from $69.99 to $59.99 takes margin from 68% to 63%. If conversion climbs from 2% to 3%, RPS moves from about $1.40 to $1.80 and PPS from about $0.95 to $1.13. The lower price earns more profit.
- **Two-unit minimum:** from a $69 AOV at 2% conversion, requiring two units roughly doubles AOV, so conversion only needs to hold around 1% to break even on RPS; at 1.3% you come out ahead, at 0.7% you fall behind.
- **Page rebuild:** say a home-goods brand rebuilds its offer page around a modest discount with a deadline bar, a headline reframing the problem, footage of rival methods failing, a short story, a three-point demo, layered proof and a clear before-and-after price. With identical ads, if conversion roughly tripled in this hypothetical at a flat CPC, RPS would roughly triple for a loss of a couple of margin points. Treat a result like this as directional: when copy, proof and urgency all change at once, the price cut's own effect cannot be isolated.
- **Diagnosis:** a $140 AOV at 1.2% conversion is about $1.68 RPS; paying $1.50 per click, the ads need work. A $250 AOV at 1% is $2.50 RPS; the page is doing its job and the task is cheaper, higher-intent traffic.

## Common mistakes
- Judging an offer on conversion rate alone or AOV alone.
- Ignoring margin, so a "winner" quietly loses money.
- Trusting store analytics or profit apps that are not reconciled with the bank balance.
- Testing an offer by editing the live product page. Duplicate the page into its own template and use its published address (never a preview link) as the ad destination.
- Overlooking returning buyers. When they make up more than roughly 30% of orders, new-customer conversion is weaker than the blended number suggests.
- Pushing subscriptions before you have repeat purchases and a known lifetime value.
