# Proof Types and Placement on Pages

## What it is
Proof is whatever makes a claim on the page credible. On landing pages, where proof sits matters almost as much as what it is. Four ranking rules cover most decisions: precise proof outranks vague proof, proof from others outranks your own assertions, something shown outranks something described, and proof placed right next to its claim outranks proof collected in a block at the end. People verify a claim at the moment they read it; they seldom link a review at the bottom back to a promise three screens up.

There are five types:
1. **Social:** named testimonials with real photos, exact customer numbers, ratings with precise review counts, customer video. It backfires with stock photos, empty praise, reviewers who look nothing like the buyer, or anything that feels staged. Never use AI-generated faces.
2. **Data:** referenced studies, measured before/after results, performance figures, survey numbers. Give a figure and a timeframe rather than an adjective like "quick": "94% saw visible results within 7 days", not "fast results". For a household-cleaning brand, adding specific efficacy data reportedly raised purchase intent by about 27%, and adding before/after images alone lifted conversion by about 22%.
3. **Authority:** genuine expert backing, qualifications, press coverage, certifications. Pick the kind of expert this audience already trusts.
4. **Visual:** before/after images (usually the most persuasive picture you can use), demos, close-ups of ingredients or manufacturing, short clips of the product doing its job. Use real photos with consistent lighting and a stated timeframe.
5. **Logical:** the mechanism laid out in steps, cause and effect, diagrams, comparison tables. Analytical and doubtful buyers need this.

(How much proof an ad needs is covered in [[Escalating Levels of Proof]].)

## When to use it
- Writing any section that makes a claim.
- Reviewing a page where every review lives in one block at the bottom.
- When you know which objection is costing you sales.

## How (steps)
1. **List the major claims** on the page; each one needs at least one piece of proof.
2. **Put proof directly under its claim** (proof beside its claim was reported 217% more effective than proof pooled at the bottom): the testimonial about a benefit under that benefit, the study under the ingredient it backs, the before/after under the mechanism, the expert quote under the boldest statement.
3. **Layer proof on the headline promise.** For a hypothetical "visible change in a week": a measured result, day-one and day-seven photos, one customer line, an expert explaining why it is quick, and a two-line mechanism.
4. **Pair proof with objections:**
   - "This won't work for someone like me": stories from customers in the same situation.
   - "I've tried this kind of thing": the mechanism plus a side-by-side of results.
   - "It costs too much": the maths of value and a customer talking about payoff.
   - "Sounds too good to be true": stack an expert voice, a study and a before/after.
   - "Let me think about it": signs of how many others bought and what waiting costs.
   - "I can get it cheaper": mechanism, quality evidence, expert backing.
5. **Address the five buyer risks:** time (stories of fast results), identity (peers and experts the buyer respects), money (a money-back guarantee), effort (stories about how easy it is), reputation (signs that many others already use it).
6. **Set proof density by page type.** Listicles need proof on every item. Advertorials weave it into the story, with testimonials that mirror the narrator and an expert at the mechanism. Offer pages carry a large social block and a guarantee. Expert pages are authority-led. Comparison pages back each criterion with data. Review pages are proof from top to bottom.
7. **Choose reviews for relevance.** A few reviews about the exact claim do more than hundreds of general ones. Include some four- and three-star reviews; perfect sets look edited.

## Example
Hypothetical: a sunscreen page says it leaves no white cast on darker skin. Instead of a review carousel at the end, the claim is followed straight away by three daylight photos of customers with deep skin tones, one short review praising the lack of a grey film, and a line explaining the tinted mineral formula. The doubt ("every mineral sunscreen leaves a cast") is answered the moment it forms.

## Common mistakes
- Stock or AI faces, fuzzy claims, reviews that sound written by the marketing team.
- Review sets with only five-star ratings.
- Experts or citations nobody can verify.
- Before/after images that look doctored.
- Pooling all proof at the foot of the page.
- Fake urgency dressed up as evidence of demand.
