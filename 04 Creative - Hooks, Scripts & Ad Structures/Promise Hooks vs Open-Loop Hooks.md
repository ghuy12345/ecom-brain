# Promise Hooks vs Open-Loop Hooks

## What it is
There are two basic ways to make someone keep watching:

- **Promise hook.** Keep the context in. You tell the viewer exactly what they are about to see, and that alone is worth watching ("Sandals vs a bed of nails"). The viewer thinks "that sounds worth seeing". Your obligation: deliver exactly what you promised.
- **Open-loop hook.** Take the context out. You withhold a key piece of information, often the product itself, so the viewer has to stay to find out ("Guess what he tiled his kitchen floor with"). The viewer thinks "what happens next?". Your obligation: the answer must be worth the wait.

Much feed content does neither, which is why it is scrolled past. Many strong hooks blend the two: "Cleaning a burnt pan with ice" promises a demonstration and contains an unexpected pairing that makes you wonder how.

## When to use it
- Use a **promise** when the idea itself is gripping: a striking demonstration, an extreme test, a visually surprising result.
- Use an **open loop** when the plain context is dull. In ads the context is usually the product, and "here is a new moisturiser" is rarely gripping, so ads lean toward loops.
- Use a **blend** when you have a demonstrable result that also contains a contradiction.

The decision question is simple: write both versions and pick whichever is more interesting to someone who has never heard of you.

## How (steps)
1. **Write the plain statement** of what happens in the ad ("A dog gets a full bath with no water").
2. **Write the withholding version.** Remove the answer, the product and any detail that excludes viewers (a location, a brand name): "The bath trick groomers don't want you to know".
3. **Or go the other way**: turn a vague question into a concrete promise. "Which shoes protect your feet best?" becomes a stunt: one person, flip-flops, a board of nails.
4. **Keep whichever version is more gripping.**
5. **Run the loop test.** After reading it, would a stranger wonder "how?", "what am I missing?" or "then what?" If not, there is no loop.
6. **Build the loop with one of three tools:**
   - *Juxtaposition*: put together two things that should not belong together ("I cancelled my gym membership and finally got lean"). It often works by breaking a belief the viewer holds. You must connect the two halves later. Test: if every competitor could say your line, there is no juxtaposition. "The world's strongest coffee" is generic; pairing the drink with a death wish is not. See [[Juxtaposition in Copy]].
   - *Ethical fear*: hint that a mistake or danger is close. People work harder to avoid a mistake than to find the best option, so "Watch out for these ingredients in kids' vitamins" outholds "The best kids' vitamins". Scaring people with lies or inflation is short-sighted and a legal liability.
   - *Do the opposite*: write down what rivals claim, how they behave and whom they address, then flip one of those. A men's body wash that talked to women instead of men is the classic case.
7. **Check delivery.** Whatever the hook implies, the body must be about that. A hook about eggs and old dogs needs content about eggs and old dogs.

## Example
Say you sell a washable rug. Promise version: "Red wine, mud and a toddler vs this rug". It works because the demonstration is genuinely watchable. Loop version: a goat standing in a pristine living room, overlay "Our landlord still doesn't know". Viewers need to know why the goat is there; the reveal (the rug goes in the washing machine) closes the loop. Run both: the promise version usually wins with product-aware shoppers, the loop with colder audiences.

## Common mistakes
- Breaking the promise. Bait-and-switch may suit entertainment, but in advertising it wrecks trust and sales.
- Answering the question a moment after asking it, which lets the viewer go.
- Loops with no connection to the buyer's interest, which attract curious non-buyers.
- Generic superlatives dressed up as hooks.
- Fear hooks that are dishonest or wildly overstated.
