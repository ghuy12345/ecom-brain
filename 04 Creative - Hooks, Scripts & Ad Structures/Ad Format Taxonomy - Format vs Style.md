# Ad Format Taxonomy - Format vs Style

## What it is
A shared vocabulary for the *shape* of an ad, separate from what it says. It rests on three distinct axes:

1. **Format** is pure structure: voiceover over B-roll, a slideshow, a product photo with a headline. A format carries no angle and no bias; any message can go into it.
2. **Style** is a recognisable treatment that does carry a bias or frame: us vs them, native post, podcast clip, street interview, long-form sales video. "Us vs them" is not a format; it is a way of positioning a message.
3. **Ways of saying it** is the third axis: the same message in a different emotional tone or wording.

Keeping these apart lets you brief an editor precisely ("subtitles over B-roll, us-vs-them style, calm tone") instead of just "make a video", and it shows you which axis you actually changed when an iteration wins or loses.

## When to use it
- Writing briefs for editors, designers and creators.
- Planning creative diversity, so the account is not leaning on one format.
- Reading test results: was it the format, the style or the message that moved?

## How (steps)
**1. Pick the video format.**
- *Voiceover with B-roll*: human or synthetic voice over clips and music. The most common workhorse.
- *Subtitles with B-roll*: no voice; edited clips with on-screen text, so each viewer hears the words in their own head. The soundtrack carries the mood and matters a lot.
- *Slideshow with text*: stills, transitions, text reveals and slow zooms. The fallback when you have no footage.
- *Talking head or authentic UGC*: a person speaking to camera with minimal editing.
- *Professional studio video*: polished and expensive. It usually takes heavy effort and often converts worse than rougher formats; it tends to pay off only for larger brands with an experienced production partner.
- *Animation*: illustrated storytelling.
- *Inner monologue over B-roll*: a voice narrating a person's thoughts; increasingly common alongside voiceover and UGC.

**2. Or pick the image format.**
- *Graphic design static*: a strong headline over brand colours; most comparison and offer ads take this shape.
- *Product shot with a headline*: the simplest static.
- *Infographic*: charts or steps; the least used.
- *Native style*: imitates an organic, unbranded post.
- *Quote or text-heavy image*: the words are the visual.
- *Collage*: several images showing uses and angles (a rug with the dog on it, a close-up, the room).
- *UGC image*: real customer photos, unpolished.
- *Meme*: a familiar template repurposed for the product.

**3. Layer a style on top, knowing its bias.** Common styles: us vs them, native, podcast clip, street interview, founder story, long-form sales video. Two worth testing in busy seasons:
- *Long-form objection-answering videos* (several minutes) that work through every doubt a buyer could have. Judge them on average watch time and the first 15 seconds, because watch time matters far more for long formats than for short ones. Break them into hook, the build-up of belief before the mechanism, the mechanism reveal and a simple close.
- *Street interviews built from one real interaction* rather than a montage of many people.

**4. Apply the format rules.**
- Meme and hyper-relatable images tend to win cheap clicks, especially when CPMs are high, but convert worse than other formats. Use them when humour suits the brand.
- Synthetic people raise fewer problems in silent still images (clothing on a model) than as talking presenters; check disclosure rules either way.
- Never run fake press releases or invented news articles. That is false advertising with legal exposure. An editorial *look* is fine; claiming coverage you did not get is not.
- Source B-roll yourself: shoot it, pay creators for it, buy stock or generate it.

**5. Diagnose by axis.** If your videos keep losing to your statics, look first at editing and copy skill rather than the product. If an iteration fails, check whether you only changed the format when the message was the problem.

## Example
Say you sell an insulated coffee mug. One message, "still hot three hours later", can run as: a subtitles-over-B-roll video of a commuter's morning (format) in a day-in-the-life style; a meme static ("me at 11am sipping coffee I poured at 8"); a collage of the mug at a desk, in a car and on a hike; and a product photo with the headline. If the meme wins on clicks but the collage wins on purchases, you learned something about format, not about the message.

## Common mistakes
- Calling an angle a format ("make an us-vs-them") and leaving the editor to guess the structure.
- Leaning on one format until the account fatigues.
- Expensive studio shoots before cheaper formats have been tested.
- Fake news-style ads.
- Ripping footage from other creators instead of sourcing it properly.
