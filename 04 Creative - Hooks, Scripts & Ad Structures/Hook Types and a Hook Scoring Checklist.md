# Hook Types and a Hook Scoring Checklist

## What it is
A toolkit for choosing and judging hooks once you understand what a hook must do (see [[Video Hook Fundamentals]]). It has four parts: a menu of hook types, a reliable structure for cold audiences, the targeted-vs-broad decision, and a scoring checklist you run before anything is filmed.

You can pull three levers in any hook: what is on screen (would a thumb pause for it?), what is heard (a dramatic noise, a surprising line, or total silence) and what is written. Improving just one lever on an average hook can lift its hook rate substantially.

## When to use it
- When writing several hook variations for a concept and you need range, not ten versions of one idea.
- Before production, to cut weak hooks cheaply.
- When a live ad lags and you need to find the weak part of its hook.

## How (steps)
**1. Decide targeted or broad first.**
- A *targeted* hook calls out the audience or problem directly ("Cold feet every night? Not anymore"). It is relevance-driven, easier to write and a safe default for beginners or large product-aware markets. The trade-off: everyone outside that group keeps scrolling.
- A *broad* hook opens on something nearly everyone relates to, then narrows to the product. It reaches people outside your current audience, earns shares (a non-buyer forwards it to someone who is a buyer) and behaves like an unaware-level ad. It takes more skill, and it still needs a curiosity gap; broad without a gap fails.
- Balance curiosity against qualification. Pure clickbait attracts the wrong audience; the ad must still pull in buyers.

**2. Pick a hook type.**
- *Contrarian truth*: takes on something most people believe; does well where buyers need educating.
- *High-stakes warning*: names the persona and an immediate risk in one breath; it filters hard and adds urgency (health, beauty, fitness). Keep it honest.
- *Specific proof*: opens on a concrete result and then substantiates it.
- *Curiosity gap*: wins clicks, converts unevenly; it needs a landing page that finishes the job.
- *Myth-busting the objection*: validate the doubt, then reframe it; good for skeptical buyers.
- *Tactical promise*: a specific, time-bound benefit.
- *Common mistake*: still usable but overused, so audiences recognise the device.

If every competitor runs the same type, look for the type nobody runs. Treat templates as a starting point only.

**3. For cold audiences, use persona, problem, interrupt, promise.**
1. Persona: name the person, situation or identity, so non-buyers drop off and the delivery system learns who to target.
2. Problem: within about two seconds, a specific pain they recognise ("your foundation separates by lunchtime" beats "struggling with makeup").
3. Pattern interrupt: once viewers sense an ad they start to leave, so re-engage with a cut, an unexpected sound, a bold line or a reframe. That earns a few extra seconds.
4. Promise: something concrete, ideally with a time frame, that rewards staying and resolves the tension the interrupt created.
Keep the presenter consistent with the persona; a mismatch breaks trust instantly.

**4. Score the hook, most important criterion first.**
1. *Clarity*: within three seconds, would someone who has never heard of you know the product, the intended buyer and what is going on?
2. *Relevance*: does the target instantly recognise their pain or desire, and do non-buyers keep scrolling? Otherwise delivery drifts toward the wrong people.
3. *Novelty*: an angle or format nobody in your category is currently using.
4. *Specificity*: a concrete number, result or person instead of a claim any competitor could make.
5. *Credibility*: a reason to believe within a few seconds (visible proof, a recognisable expert, before-and-after).

**5. Apply the decision rules.**
- Passes clarity and relevance plus any one of the others: produce it and test.
- Passes clarity and relevance alone: fine to test, but sharpen it with a number or a fresher angle before you put scale behind it.
- Fresh but confusing or off-target: keep it out of paid; rework it until the product shows up sooner.
- Fails clarity or relevance: start again.
- Diagnosing a live hook that is losing: look at clarity, then relevance, and only then the secondary criteria.

## Example
Say you sell a dog dental chew. Targeted: "If you've never brushed your dog's teeth, watch this." Broad: "Who has the better life, cats or dogs?" then funnel into teeth as the one thing dogs lose out on. Scoring the broad version: clear by second three only if the chew appears early, relevant once teeth are named, novel in a category full of vet-callout ads. It ships; the targeted version becomes the control.

## Common mistakes
- Attention gags where nobody can tell what is being sold.
- Memes and humour irrelevant to the buyer.
- Generic claims such as "the number one mistake people make".
- A presenter who does not look like the persona being called out.
