# Proven Static Ad Structures and Why They Work

## What it is
A set of static ad structures that keep winning across categories, each paired with the reason it converts. The point is the reason, not the layout. Layout never sells on its own; the message placed in it does. Copying a layout and pasting in random copy can work by luck, then crashes with no way to recover, because you never knew why it worked.

## When to use it
- You are new to statics and need starting points that are known to work.
- You have a desire or angle and need a frame to express it.
- You want to adapt a competitor's or another category's winner without cloning it.

## How (steps)
1. **Validate the swipe.** Favour ads with heavy shares and engagement over the past three months or so. "Running a long time" alone is a weaker signal. Do not save ads you like; save ads customers clearly respond to.
2. **Name the mechanism** using the list below.
3. **Rebuild it for your brand**: your fonts, colours and product, not a one-to-one clone.
4. **Use real proof only.** Every review or comment must be a genuine one from your customers.
5. **Keep wordplay tied to the product.** If the pun stretches beyond the product, it flops.
6. **Put the headline first.** When you must pick, words beat design polish. A great visual with a weak headline loses to a plain visual with a strong one.
7. **Mind the destination.** Sending a static to a plain product page leaves half the argument unmade; a page built around the ad's promise often lifts conversion (see [[Landing Pages as a Scaling Lever]]).

**The structures and why each works**
- *Avatar call-out*: addresses one specific person ("for mums of teenagers") with a matching real review and photo. Works through instant self-identification.
- *Solution exaggeration*: one bold, true number as the hero ("Ten-hour shift, feet still fine"). The number stops the scroll and makes the claim concrete.
- *Borrowed credibility*: ties your product to a way of working that buyers already trust, usually with an unusual image to intrigue and a plain headline to explain. Belief transfers from the familiar to the new.
- *Curiosity visual*: a strange image (hundreds of vitamin gummies lined up like soldiers) with copy that resolves it. The unresolved image pulls the eye into the text.
- *Category reframe*: "It's not a shake. It's lunch." It moves the product into a more valuable category in the buyer's mind.
- *Named pain point*: the buyer's own colourful phrase in place of a bland goal ("muffin top" instead of "lose weight"). Specific language signals that you understand them.
- *Accidental outcome*: "I accidentally dropped a dress size at 50." The word "accidentally" implies the result needed no effort.
- *Indirect avatar*: speak to the buyer through someone they care about ("Teen always grumpy before school?" reaches the parent).
- *Relatable hook plus advertorial*: a familiar situation leading to a longer story; best for visual-first products, as long as the product is not hidden.
- *Free item or clean offer*: a free gift or a neatly displayed buy-one-get-one, for product-aware shoppers ready to buy.
- *Simplified us vs them*: a handful of words contrasting the old way and the new (the spread brand that recast plain bread as breakfast).
- *Minimal us vs them*: show only what your product leaves out or doesn't demand. Simplicity lowers the perceived risk of trying.
- *Trend-led article style*: ties a current topic to the product in an editorial look. Keep it clearly an ad; never imitate a real news outlet or fake a news story.

8. **Use AI carefully.** Hand an assistant the reference, your note on why it works and your brand and buyer details, then request a version for you. Know the mechanism well enough that you can judge the output and keep going without it (see [[Building Static Ads With an LLM]]).

## Example
Say you sell a long-wear foundation. The solution-exaggeration structure from a deodorant ad transfers directly: hero text "16 hours. 34°C. Zero touch-ups." over a selfie at an outdoor wedding, a real review strip below ("Danced till midnight and it didn't budge"), and a small shop button. Variation 2 uses the named-pain structure instead: "Foundation sliding off by lunch?" Variation 3 tries the accidental-outcome frame: "I accidentally went 3 days without retouching." Each keeps your brand look; only the persuasion mechanism changes.

## Common mistakes
- Cloning a template pixel for pixel, including someone else's brand style.
- Fabricated reviews or invented "customer" comments.
- Choosing swipes by how much you like them rather than by evidence.
- Spending hours on polish while the headline is weak.
- Treating a high hook rate on a static as proof it works; strong hooks can attract the wrong audience.
