# Video Ad Pre-Launch and Post-Mortem Checklist

## What it is
A set of questions based on what high-spending video ads tend to have in common. You use it twice:

- **Before launch**, on the brief or rough cut, to catch weak ads while they are cheap to fix.
- **After a failure**, on an ad that never scaled or sold, to see what was absent.

No ad needs to tick every box. The more it ticks, the better its odds. The same list works as a prompt if you want an AI reviewer to give a first opinion, but a human makes the final call.

## When to use it
- Every video brief, before it goes to an editor or creator.
- Every finished cut, as a last review before upload.

## How (steps)
Ask each question honestly. A "no" is a prompt to improve, not an automatic rejection.

**Attention and fit**
1. **Do the opening one or two seconds grab the *right* person and hold them?** Picture someone lighting a firework and dropping it in their pocket: you couldn't look away until it went off. Aim for that feeling.
2. **Would your buyer choose to watch this**, or is it just to your taste?
3. **Does the copy paint a picture?** A vivid, slightly absurd image ("enough earwax to fill a teacup") sticks because everyone can see it.
4. **Does it feel at home in the feed?** Anything resembling a TV spot or a film trailer will probably struggle on social. Native does not mean one fixed style.

**Relatability**
5. **Does it name a type of person the viewer recognises in themselves?** Specific habits ("the person who packs the night before a trip, at 2am") create more recognition than broad labels.
6. **Is there a moment that raises a smile?** Funny and informative can coexist, and humour makes ads stick.
7. **Does the setting, language and problem feel familiar** to the buyer?
8. **Does it scream "advert"?** Creative that blends into the feed tends to scale furthest, above all with colder audiences. Buyers who are ready don't mind a pitch; the constraint is awareness, because someone who doesn't yet know they have the problem won't buy on the spot.
9. **Gut check: does it feel real?** If not, remake it.

**Strategy**
10. **Which insights from past winners have you applied, and at the right level?** Apply principles ("this buyer responds to breakdowns", "this benefit matters most to them"), not a single hook template copied onto every ad. Repeating one device everywhere causes iteration overload.
11. **Does it creatively teach the viewer a route to what they deeply want?** Don't state the desire flatly ("we know you want a better relationship"); deliver it as something useful ("how to get her laughing again on date night").
12. **Have you used escalation?** Push a claim to a playful but clear extreme: a cleaning-obsessed cartoon character "designing" an easy-clean dish rack, or a hair supplement so potent that a famously hairy movie monster swears by it.
13. **Story, entertainment or a credible new mechanism?** Every ad needs at least one: a story the buyer cares about, entertainment that makes them smile or share, or education with a credible mechanism. If plain feature-benefit-buy ads keep failing, get more creative. An ad that does none of these goes back for rework.
14. **Does it set your brand apart?** Copying competitors keeps you behind them. Aim for a moat: the viewer should feel your product is the only one that delivers this the way they want. Unique selling points and mechanisms help, yet proving you know one particular kind of customer inside out is the stronger lever.

**After a failure**, go through the same list and mark which questions the ad failed. Patterns across several losers (always failing 5 and 8, say) tell you more than any single ad. Pair this with [[Diagnosing Ad Execution Problems]] for word- and edit-level faults.

## Example
Say a brief for a travel-pillow video reads: "Woman on a plane, pillow, five benefits, discount." Running the checklist:
- Q1 fails: the opener is a plane interior, which stops nobody in particular.
- Q5 fails: no recognisable type of person.
- Q8 fails: studio lighting makes it look like a commercial.
- Q13 fails: no story, no humour, no mechanism.
The rewrite opens on a man jolting awake with his head on a stranger's shoulder (Q1, Q6), labels him "the person who swears they never fall asleep on flights" (Q5), is filmed on a phone in a real seat (Q4, Q8), and explains in one line why the pillow's shape stops the head-drop (Q13). It now passes most questions before a cent is spent.

## Common mistakes
- Making the video you like instead of the one your buyer wants.
- Polished, commercial-looking footage when native is the goal.
- Skipping the post-mortem, so losers teach nothing.
- Assuming competitors are watching you; strong competitors usually ignore you and focus on their own space.
