# Cold-Audience Video Ad Script Structure

## What it is
A fixed order for a video script aimed at people who do not know you. Using a structure means the right message components appear in the right place, so winners come from a repeatable process instead of luck. Copy never manufactures desire from nothing; it points wants and worries the viewer already has at your product, and the structure is how you do that inside a minute.

The broad shape: open on emotion, argue with facts in the middle, and close by taking away whatever doubt is left.

## When to use it
- Any cold-traffic video ad, especially UGC, talking-head and voiceover formats.
- Checking any draft for missing slots.
- Rebuilding an ad that gets views but no sales.

## How (steps)
**Build your message components first.** Keep a sheet per product with five types, marking the ones already proven in winning ads:
- *Benefits*: the emotional outcome. They come first; never overpromise.
- *Features*: factual, used after benefits to handle doubts; must be instantly understood and differentiating.
- *Proof and scarcity*: at least one per ad, and specific ("4,812 reviews" beats "loved by customers"). No fake scarcity.
- *Offer*: a reason to buy today, with a real deadline.
- *Mechanism*: the way the product gets its result.
Pull them from your product page, five-star and one-star reviews, winning ads and current offers.

**Then write the script in seven slots:**
1. **Hook (first 2 to 3 seconds).** Immediate tension: high stakes (a fear or pain) or a curiosity gap. It must be instantly clear. Weak: opening on the product when nothing is at stake, or a pronoun ("it", "this") the viewer cannot place.
2. **Bridge.** Hold the pitch back. Make the problem bigger, or start a second question. Jumping from hook to "introducing our new..." tells viewers they are being sold to. See [[Writing the Bridge After the Hook]].
3. **Education.** Show why the problem happens, so your product arrives as the obvious answer rather than a pitch. Put evidence on screen; bare assertions teach nothing.
4. **Objection handling.** Raise the one or two biggest doubts early, while most viewers are still watching, rather than saving them for the close ("sounds too easy", "tastes awful"), and reposition each. Fold answers into the benefits. Common doubt categories: how fast it works, whether it suits me, effort, ease of use, delivery, and how proven it is.
5. **Proof.** Objective and visible: demonstrations, results, recognisable logos, the long history of the category. Saying "lab-tested" without showing the results wrecks trust.
6. **Benefits, then features.** Lead with what the viewer gets, then name the feature responsible. A naked list of features leaves viewers to work out why it matters, and most won't bother.
7. **Call to action.** Soft and in the same tone as the ad. Framing the product as already theirs ("yours is ready to ship") or reframing the price as an investment both fit; lurching into a hard sell makes people leave. Use real deadlines only. Close with a risk-reversal tool: social proof, borrowed authority or a guarantee.

**Pick a formula to order the slots when it helps:** problem-agitate-solution; attention-interest-desire-action; picture-promise-prove-push (useful when results take weeks); or a testimonial arc (before, discovery, struggle, transformation).

**Rules of thumb:**
- Retention only falls, so the most important benefit goes early.
- Cut at least every three seconds.
- Keep the sell back for as long as the story keeps people watching.
- If the ad would die with an unknown face in place of the presenter, you have made organic content, not an ad. Put the proof inside the creative itself.
- Anything promised in the ad must be visible on the landing page.
- Write at least four hooks per script; a hook that wins can be recycled onto a new body even when the original body flopped.
- Cap short ads near 45 seconds.

## Example
Say you sell a magnesium sleep drink. Hook: "Why you wake up at 3am every night (and it's not stress)." Bridge: "It's something your body runs out of by midnight, and the fix is almost too simple." Education: low magnesium and night-time waking, with a simple animation. Objection: "I know, another sleep drink. I hated the chalky ones too," then a taste reaction shot. Proof: a sleep-tracker screen before and after two weeks. Benefits then features: waking rested, because of the absorbable form and no sugar. Call to action: "Your first box is 30 percent off this week, and if you don't sleep better in 30 days, it's free."

## Common mistakes
- A hook with nothing at stake, and no bridge.
- Claims with nothing on screen to back them.
- Objections left to the end or ignored.
- An abrupt hard-sell close in a soft, story-led ad.
- Studying only the words in competitor scripts while ignoring their scenes, sound and transitions.
