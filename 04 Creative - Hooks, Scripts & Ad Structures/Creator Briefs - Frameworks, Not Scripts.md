# Creator Briefs - Frameworks, Not Scripts

## What it is
Scripts are for actors; creators need a framework. It sets direction (what the video is for, what matters, a few hook ideas) while leaving the words and the delivery to the creator. The idea a creator connects with is often different from what you pictured, and their genuine take tends to beat your version.

Think of a spectrum:
- **Freestyle**: no guidance at all. Works only with unusually strong creators.
- **Framework**: direction but no fixed wording. Usually the best option.
- **Full script**: usually underperforms, because it rarely suits the creator's natural style and sounds recited.

When tightly scripted videos keep underperforming, loosen up and let creators bring their own ideas.

## When to use it
- Briefing seeded or paid UGC creators.
- Onboarding a new ambassador.
- Whenever results from scripted creator videos sound stiff.

## How (steps)
**1. Build a master framework document** with these sections:
1. *Mission*: why this video matters, plus the upside for the creator (see step 5).
2. *About the brand*: two to four lines, never paragraphs.
3. *Rules*: be genuine, show real situations, film in natural light, speak off the cuff. Steer clear of polished marketing language, irrelevant settings and product-only demos with no real-life problem.
4. *Video specs*: length, orientation, file delivery. These stay the same for every brand.
5. *Tone and vibe.*
6. *Concepts*: a numbered list, each an opening line with a one-line scenario. Build up to about nine, never more. A hook alone can be enough; extra setup adds context but can box the creator in. Overlapping concepts are fine.
7. *B-roll ideas*: suggested shots, clearly labelled as ideas.
8. *Learn more*: the product page and brand socials.
Name the file with the brand, the audience and "creator framework".

**2. Keep it short and benefit-led.** Focus on what the customer gets, not a feature list.

**3. Tailor it per creator.** Most creators grab whichever idea sits at the top, or whichever is simplest. Send everyone an identical list and you'll get ten takes on idea number one. So duplicate the master for each creator, look at their existing content, delete concepts that don't suit them, and send three or four angles they can choose from.

**4. Add reference B-roll clips.** Record short GIFs of shots from existing ads or viral videos with a free screen recorder that exports GIFs, paste each into a numbered table, and label it ("slow pour of coffee into the mug"). A couple of references per brief is plenty at first. They are ideas for creators who don't know what to film, not a shot list. Prefer your own reference footage where possible, since third-party clips can raise rights questions.

**5. Give a reason to try hard.** Even when the video is unpaid, show what could follow: a possible monthly retainer, sales commission, a cut of spend on ads that win, and real income if their video turns into a top performer.

**6. Stay hands-off.** Bounce ideas around with the creator, point them in a direction, and let their personality come through. Offer a full script only to creators who ask for one or who are good actors.

**7. Adapt rather than narrow.** Make gender-specific versions only to move the same idea into a different setting, not to restrict who sees it. When one gender barely applies, keep a single open campaign, or ask creators explain why someone else in their life needs the product.

**8. Allow time and test the brief itself.** For products whose results take a while, give creators a couple of weeks of use before filming. Make clear that sample ads are for inspiration only. Give separate creator groups different frameworks to learn which brief performs.

**9. Expect the second video to be better.** First videos often aren't winners. Onboarding, communication and feedback after video one are what produce the winner on video two.

Creator UGC tends to perform best in broad product-aware markets, and better still when the product has a distinctive mechanism.

## Example
Say a drinkware brand briefs creators on an insulated tumbler. The master framework has nine concepts, including a car-roof survival story (hook: "I drove off with this on my roof...") and "ice still there at 5pm". A creator who films gym content gets the durability and all-day-cold concepts; a parent who films school runs gets the car-roof and spill-proof ones. Each brief carries two reference GIFs (a slow pour and an ice-rattle shot) and the retainer ladder. One gym creator's second video, after feedback on the first, becomes the top spender.

## Common mistakes
- Sending one identical brief to everyone.
- Long brand essays that nobody reads.
- Scripts that don't fit who the creator is.
- Treating reference clips as strict instructions.
- Giving up on a creator after one average first video.
