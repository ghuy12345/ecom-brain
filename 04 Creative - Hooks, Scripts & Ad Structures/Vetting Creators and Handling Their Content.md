# Vetting Creators and Handling Their Content

## What it is
The operational middle of any creator programme: choosing the right people from a pile of applications, keeping deliveries moving, and organising what arrives so editors can turn it into ads fast. It applies to both free seeding (see [[Product Seeding Campaigns]]) and paid UGC briefs.

Paid and free campaigns behave differently. Creators gravitate toward briefs that pay: on a niche product, one paid brief can draw more applicants than several seeding briefs combined. Once money changes hands, selection is about quality rather than quantity, so raise your bar.

## When to use it
- Applications are arriving and you need consistent criteria, especially if an assistant will run the process.
- Deliveries are slipping and creators have gone quiet.

## How (steps)
**1. Study the brand first.** Look at its site, socials and existing content so you judge creators against something concrete.

**2. Review each application.**
- *The pitch*: is it tailored, with a specific video idea? A strong application describes the video, the angle and why it fits the buyer (for example, a parent offering a start-to-finish demo of a kids' baking kit made with her daughter, framed as a gift).
- *Their socials*: are they comfortable on camera, what's their style, do their posts get watched, and are they at the same stage of life as your typical user?
- *Their marketplace page*: is it filled in, is the portfolio good, and what have previous brands said?
- *Follower count*: a weak signal on its own. One clever video can outweigh a small following.

**3. Decide quickly.** Yes goes to the shortlist; when unsure, pass, as applicants are plentiful. A dead profile link deserves a quick request for a new one, not a rejection. Missing or unverifiable profiles are a warning sign. If something feels off, pass even when the niche looks perfect.

**4. Shortlist before hiring.** Signal interest, add them to the shortlist, and wait for brand sign-off if someone else approves. Send a holding message that explains any delay and uses the creator's correct name.

**5. Write the criteria down.** If an assistant will vet, a written rubric (tailored pitch, on camera, life-stage match, reviews, views) replaces your intuition.

**6. For paid UGC, set rates and budgets.**
- Around $100 to $150 per video is a sensible range; going far above about $200 is rarely justified.
- Set aside a four-figure budget at minimum, more if you want creators fast. Without that, stick to seeding.
- Expect a platform fee on top, plus any subscription plan cost.
- Put both the product's price and the creator's fee in the brief.
- Open one campaign per customer type instead of a single catch-all. Niche products (specialist sports gear, say) are harder to source for.

**7. Keep deliveries moving.**
- When the product ships, tell them and ask them to confirm receipt; follow up a day or three later if needed. Tell them to expect it within about a week.
- On arrival, say you're looking forward to their content.
- While waiting, check in every two or three days, a little looser over weekends, and never let a week pass without contact. Adapt for circumstances such as travel.
- Use the platform's filters to see who still owes content.
- If a video is weak, request one reshoot and then move on; a free product earns goodwill, not studio quality.
- Be human with creators. Fast, responsive ones are your future ambassadors; flag them.

**8. File content consistently.** Request the unedited footage too, not just their final cut. Store it all in a shared drive, nested by brand, then content, then creator. Mark the deal complete once acceptable content arrives.

**9. Triage for quick-cut ads.** Some clips can be chopped into three short variations with almost no editing; mark those so editors go straight to them. A simple convention: creator name plus a tick when variations are ready, creator name plus a cross when the footage needs real work.
- *Usable*: someone on screen discussing the product, in a clip short enough to chop.
- *Not usable for quick cuts*: nothing happening on screen, nobody mentions the product, or the clip runs very long.
You're judging edit effort, not worth; archive everything.

## Example
Say a kids' craft brand receives forty applications. The assistant's rubric quickly removes twenty with generic pitches or no on-camera content, leaving twelve. The brand approves seven. Five deliver within two weeks after regular check-ins; two need one reshoot request. In the drive, three creator folders get a tick (chatty, product-focused clips under 30 seconds), and the editor cuts nine ads in an afternoon.

## Common mistakes
- Choosing by follower count.
- Mixing up creators' names in messages.
- Letting a creator go a week or more without contact.
- Pouring editing time into weak footage instead of easy quick cuts.
