# Hooks by Awareness Stage

## What it is
A hook only scales if it speaks to the right awareness level. Strong click-through or hook rate is the result; the reason underneath is that the opener met the viewer at their current level. Copying a big brand's visual style without that match is not strategy.

The general rule: the higher in the funnel you aim, the harder the ad is to write and the more people it can reach. Unaware ads are hardest and most scalable; most-aware ads are easiest and smallest. You can still build meaningful volume with product-aware ads in high-search categories, mostly by improving a proven performer.

## When to use it
- Before writing any hook, to decide what the opening clip and line must do.
- When two hooks with the same callout and benefit perform very differently: the gap is often awareness, not wording.
- When a winner plateaus and you want to reach a new pool by moving up a level.

## How (steps)
1. **Decide the stage** the ad speaks to (the levels themselves are in [[Market Awareness Levels]]).
2. **Match the hook to the stage.**

| Stage | What the hook does | Typical opening clip |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Story, secret, surprising information, an expert revealing something new | A story moment, the problem, or the ideal outcome |
| Problem aware | Names the pain, then points toward a fix | The problem visibly happening |
| Solution aware | Shows the kind of solution, then that you offer it | The solution in use |
| Product aware | Why yours beats the alternatives: features, reviews, proof | The product working |
| Most aware | Offer, price, urgency | The product, kept simple, with the deal |

3. **Pick a clip type** from four common options: a relevant odd visual, the ideal outcome, the problem, or the product. Use the table to choose.
4. **Add hook text** that names the right person, offers a benefit and makes them want the next line.
5. **Apply an enhancer** if the hook feels flat:
   - *Reverse the clip*: footage played backwards (a bottle dropped onto a table seems to leap back into the hand) adds instant novelty.
   - *Shift the perspective*: say it as someone else would. "I can't believe how much weight you lost" beats "I lost weight", since people mind how others judge them, their kids and their homes.
6. **Check relevance of any shock visual.** A random stunt for a dental product rarely works; a relevant sensory clip (foam, a satisfying peel) often does.

## Example
Say you sell a back-support cushion. One product, five hooks:
- Unaware: an expert explains a little-known reason desk workers' lower backs fail by midafternoon, told as a story.
- Problem aware: "Lower back aching by 3pm?" over a shot of someone wincing as they stand.
- Solution aware: "This is what fixed my desk-chair back pain" with the cushion in use.
- Product aware: "Why this beats memory foam" with a side-by-side compression test and reviews.
- Most aware: "Half price this week" with the product and the offer, nothing else.

A subtle case: say two hook texts for an air purifier share the same callout and benefit. One assumed viewers already wanted cleaner air (solution aware). The other told them something they did not know about how dry, dirty air affects health (a higher stage, more curiosity). The second would usually win, because it meets people earlier in their awareness.

## Common mistakes
- Treating a high hook rate as proof the stage is right; it may be stopping the wrong people.
- Using most-aware messages (discounts) on cold audiences who do not know why they need the product.
- Unaware hooks that never connect to the product, so the story is enjoyed and forgotten.
- Shock visuals with no link to the offer.
- Hook text that only grabs attention and neither names the buyer nor promises anything.
