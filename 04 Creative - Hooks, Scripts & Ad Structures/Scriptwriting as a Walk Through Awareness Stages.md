# Scriptwriting as a Walk Through Awareness Stages

## What it is
A way to see every ad structure as the same device: pick the awareness stage the viewer is at when the ad starts, then walk them down, stage by stage, until they are most aware and ready to buy. A typical short ad maps like this:

- Hook: problem aware ("Can't get your toddler to sleep past 5am?")
- Prompting question: solution aware ("What if the room just stayed dark?")
- Product introduction and reviews: product aware
- Closing ask: most aware

The entry point can be any level, product aware included. Wherever you start, every later section has a clear job. If you know what is new about your solution (the mechanism), it belongs in the solution and product sections. Product-aware ads in particular tend to lead with the mechanism, showing how it solves what rival products don't.

Scripts come from research. If the research is thin, scripting feels hard. Inputs you need: the concept, the target persona, the product's unique selling point, desires, sophistication, beliefs, and the pains, objections and questions people raise in forums and reviews.

## When to use it
- Writing a script for a stage other than product aware, where it is easy to skip steps.
- Checking a draft that jumps straight from hook to "buy now".
- Moving an existing winner up or down an awareness level as an iteration.

## How (steps)
1. **Draft.** Draft one opening line per awareness level. Pick where to enter, read the lines top to bottom to test the flow, then grow each into conversational sentences. The product-aware part will run longest, since it holds benefits, the demo, features and proof.
2. **Reinforce.**
   - Paint both pictures: make the pain vivid and the solved life vivid ("heaven and hell").
   - Check the hook does three things: names who it's for, makes them curious, and hints at a payoff. Add whichever is missing.
   - Dig deeper into the pain with more emotional, concrete words.
   - Add a time frame to the outcome ("within two weeks"); a result with a deadline feels real.
3. **Refine.** Read it aloud and rewrite anything awkward. Cut words that add no selling power. Swap in the persona's own phrases from research. Expect to need more than one pass.
4. **Plan visuals.** Borrow the look of content this persona already consumes, then dramatise the claim with visual metaphors. Show the good as very good and the bad as very bad. A disruptive visual is fine as long as the right viewer still recognises themselves.
5. **Reflect.** Come back to it later and ask:
   1. Within three seconds, is the target named or their problem visible?
   2. Is on-screen text legible on a phone and suited to the persona? Fast edits and tiny text lose older viewers.
   3. Would it blend into this persona's usual feed?
   4. Does the language sound like them?

## Example
Say you sell a posture corrector, entering at problem aware.
- Problem aware: "If your neck aches after every workday, it's probably not your pillow."
- Solution aware: "Physios have a name for it, and the fix is retraining how your shoulders sit, not more stretching."
- Product aware: the corrector's mechanism (it gently pulls the shoulders back so the muscles relearn the position), a demonstration, then three customer clips.
- Most aware: "Wear it 20 minutes a day; most people feel the difference within two weeks. 30-day money-back guarantee."
In the reinforce pass, the hook gains a stronger pain image (a woman rubbing her neck at a laptop at 9pm), and "feel better" becomes "turn your head to reverse the car without wincing". In the refine pass, "discomfort" becomes "that burning knot", a phrase lifted from reviews.

## Common mistakes
- Skipping a stage, usually going from problem straight to product with no solution framing.
- Robotic UGC lines that no real person would say.
- Never checking the wording with real people from the audience.
- Borrowing a service-business offer structure for a physical product; the awareness walk transfers, the offer shape does not.
- Concluding an angle failed when the execution (hook, legibility, native look) was the real fault.
