# Founder Ads

## What it is
What sells in a founder ad is the case the founder makes, not the fact that the founder appears. Almost everything that decides performance happens at the script stage, before filming. It works because it combines authenticity (a real person rather than paid talent), authority without celebrities, and trust. But those three alone produce bland ads; the script is what converts.

This is not personal branding either. Plenty of high-spending founder ads star founders nobody has heard of; paid media supplies the reach. Viewers don't care about the founder as such: the story only sells when it is framed around the viewer's own situation.

## When to use it
- Commoditised categories where a specific persona-problem-story differentiates you cheaply.
- When the founder can credibly say things nobody else can (see the story moat below).
- As one content type among several, never the only one. When one face carries most of the budget, a single tired concept can drag the whole account down.

## How (steps)
1. **Find the story moat.** Three claims that sound credible only from a founder:
   - *Origin*: the reason the company was started.
   - *Mechanism*: what the product does differently.
   - *Adversarial argument*: you tested the competition and it fell short.
   Personal results are not a moat; let customers who resemble the viewer deliver those.
2. **Tell it through the viewer's eyes.** Recognition ("that's me"), make the problem sting, raise a question, explain how the product works, then answer the question.
3. **Use a proven structure.** One that performs well: adversarial claim, immediate proof of that claim, mechanism tied to the founder's story, evidence for the mechanism (factory shots, independent tests, labels), then a call to action with real scarcity and customer proof. A claim with no proof right behind it does not work.
4. **Multiply in scripting, not filming.** Ten hooks times three genuinely different bodies is 30 ads; two endings make 60. Keep two to four hooks per body (eight at most); dozens of near-identical hooks on one body spread a test budget so thin nothing exits learning. Every hook must fit the body it sits on.
5. **Vary the founder's involvement.** On camera; voice over B-roll; evidence only (lab, factory, hands); a proxy (an employee, a dietitian, a supplier); or absent (animation of the journey).
6. **Vary the founder's role.** Protagonist ("here's why I made this"), witness ("an email from a customer last week..."), interviewer ("I put the question to our chemist"), reader (a one-star review read out on camera).
7. **Build a concept matrix** covering role, proof, offer, angle, awareness and persona. Swap any one of these and you have a fresh concept. Offer is the easiest to change, persona the hardest; reach a new persona through true neighbouring stories, such as the founder's mother using the product.
8. **Stockpile story inputs.** Static inputs (origin, the founder's personal problem) stay consistent. Renewable inputs (reviews, support emails, supplier calls, complaints) keep scripts fresh; pipe them into one place so that one filming session a month has every script ready.
9. **Edit properly.** An unedited talking head should never go live: cuts, overlays, motion and changes of scenery. If the founder freezes on a teleprompter, give a bullet outline. Stiffness in the first sessions is normal; when a team concludes the format failed, the real cause is often a founder who had not yet relaxed on camera.
10. **Keep the funnel balanced.** Aim most founder ads at the colder awareness stages. A library of ingredient-and-dosage founder ads only harvests existing demand.
11. **Test the posting identity.** Running a founder ad from the founder's own profile as a partnership ad can lift results, but only if that profile has relevant content.

## Example
Say you run a protein-bar brand. Seed concept: a doubtful buyer new to the category, problem aware. Hook: "I sent five best-selling protein bars to a lab. Three of them lied on the label." Proof: the lab report on screen. Mechanism: why your bar uses whole-food protein, tied to the founder's own gut issues. Proof of mechanism: a walk through the kitchen and the third-party certificate. Close: "We sell out every month; this batch ships Friday," plus a wall of reviews. Swap the role to "reader" (the founder reads a one-star competitor review) and the offer to a variety bundle, and you have new concepts from the same hour of filming.

## Common mistakes
- Fabricating a founder story. It is unethical, customers find out, and it destroys brand and exit value. If there is no personal origin story, build on the mechanism or on customer insight instead.
- Raw, unedited footage.
- Putting the product on screen in the opening seconds, which flags the video as an ad.
- Opening a curiosity gap and never paying it off.
- Running only founder ads, and producing variants of a fatigued concept instead of new concepts.
