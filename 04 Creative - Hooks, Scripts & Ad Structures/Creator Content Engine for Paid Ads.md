# Creator Content Engine for Paid Ads

## What it is
A repeatable system that turns a pool of creators into a steady stream of test ads. Each creator is effectively a strategist, writer, camera operator and editor rolled into one, and paying a group of them per video typically costs far less than staffing those roles separately. With enough creators filming regularly, finding winners becomes a matter of throughput rather than luck.

The engine produces paid-ad assets, not organic posts. Many brands set it up ahead of hiring a strategist: once a winner turns up, strategy is run off that ad and the learning goes straight back to the creators.

## When to use it
- You need more test volume than your in-house team can make.
- Seeding has surfaced a few good creators and you want to systematise.
- You are deciding whether to hire strategists or build a creator pipeline first.

## How (steps)
**1. Framework.** Write one document covering what creators must know: brand, product, tone and opening ideas (see [[Creator Briefs - Frameworks, Not Scripts]]).

**2. Casting and outreach.** Choose where to recruit, sift and shortlist applicants, raise orders, send product and log every delivery (see [[Vetting Creators and Handling Their Content]]). A creator marketplace is usually the main source at first; over time your own network of past creators becomes the best one. Casting-style platforms tend to produce content that looks less like ads.

**3. Production.** Creators film with the product and the framework and submit. You cut each video into about three variations with different openings, and launch them. Raw, barely edited UGC can be a serious revenue source in its own right; by default it tests in the same campaign as your edited ads, with a separate campaign only when you want to compare creators against each other. Raw ads also act as a signal source for what to put into produced work.

**4. Review and reinvest.** Monthly and quarterly, look at UGC spend, how many winners it produced and how much spend those winners absorbed. Sign the creators who produce winners as ambassadors; when budget allows, move from free seeding to paid briefs. Then repeat.

**Illustrative volume:** ten creators each delivering one or two videos a week, cut three ways, adds roughly 30 test ads a week with little internal effort beyond onboarding.

**Staffing:** an assistant who recruits, runs campaigns and chases deliveries, plus one person who feeds in ideas and otherwise lets creators get on with it.

**5. Name every ad by creator.** Without creator names in ad names you cannot filter, report or judge profitability.

**6. Send each creator a performance report.** Creators default to organic instincts, and what works organically often fails as an ad. Showing them paid results turns them into better strategists.
- Build a saved report per creator in an ad analytics tool, filtered by their name, and share the link or a short walkthrough video.
- Useful metrics: thumb-stop ratio (did the first frame stop people), the share of those who continued to about 15 seconds, overall hold, conversion rate, outbound click-through, shares and average play time.
- A high thumb-stop means the opener caught attention; a high conversion rate means it caught buyers.
- Read the comments on their ads and turn patterns into the next brief.
- Share the results with every creator, whatever their pay basis, so they can see which openers and ideas earn spend.

**7. Define a winning creator by spend.** A winning creator is one whose ad the account can scale. What counts is budget absorbed at an acceptable return, not polish. Two creators can both deliver appealing videos while just one of them produces an ad that soaks up real spend at target.

## Example
Say an outdoor-apparel brand runs twelve seeded creators. Each video is cut three ways and named with the creator's handle, product and concept. After a month, two creators' ads have absorbed most of the UGC spend at target; the rest barely spent. Both get reports: one creator's hooks have a high thumb-stop but low conversion (fans, not buyers), so the next brief asks her to open on a cold commute rather than a scenic hike. Comments on a coat ad show many husbands buying it for their wives, so the next brief includes a "the gift my husband got me" concept.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming only in-house marketers can make ads.
- Leaving creator names out of ad names, so profitability review is impossible.
- Judging creators on how much you like their content rather than on spend absorbed.
- Leaving creators guessing about what works in paid.
- Over-relying on influencer-owned content without clear usage rights agreed up front.
