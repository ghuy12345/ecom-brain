# Writing the Bridge After the Hook

## What it is
A video ad can be split into three parts: the **hook** (stop the scroll), the **bridge** (keep attention) and the **hold** (the body that sells). Those few seconds after the hook are where most viewers choose whether to keep watching. It connects whatever the hook raised to the reason the product exists.

A useful test: if you cannot write a sensible bridge from a hook, the hook is defective. Change the hook rather than forcing the connection.

## When to use it
- Every time you write multiple hooks for one ad. Give each hook its own bridge instead of funnelling every hook into one shared second line; it also makes the variations genuinely different in the ad platform.
- When hold rate drops right after the hook even though hook rate is healthy.
- When building a hook-bridge-hold script, whether by hand or with an AI first draft (see [[Drafting Video Ad Scripts With an LLM]]).

## How (steps)
1. **Write the hook first**, then ask what the viewer is now wondering.
2. **Pick a bridge type** (these are common patterns, not a closed list, and they can be stacked):
   1. *Introduce the mechanism.* Name what in the product creates the result, and what deals with the linked problem: "Watch how this stops shedding" then "It's the enzyme coating that actually does it." Rarely used, and at its best with visual products and buyers who already know the category. Show the result rather than describe it.
   2. *Escalate the problem.* Make it feel more serious or more personal: "I brushed his teeth every week, and by spring his breath was somehow worse." Show the worse outcome on screen rather than just using the word.
   3. *Escalate the desire.* Take the hoped-for outcome as far as it will go: a dog dozing off mid nail trim, strangers refusing to believe someone's age.
   4. *Reposition the problem.* Reframe what is really behind it, ideally through an authority ("Scratch mittens treat the symptom; the trigger is somewhere else"). Pairs well with an advertorial or listicle that continues the explanation. Stay compliant: say "could be" and avoid claiming a definitive cause.
   5. *Hint at something new.* Tease something surprising, ideally from an expert ("A vet told me something about kibble I'd never heard").
   6. *Set the timeline.* Tell them how long this takes or what they are about to learn: "Your skin, day by day, for one week." Set up credibility before the timeline so it is believed.
3. **Stack when useful**, for example escalate the problem and then set the timeline.
4. **Lead into the hold.** A reliable hold runs: objection, claim, proof, main benefit, secondary benefit, call to action. Keep a short ad to roughly 45 seconds.
5. **Use specifics from research.** Swap generic lines such as "you've tried everything" for the specific products and routines your audience has tried, pulled from forums and reviews.
6. **Test opening on the bridge (starting mid-action).** Finish the ad, delete the hook, and watch it. If it still makes sense, add it as an extra variation: three hooks become four variants. Some strong ads open directly on a mechanism bridge. Also scan deeper in the edit for action shots that could start the video.

## Example
Say you sell a dandruff shampoo with three hooks:
- Hook A, "If you've got white flakes on your shoulders, watch this," pairs with *escalate the problem*: "Turns out the flakes aren't the real issue; what's happening on your scalp is worse."
- Hook B, "Watch how this stops flaking in one wash," pairs with *mechanism*: "It's the zinc complex that actually calms the scalp, not the scent."
- Hook C, "Your scalp, one week, day by day," pairs with *timeline*: after a dermatologist-style intro, day one, day three, day seven.
All three lead into the same hold: "You've tried anti-dandruff shampoos that dry your hair out" (objection), the claim, a before-and-after (proof), soft hair as a second benefit, then the offer.

## Common mistakes
- One generic bridge after every hook, so the variations are near-identical.
- Bridges that ignore what the hook raised.
- Telling instead of showing ("it gets worse", "it's easy"). Easy claims invite "easy for you"; show a normal day instead.
- Too-specific numbers that narrow the audience more than intended.
- Repositioning the problem with medical certainty that breaks ad policy.
