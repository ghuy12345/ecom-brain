# Video Hook Fundamentals

## What it is
The hook is roughly the first three seconds of a video ad: the visual, the on-screen text and the first spoken line working together. When an ad underperforms, the hook is the most likely culprit, so it deserves more drafting time than any other part of the script.

A working hook has four jobs, and treat it as a checklist, not a template to fill in:

1. **Stop the scroll.** Grab attention through the visual, the text overlay (which behaves like the ad's headline) and the opening words. Sound is a secondary lever: a whisper, silence, or a real-world noise such as packaging crinkling.
2. **Qualify the viewer.** Make it obvious who the ad is for. Name the persona, the specific pain, the specific benefit, or let the visual do it (talent who look like the buyer, a close-up of the problem).
3. **Open a loop.** Leave a question hanging that the viewer can only answer by staying.
4. **Give hope.** Signal that sticking around will pay off. That promise concerns what the next seconds of video deliver, not just what the product does.

Jobs 1 and 2 mostly drive hook rate (the share of viewers who get past the first seconds). Jobs 3 and 4 drive hold rate (how many keep watching). Most weak hooks stop after attention and callout, which is why they get views and few sales.

## When to use it
- Every time you write or brief a video ad, before writing the body.
- When an ad has decent spend but poor hold rate or poor conversion: check which of the four jobs is missing.
- When reviewing an editor's cut, because the visual and overlay can complete or break jobs the script assumed were done.

## How (steps)
1. **Decide who must stop.** Platforms read your opening words and images to decide who should see the ad, so a vague opener teaches the algorithm to find the wrong people. Qualify early.
2. **Choose the attention lever.** Lead with a visual that connects to the product, pain or benefit. A shock image unrelated to the offer pumps up hook rate while stopping people who will never buy.
3. **Qualify in the cheapest way.** Where possible let the visual qualify (the right person, the problem on screen) so the spoken line can carry the loop.
4. **Open the loop.** Options: keep the product hidden and refer to the solution by a nickname ("a two-minute fix"), challenge a belief the viewer holds, or promise a list.
5. **Add the payoff signal.** Make it clear that the next 20 seconds answer something they care about.
6. **Test for stickiness, not just clickiness.** Ask three questions: does it spark a feeling right away (emotion), does it open a gap viewers must keep watching to close (curiosity), and is something major riding on the outcome (stakes)? Stakes can be carried by the image instead of the words, so editors need to grasp them too.
7. **Follow the four supporting rules:**
   - Open in the middle of the action, whether that is an argument or a scene already under way.
   - No backstory; the viewer did not plan to watch, so never make them feel they missed context.
   - No chaos; heavy cuts and sensory overload make people confused, and confused people scroll. Aim for curious, not confused.
   - Make it work with the sound off.

## Example
Say you sell a digestive supplement. Weak hook: a smiling presenter says "Meet the supplement everyone is talking about." It grabs mild attention, qualifies nobody and reveals the product with no mystery.

Rebuilt hook: close-up of an unusual-looking jar beside a bloated stomach emoji in the overlay (attention plus qualification without words). The presenter says, "I stopped taking probiotics after I found out what this one ingredient does" (a loop and a challenged belief). Overlay: "the 10-second morning fix" (hope tied to what the video will reveal). All four jobs are done before second three.

A hook can also go further than the buyer: a keepsake jewellery ad might open with a pregnancy test being smashed with a hammer. Emotion, gap and stakes are all present and almost anyone will watch, which is fine as long as the story then narrows to the buyer.

## Common mistakes
- Grabbing attention and naming the buyer, then offering no loop or payoff, so hold rate collapses.
- Showing the product immediately, leaving nothing to discover.
- Judging a hook by hook rate alone. Earning a glance and then losing the viewer still counts as failure; you want purchases, not bites.
- Opening with "for everyone" language that tells neither viewer nor algorithm who the ad is for.
- Over-edited openers that need sound or context to make sense.
