# Iterating a Winning Ad in Order

## What it is
Scaling needs a steady stream of new creative, and inventing brand-new concepts every week has a high failure rate. The sustainable supply is systematic iteration: treat every winning ad as an asset, squeeze it for variations, and only revive it through new versions rather than abandoning it when it tires. Iterations make up the bulk of weekly output; fully new concepts are the smaller share.

The key is *order*. Each stage builds on winners from the stage before, and you change one thing at a time so you know what caused the result.

## When to use it
- After an ad has proven itself: at least about a week of steady spend at or near your target, not a two-day spike.
- When a long-running winner starts to fatigue and you want to extend its life.

## How (steps)
**0. Validate, then dissect.** Wait for roughly a week of spend before treating an ad as a winner. Then break it into the parts that matter: angle, mechanism, authority and avatar. Stay at that level rather than splitting it into trivia. Form a view on what carries it (the creator, the setting, the message, the format) and test that view by isolating one element at a time against the original, which stays live as the control.

**1. Hook iterations (cheapest and fastest).** Change only the opening:
- New opening footage: different location, camera angle, B-roll, pace.
- New voice: accent, age, gender, a human voice instead of a synthetic one, or narration added to a silent cut.
- New music: upbeat vs emotional, trending sound vs original.
Headline and copy changes on statics belong here too.

**2. Script iterations (angle fixed).**
- Shorter or longer: cut a 35-second ad with five benefits down to the three that matter, or extend it for audiences who need more education. Shorter wins when nothing important is lost.
- Move the social proof: try it at the start, middle and end, or add a review montage.
- Ask what buyers still need: more time on the problem? failed alternatives? an objection answered? fewer words?
- Different ways of saying it: new wording that still communicates the *same* value and angle. If the meaning drifts, it is no longer an iteration of the winner.

**3. Creator iterations.** Same script, music and format with a different person on screen: a different gender, age, background or stage of life. People buy from people who look like them, so this round often yields the most winners for products with broad appeal.

**4. Format changes.** Carry the message into new formats: voiceover video, static, comparison, before-and-after, review compilation, organic-style screenshot. Sometimes a format carries the message better than the original did. For statics, keep your strongest primary text; fresh body copy seldom outperforms it, native-style ads aside.

**5. Mix and match (needs a pool of winners).** Splice proven openers, problem setups, demonstrations, proof, mechanism explainers and closes from different winners. Apply a winning script to another product in the range, or the same structure to a different problem.

**6. Broaden.** Once the core is exhausted, move outward:
- *Avatar*: a neighbouring sub-avatar with the same desire.
- *Awareness*: drop to a product-aware version that opens on the mechanism, or climb to an unaware version.
- *Emotional zone*: test a different valence and intensity (see [[Emotional Valence and Intensity]]).
- *Identity*: frame it for a self-image ("if you're the kind of person who...").
- *Start mid-action*: open on the most dramatic moment.
- *Landing page*: where it makes sense, give the angle its own matching page.

**Rules**
- Don't skip ahead: mixing and matching without winners is guessing, and script rewrites before hooks are validated waste editing time.
- One variable per iteration; keep everything else identical.
- Build fresh, high-quality assets that emphasise the winning element rather than recycling the same footage forever.
- Iterations that are too small can be treated as the same ad by the platform; if a whole round shows no difference, the steps were too timid.

## Example
Say a reusable water bottle has a UGC winner: a commuter showing the bottle staying cold all day. Round one tests three new opening shots and a different voice. Round two cuts the ad from 40 to 20 seconds and moves a "12,000 reviews" moment to the start. Round three re-shoots the same script with a gym-goer and a parent on the school run; the parent version wins. Round four turns the parent version into a static comparison and a review compilation. Round five broadens to an unaware hook about how much plastic a family throws away in a year.

## Common mistakes
- Calling a winner after two good days.
- Changing hook, length and creator in one go.
- Rewording until the ad says something different from the original.
- Jumping to new angles while cheap hook and creator iterations remain untested.
