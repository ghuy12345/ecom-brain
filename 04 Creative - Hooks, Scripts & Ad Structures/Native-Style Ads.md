# Native-Style Ads

## What it is
A native-style ad is camouflaged creative: an image that does not show the product (a tree for a memorial urn, a cluttered kitchen counter for a storage product) paired with long, story-led primary text that eventually sells. It looks like a post from a real person rather than a brand.

It converts for two reasons:
- **No brand bias.** People drop their guard when the source does not look like an advertiser.
- **The image does the stopping.** An odd or striking photo invites people to guess what it means and then read on to check, so you do not need a perfect headline. This is juxtaposition in visual form: an unexplained image paired with a line that only later makes sense.

A principle that reaches beyond native ads: the most persuasive creative leaves readers feeling there is no real alternative, without attacking competitors by name. Ask what a biased seller would obviously say, and avoid saying it; communicate it instead.

## When to use it
- Photo-led placements, where a picture stops the scroll and the headline just needs to reward that.
- Products with a story behind them, or a reason to buy that is better told than shown.
- As a different execution of an angle that already works in UGC or standard statics.
- Native ads can scale sent straight to product pages; prove the ad works before you invest in the page.

## How (steps)
1. **Start from the angle and mechanism**, not the image. Decide who it is for, what they struggle with and why your product solves it.
2. **Choose an image that creates a question** and that the story can logically connect to the product. If the link is forced, readers feel tricked.
3. **Write the story copy so it walks the awareness stages**: an opening that matches the image, the problem, the failed alternatives, the discovery, the mechanism, proof, then the offer. Long copy that does this can send traffic straight to a product page without a pre-lander.
4. **Make claims visual and checkable.** Concrete details persuade; opinion does not.
5. **Set up the test**: three images, three primary texts and two headlines, with each text written for its image. Test one variable at a time. Take bigger swings first (different images and stories), then get specific.
6. **Iterate from learnings.** Form a reason why a winner wins and emphasise it. The image and headline carry most of the weight. You can turn a winning native image ad into video by rewriting the story as spoken script while keeping its structure.
7. **Settle headline-vs-image debates.** If forced to choose, the headline matters more in most placements. In photo-led placements a striking image earns the stop, and then the headline must pay it off. When the words are strong but the picture undercuts them, replace the picture.

**Keep it honest.** Native ads are often written by pasting a prompt and inventing a persona ("I'm a nurse of 20 years and..."). It builds no skill, works only briefly and is ethically dubious, and fabricated testimonials can breach advertising rules. Tell true stories (a real customer, the real founder) or write in a clearly brand voice. Brand-building creative earns more over time.

## Example
Say you sell a biodegradable memorial urn that grows into a tree. Image: an ordinary young oak in a backyard at golden hour, no product. Primary text: "This tree is my grandfather. When he passed, we couldn't face a cold shelf of ashes..." The story covers the problem, what the family tried, discovering the urn, how it works, and closes on the offer and a guarantee. Headline: "The way he'd have wanted to be remembered." The test pairs three images (the oak, a seedling in hands, a family planting) with a story for each, then iterates on the winning image with a second story.

## Common mistakes
- A striking image with no logical bridge to the product, so curiosity turns into distrust.
- Invented personas and fake testimonials.
- Lecturing instead of telling a story.
- Treating a high click-through rate as success when the topic is broad and dramatic but never connects to the product; fewer clicks with more intent is better.
- Changing image, story and headline all at once, so you learn nothing.
