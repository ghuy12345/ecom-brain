# Diagnosing Ad Execution Problems

## What it is
When the research is solid and the avatar is clear, what usually decides the outcome is **execution**: the actual wording and the actual footage. An execution failure is an ad with a correct angle and a well-understood buyer that still does not sell. Plenty of good angles get thrown away because nobody managed to express them well.

Think of the decisions as layers: concept, then angle, then variation, then format, then how you test, and finally execution. This note covers that final layer. AI does not remove the gap between writers; people who already know good copy get far better results from it. And formats that are trivial to produce, like template statics with machine-written headlines, are crowded, so the message itself has to do more.

## When to use it
- A well-researched angle keeps losing.
- An "improved" iteration performs worse than the original.
- You need to decide whether to keep pushing an angle or move on.

## How (steps)
**1. Split angle from execution.** Express the same angle three ways, for example a product shot with a headline, a designed comparison static and a narrated B-roll video.
- Nothing works anywhere: strategy is the likelier culprit, although weak execution can't be ruled out.
- At least one shows promise: the angle is alive; improve how it is communicated.

**2. Prove your execution on familiar ground.** Use formats and script shapes that have already scaled for you or your industry before chasing new avatars, adapting them properly to the new angle. Benchmark against ads that scale beyond yours, not merely ones that win.

**3. Check the common faults:**
1. *Flat emotion.* Copy that sounds like the buyer's private thoughts hits harder; vivid specifics ("muffin top") beat abstractions ("weight").
2. *Nothing to wonder about.*
3. *No reason to keep watching*, or a payoff that never arrives.
4. *Too hard to read.* Target about sixth grade; "metabolic gummies" sounds academic, "gummies for your tummy" does not.
5. *Unsupported claims.* Add rankings, credentials or numbers. Odd, precise figures feel true; round ones feel made up.
6. *Commands without reasons.* Replace "stop scrolling" with a reason to stay.
7. *Me-too claims.* Search the ad library before leading with a benefit.
8. *Copy that reads like an essay.* Say it out loud or dictate it.
9. *Written to please yourself* rather than the buyer.
10. *Angle too vague* for anyone to see themselves in it.
11. *Angle the whole market already uses*, a sophistication issue.
12. *Edit style mismatched to the age group*, such as a lab-heavy explainer for a young audience used to casual creator clips.
13. *Format unsuited to the angle.*
14. *Bait with no reward*, which destroys trust.

**4. Check the emotional zone.** Every ad sits on two axes: valence (positive to negative) and intensity (calm to aroused). A common hidden reason an "improved" version loses is that it slid into another zone, such as a calm, reassuring winner rewritten as an excited promise. Classify your winners' hooks by zone (AI can help at first), keep at least one in three iterations in the zone that works, and move between zones within an ad gradually, via reassurance. See [[Emotional Valence and Intensity]].

**5. Treat soft metrics as clues.** About a quarter of viewers getting past the hook, hold near a third of that, and click-through around 1 percent or better are rough reference points. Ads break these rules in both directions, so let ROAS and acquisition cost decide.

**6. Ask the right questions of every ad.** Loser: why does the intended buyer not care, and where is the gap? Winner: why do people care, why does the platform favour it, why do they buy? Expect this to take a while per ad at first.

**7. Decide whether to persist.** Round one tests the angle. If your belief in the avatar holds, round two sharpens the targeting. Round three follows a proper review. Three failed rounds means you can't currently execute it; move on. Go deep on one or two avatars instead of hopping between many.

## Example
Say a robot-mop brand's "mops without streaks" angle fails twice. A quick library search shows every competitor leads with the same claim (fault 7), and the comparison static is full of spec jargon (fault 4). Round three keeps the angle but uses customers' words ("my floors stopped looking streaky"), adds a real before-and-after, and runs as a static, a talking head and a voiceover. Two of the three find spend: execution, not the angle, was the problem.

## Common mistakes
- Writing off a format after a single loss; the same template can flop with one message and scale with another.
- Dropping an angle after a single batch.
- Treating hook rate as the verdict.
- Iterating without noticing an emotional-zone shift.
- Pasting a script structure word for word into a new angle.
