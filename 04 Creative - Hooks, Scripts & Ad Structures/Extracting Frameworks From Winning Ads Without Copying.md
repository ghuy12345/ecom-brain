# Extracting Frameworks From Winning Ads Without Copying

## What it is
When you find an ad that works, the temptation is to copy it: same layout, same lines, your product. Inside your own niche that rarely produces a breakthrough, because you are copying what everyone else has already copied, and the audience has seen it. What transfers is the **framework**: the structure and the feeling each line creates, with the specific words removed.

This move is called *chunking up*. You rise one level of abstraction from the literal ad to the principle, then come back down into your own market. A good extraction passes one test: same shape, same emotion, entirely new wording.

Underneath the framework sits the **insight**: what the winning ad reveals about what the market cares about. Extract that too, not just the format. A winner that leans on a no-questions-asked return policy is really telling you the market fears wasting money, and that insight can power ads in any format.

## When to use it
- You have found a winner in another brand's account (see [[Studying Winning Ads You Did Not Make]]).
- You want to reuse one of your own winning scripts for a new mechanism or product.
- A hook style is saturated in your niche but you suspect its structure still works.

## How (steps)
1. **Take the literal line.** Write down exactly what the ad says.
2. **Name what it communicates emotionally.** Not what it says, but what the viewer feels.
3. **Write the framework** with the specifics removed, as a fill-in template.
4. **Refill it for your product,** in your market's language.
5. **Keep the speaker's perspective consistent.** If the original is first person, keep first person. "I just found out these brands have been lying to us" works better than a brand-voiced "companies are lying to you".
6. **Upgrade the words.** Stronger, more vivid verbs and nouns ("ripped off" rather than "disappointed").
7. **Test three versions:** one control that follows the framework almost one-to-one, and two reworks in your own wording. Your own rewording may underperform the near-copy, which is exactly why you test.
8. **Cross niches for close copies.** If you must follow a script structure closely, borrow it from an unrelated category, where it will feel new to your buyers.
9. **When reusing your own winner for a new mechanism,** pull out what it communicates and in what order, then write a related script rather than a clone. Simply swapping in a different mechanism is acceptable when your product has several that suit the same flow.

**Mechanics worth extracting** (from offer-led winners in particular):
- *Push and pull*: the hook raises a small worry that the next line dissolves. Resolve it quickly; dragging out the joke annoys viewers.
- *Power words with matching visuals*: a strong word paired with a reaction shot that shows the same emotion.
- *An odd setting* as a quiet pattern interrupt.
- *Repetition of the core offer* in copy and on screen.
- *Scarcity tied to the ad itself* ("this offer only through this video"), which discourages leaving to search later. Use it only if it is true.
- *A quick pivot to social proof and straight back to the offer*: in big product-aware markets, a strong offer stated plainly can lift an unremarkable ad without the full problem-solution arc.

Note the trade-off: heavily freebie-led offers can pull in lower-lifetime-value customers. That may be acceptable if the ad sells, but watch repeat rates.

## Example
Say a winning coffee ad opens with a creator saying she thinks she got scammed, then revealing the brand sent far more than she ordered.
- Literal: "Pretty sure I've been scammed." / "I ordered one bag and they sent me a whole box."
- Feeling: brief alarm, then relief and delight.
- Framework: "I think [something bad] just happened to me." / "I expected X, I got Y instead."
- Refill for a skincare serum: "I think my dermatologist is going to be annoyed with me." / "I bought this for my dark spots and my whole texture changed instead."
- Test: a near-literal control, plus two reworks with different worries and payoffs.

## Common mistakes
- Copying a competitor's ad line for line in the same niche.
- Copying the format but not the insight about what the market wants.
- Switching from first person to brand voice and losing the intimacy.
- Fake negativity dragged out too long, so viewers feel tricked.
- Fake scarcity.
- Skipping the control, so you cannot tell whether your rework helped.
