# Studying Winning Ads You Did Not Make

## What it is
You can't build a winner without knowing what one looks like. Most newcomers have never run one, so their idea of "good" is calibrated on ads that barely broke even at small budgets. Studying validated winners from other brands resets that standard.

The purpose is calibration, not copying. A well-built swipe file teaches you what winners tend to do (make the viewer feel something, teach them something, or make them curious, and give them a reason to keep watching) so your own judgment improves. When you do have winners of your own, apply the same method to them first.

## When to use it
- Before your first creative batch, or whenever you have no winners of your own.
- When your ads look "fine" but never take off and you suspect your standard is off.
- As a standing weekly habit for anyone writing or briefing ads.

## How (steps)
**1. Find real winners, not just old ads.**
- In an ad-spy tool, add the brand by its social page name (which can differ from the store name), limit results to roughly the last six months, and order them by **shares**; ads people share heavily are very often the best performers; long run time alone is a weaker signal and can hide the true best ad.
- Cross-check in the public ad library if a brand seems to be missing ads; tools have gaps.
- Look well outside your niche too. A structure borrowed from an unrelated category feels new to your buyers.
- Don't dismiss ugly ads. Plenty of winners look rough.

**2. Watch the whole funnel.** Watch the full ad, read the comments on the original post (free customer research), then follow the click to the landing page and offer.

**3. Annotate every ad in the same format:**
- Angle
- Awareness level (a judgement call; a niche product's buyers may be less aware than you assume)
- Core desire or desires it targets
- A beat-by-beat note of why it works
- A link back to the live ad and its comments

**4. Study the opening seconds closely.** Stop the video at the end of each phrase. Read the words first as a customer, then as a marketer, ignoring visuals while analysing the copy. For each beat, note the feeling it creates and the reason. Repeat "so why does that matter?" until you hit a principle, then generalise it one step so it fits your own brand.

**5. Look for portable principles.** Recurring ones include:
- Blame the category's old method or material, not a named competitor; it paves the way for your mechanism without bragging.
- Let someone else supply the credibility (experts abandoning the old way) instead of claiming to be the best. Implied claims often persuade more than stated ones.
- Break the price into a tangible unit (per meal, per wear).
- Use subtle desire cues in visuals, not only the product claim.
- Stay in the pain long enough before pitching.

**6. Check context.** Where available, note spend, ROAS, conversion rate, order value, frequency, hook and hold rates. Verify that the pain language is real by searching forums for it.

**7. Organise and revisit.** Group your swipe file by type: video, UGC, statics, offer ads, standout inspirational ads, long sales videos. Review with a teammate, and return after a couple of days with fresh eyes. Record your conclusions as hypotheses, not facts.

**8. Compare against your own losers.** Put your weakest ad beside a known winner and ask: does mine hit a core desire, create curiosity, give a reason to keep watching, and stay in the pain point?

## Example
Say you study a heavily shared men's apparel video. It opens on a negative frame about clothes that make men look bad, aimed at a core desire (being attractive) through fear, which tends to motivate more than aspiration. Nothing looks wrong in the shot, which creates a gap. A woman delivers the lines, which suits an ad aimed at men. The follow-up names three ways clothes go wrong, so at least one lands for everyone. The pitch arrives quite early; you note that sitting longer in the problem might improve it. The close pushes a multi-pack, which nudges order value up. Your annotation records the angle, problem awareness, the desire, these beats and the link.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the swipe file as a list of scripts to copy.
- Sorting only by run time.
- Noting surface details ("she's older", "there's a dog") instead of why it works emotionally.
- Studying only the hook and never the landing page.
- Studying very old ads that may no longer reflect what works.
