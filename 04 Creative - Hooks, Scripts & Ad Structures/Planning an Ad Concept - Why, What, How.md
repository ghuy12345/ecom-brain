# Planning an Ad Concept - Why, What, How

## What it is
A planning discipline that happens before any script or design. Half-formed pitches such as "let's do an us-vs-them ad" fail because they never say what it is about or why it should exist. A short memo forces that thinking up front, kills weak ideas before they cost money, and leaves the writer free to focus on execution when drafting.

Most of the work in a good ad is preparation. Put the bulk of your effort into thinking the concept through; execution then flows. Once you have some training, you will usually have more ideas than budget, and the memo is how you choose between them.

## When to use it
- Before briefing any new concept to a writer, editor, designer or creator.
- When deciding how to structure a test batch (one angle in depth or several angles at once).
- When a test fails and you need to know whether the angle or the execution was the problem.
- If inspiration strikes and you just want to write, write; treat the memo as a sieve, not a rule.

## How (steps)
1. **Answer three questions** in addition to the standard angle, sub-avatar and desire fields:
   - *Why* make this ad? The strategic reason, ideally evidence from existing performance.
   - *What* does it say? The real argument and content, not the name of a format.
   - *How* will it be made? The execution, often modelled on a reference ad.
2. **Pressure-test vague concepts by asking "comparison of what? about what?"** "A comparison static" is not a concept. "A comparison showing our harness stops pulling where others don't, aimed at owners embarrassed on walks" is.
3. **Fill the concept grid:** the concept, a sentence explaining it, each angle with its sub-avatar, variations, format, test structure. Write down anything that is a genuine reason to buy; angles and sub-avatars sit on a spectrum, so do not get stuck perfecting the matrix.
4. **Choose the test structure.**
   - Several angles in one concept: every variation carries its own angle, expressed once.
   - One angle in a concept: every variation expresses that same angle in a new way.
   - Default: first image tests are multi-angle; first video tests are single-angle; every follow-up test, image or video, is single-angle. Multi-angle video is an advanced option.
   - A batch of about three creatives is standard; two or four is fine, more than four is not. Do not mix images and videos in one ad set.
5. **Apply the hook/hold rule for video.**
   - *Multi-angle video*: make each hook speak to its own sub-avatar (POV-style labels work well) and keep the hold generic, relevant to the shared core desire. If the hold is tied to one sub-avatar's scene, it stops making sense behind the other hooks. The goal is direction, not a super-winner.
   - *Single-angle (depth) video*: everything after the hook has to live inside that one person's habits, feelings and daily life. Add a story, statistic or proof aimed at them.
   - *Swap test*: put an unrelated hook on the front. If the body still makes sense, it is not specific enough for a depth test.
6. **Make images congruent with their angle.** Static ads have one frame and no time to explain, so they need a strong headline, clear primary copy and a visual that matches the angle (a doctor for an authority angle, visible steel for a durability angle).
7. **Close the loop after the test.** Before deciding the market doesn't want an angle, ask whether the execution let it down; execution is the usual bottleneck.

## Example
Say you sell blackout blinds.
- *Why*: a static calling out night-shift workers took the most spend last month.
- *What*: sleeping through daylight after a shift, with 100 percent darkness as the reason.
- *How*: a UGC-style video modelled on a high-spending reference.

Multi-angle version: four POV hooks (shift worker, insomniac, light sleeper, dawn riser) in front of one shared body of blinds closing and a dark, calm room. If night workers win, the follow-up is a depth test: three hooks about failing to sleep after a shift, then a hold showing a nurse arriving home at sunrise, closing the blinds and sleeping, with a testimonial from a shift worker. A generic "fix your sleep" hold behind those hooks would waste the depth.

A hypothetical failure: a sports base layer whose hooks all warn about serious injury while the body simply lists features. No behaviours, no experience, no emotion; the swap test would have caught it.

## Common mistakes
- Pitching a format instead of an argument.
- Specific hooks with a hold built for only one of them.
- Generic holds in a depth test.
- More than four creatives per test, or images and videos mixed together.
- Blaming the angle for an execution failure.
