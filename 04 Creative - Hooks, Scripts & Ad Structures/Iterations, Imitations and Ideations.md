# Iterations, Imitations and Ideations

## What it is
Every new ad comes from one of three places:

- **Iteration**: a new version of an ad that already works (new hook text, a different image, a new creator). Cheap and fast.
- **Imitation**: borrowing another brand's creative approach. The weakest source on its own, because it copies surface without the reasons underneath (see [[Extracting Frameworks From Winning Ads Without Copying]] for the safe version).
- **Ideation**: an original concept that grows out of studying your product and buyers. Slower, but the source of fresh, scalable growth and of angles competitors cannot copy.

A useful distinction inside iteration: a small change to a live ad is an *iteration*; a deeper variation that needs new research or new footage is a *concept*. Both count toward a weekly output goal.

## When to use it
- Planning each week's batch and deciding how much goes to new ideas.
- When a new winner appears and you need to milk it without stalling.
- When the account plateaus and iterations stop producing winners.

## How (steps)
**1. Run both tracks at the same time, always.** Iterating winners and testing genuinely new angles are not alternatives you switch between; they run in parallel every week. Set a standing split of creative effort, for example roughly 80 percent iterating proven winners and 20 percent on net-new angles and concepts (a rough starting point, not a benchmark). You can lean the split one way or the other as results change, but neither track ever drops to zero. Before you have a winner, the "iteration" share goes to refining whatever is closest to working. Concentrate on the top-selling product rather than spreading effort across the catalogue.

**2. Climb the iteration ladder.**
1. *Big-swing batch*: several distinct claims across a few angles, so the platform can show you where spend wants to go.
2. *Reasoned iteration*: take whichever concept the platform favoured and change one thing per round. For example, keep the headline and test three images, each showing why the winning attribute matters. Turn superlatives into reasons: "the most hygienic dish rack" becomes "no more scrubbing mould out of the corners".
3. *Fresh research on that winner*: go back to forums and reviews about the specific attribute that won. Research never stops just because something is working.
4. *Research-led video*: build a new video from what that research revealed.
Pay attention when spend flows to a concept you thought was weak. A middling-ROAS concept that attracts spend deserves improvement, not dismissal.

**3. Scale a winning sub-avatar three ways.** Vary the execution (educate, entertain, a "three reasons" structure); go deeper (a narrower, more relatable version of the person); or go broader (drop one specific detail so more people fit).

**4. Cross-pollinate.** Lift phrasing from other long-running winners in your account into new iterations.

**5. Inject ideations regularly** even during a run of iterations, built from research rather than competitor swipes. Giving creators freedom often surfaces authentic angles you would not have scripted.

**Why both tracks, every week.** Iterations compound what already works, but every winner fatigues, and the next one has to be found before that happens. Running only iterations leaves nothing ready when the winner plateaus; running only new swings throws away the cheapest wins you have.
- Keep each iteration distinct enough that the platform treats it as a new ad; near-duplicates waste the iteration share.
- Make the new-angle share genuinely different, well-executed bets.
- Judge execution before blaming an angle: weak execution can make a strong angle look finished. No universal spend number tells you to quit an angle; weigh your conviction against the data (see [[Prioritising and Dropping Angles]]).

## Example
Say you sell a water bottle with a self-cleaning cap (a hypothetical example). Batch one: six statics across three angles (clean, safe, design). The "easy to clean" static takes most of the spend, which surprises the team. Batch two keeps that headline and tests three images, each answering why easy cleaning matters. Research then turns up forum complaints that caps trap a sour smell and that nobody makes a bottle that cleans itself; many people did not know one could exist. Batch three is a video built on that insight: a relatable problem, a casual "someone finally made one that cleans itself" discovery, a sceptical "okay, but why?", a teardown demo, no brush to buy, a gym-bag moment, and "wish I'd known sooner". The next iteration opens on the insight itself. Throughout, a smaller share of each week's production keeps testing unrelated new angles.

## Common mistakes
- Imitating competitors instead of ideating from your own research.
- Treating iteration and new-angle testing as a choice, so one track stops entirely.
- Waiting for the winner to fatigue before starting to look for the next one.
- Iterations so small the platform treats them as duplicates.
- Dismissing the concept the platform picked because you expected another to win.
- Changing several things per round so you cannot tell what helped.
