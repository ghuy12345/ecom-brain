# Hiring and Developing Video Editors

## What it is
The editor assembles the ad, so the editor sets a ceiling on execution quality. It is common to see creative performance rise when nothing changes except the editor, and accounts at high spend almost always have an excellent one. Great research and writing can mask mediocre editing for a while, but weak editing eventually caps results.

When video ads keep losing to statics, the usual cause is editing or copy skill, not the product. A dedicated editor working with a sharp strategist can take an account to very high daily spend. The fastest-scaling small brands typically run on a founder plus an editor, sometimes with a strategist in between.

## When to use it
- You are spending most of your own time editing instead of on strategy and research.
- Video output is too slow or too low quality to test properly.
- You have editors but they only execute briefs and never improve them.

## How (steps)
**1. Know when to hire.** Below roughly $20K to $30K of monthly spend, editing yourself is usually more realistic (see [[Sourcing Footage and Editing Basics]]). By around $150K a month in revenue, hire an editor to free your time for strategy. A useful rule: delegate when you can pay someone well under your own hourly value and they deliver most of your quality.

**2. Find candidates.**
- Headhunt from established agencies, where editors have been trained on ads.
- Use freelance job boards with a clear job description, and look at graduates of editor training programmes.
- Before giving anyone account access, review five to ten ads they have already made.
- For juniors, attitude and coachability matter more than current ability.

**3. Test the real skill: clip selection.** The hardest part of ad editing is choosing the right few seconds from large volumes of footage. Give candidates a paid test: a script and a folder of raw clips, and see which shots they pick for each line, how fast they cut, and whether the opening seconds grab attention.

**4. Set output expectations.** As rough benchmarks:
- A full-time editor on everyday iterations can manage three or four video concepts a day; for full ads, at least one a day, ideally two, which is roughly 30 to 60 ads a month.
- A single video ad typically takes 45 to 60 minutes; a static designer needs 15 to 30 minutes per static. Use these to work out when you need a second editor.
- Pay for strong remote editors varies widely by region and skill; very good ones command several thousand dollars a month, while trainable juniors cost much less.

**5. Brief with a visual direction for every line.** Editors should not be doing strategy from scratch. Give each brief a shot idea per beat, references, and the hook variations to cut.

**6. Train them like you train yourself.** A top editor understands marketing fundamentals, reads what the script is trying to say, joins learnings reviews and proposes better clips and ideas. Build that by:
- Including editors in weekly ad reviews, so they hear why ads won or lost.
- Explaining the reasoning behind feedback, not just the change you want.
- Giving them winning ads to study and asking for their own breakdowns.
An editor nobody has trained holds the whole team back; a trained editor can produce an account's top-spending ad. When an editor starts challenging your visual ideas with better ones, the system is working.

**7. Keep good editors.** Offer growth paths (editor to senior editor to creative strategist), hire juniors beneath strong editors so they can delegate, and tie bonuses to ads that scale. People given room to grow often end up managing other editors. Losing a trained editor resets months of investment.

**8. Structure the team as it grows.** Start with one editor (plus a designer for statics). Add a second editor as volume rises. Once several editors exist, have a senior editor manage them rather than one strategist overseeing everyone.

## Example
Say a supplement brand spending $5K a day has a founder who edits every ad and launches four a week. She posts a role, reviews portfolios from applicants with ad experience, and gives three finalists a paid test: a 40-second script and 30 raw clips. One candidate picks a striking first shot and cuts tightly; she is hired. For the first month she sits in on every learnings review. By month three she is delivering five ads a week and suggesting her own hook visuals; a junior editor is hired under her six months later.

## Common mistakes
- Hiring on showreel polish instead of testing clip selection on real ad footage.
- Treating editors as button-pushers and never sharing results.
- Giving briefs with no visual direction, then blaming the edit.
- No growth path, so trained editors leave.
- Judging an editor by one ad's spend; spend shows execution quality, not profitability.
