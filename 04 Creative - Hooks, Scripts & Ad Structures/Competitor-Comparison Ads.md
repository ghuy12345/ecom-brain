# Competitor-Comparison Ads

## What it is
A comparison ad shows your product beating the alternatives a buyer is already weighing. It fits solution-aware and product-aware viewers in categories they already understand (air fryers, air purifiers, supplements, keyboards): they believe the category works, distrust particular brands, and need a reason to pick yours.

Two formats dominate:
- **The "I tried several" video**: a person who looks like the buyer tests three competing products and only yours works. It confirms objections the audience already has about the competitors and positions you as the opposite.
- **The comparison static**: a simple, on-brand image framed around customer problems. Classic two-column spec tables usually underperform; framing the contrast around buyer problems does better.

## When to use it
- Solution- or product-aware markets with several well-known alternatives.
- When buyers are skeptical researchers who read reviews before buying.
- When your account has plateaued on generic UGC copied from other brands; comparison built from real complaint language can break the plateau.

There is a genuine strategic choice here: position the product as the *alternative* (poking holes in other solutions) or show the *outcome as easier to reach* and skip the comparison entirely. Neither is always right; test both before assuming.

## How (steps)
**Research first.**
1. Name the three competitors your buyers mention most.
2. Compile their pros, cons, use cases and unmet expectations from marketplace reviews (a review-scraping tool speeds this up), plus what your own customers write in reviews and under your ads.
3. Mine comments on videos your audience watches, especially "reasons not to buy X" videos.
4. List the top two or three objections to the category, condense your mechanism to one or two lines, and rank the persona's three most wanted benefits.
5. Turn each competitor con into your advantage: if their weak point is poor suction, show your suction; if their strength is ease of use, show yours is easier.

**Write the video script.**
1. *Hook*: "Three [category], one month, only one [specific outcome]." Naming the persona, for example by age, sharpens it.
2. *Two knockdowns*: name each competitor's approach, its price, and how it failed or created a new problem, using the persona's real complaints rather than generic ones. Keep claims factual and fair; you can describe a category of product without naming a brand.
3. *The winner and why*: the last option came out on top; one or two simple sentences of mechanism that sound new, with objection-defusing facts woven in.
4. *Three escalating benefits*: start with an emotional one, raise the stakes with the second, and save the biggest for last.
5. *Price, guarantee, call to action*: state the price and back it with a guarantee to show confidence, then end on the headline benefit. Premium brands lead with quality or the guarantee instead of price.

One script can be re-shot as a podcast clip, a voiceover on B-roll, an animation, a handwritten note or a green-screen piece, so get several formats out of it.

**Build and iterate the static.**
1. Record the awareness level, the target desire and your reasoning in a sheet before anything goes live.
2. Start from a proven simple template and swap text and imagery.
3. Run three versions of each concept, since a good idea can lose to a bad headline alone. Vary, in order: imagery, headline, the benefit called out, then persona. For video: first three seconds, opening text, the body, then persona.
4. Log every result with your reason for why it won or lost, so you never retest the same idea blindly.
5. Wait at least a week of spend before calling a winner, then iterate small first (see [[Iterating a Winning Ad in Order]]).

Favour fewer, better-researched comparisons over blasting dozens of creatives a day; a higher win rate does more than raw volume.

## Example
Say you sell an air purifier (a hypothetical example). Awareness: product aware. Desire: clean air at home. Three statics, identical except one benefit tag: "No more constant filter changes", "Not cheap, flimsy plastic", and a safety certification. The filter-changes version might win clearly if "filter changes", "costs a fortune to replace" and "never sure when it's due" were the exact phrases buyers used across reviews and comments. The next round kept that tag and tested three images.

## Common mistakes
- Feature-table comparisons nobody reads.
- Generic knockdowns ("other brands don't work") instead of the persona's real complaints.
- Unfair or unverifiable claims about named competitors.
- Borrowing another brand's comparison look without first mapping your own desire and awareness level.
- Testing one version, losing, and abandoning the concept when only the headline was wrong.
