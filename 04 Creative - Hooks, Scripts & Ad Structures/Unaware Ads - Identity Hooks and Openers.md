# Unaware Ads - Identity Hooks and Openers

## What it is
An unaware ad targets people who either haven't noticed they have a problem or haven't heard that a new route to something they want exists. In most markets this is the biggest slice of potential buyers. As an illustrative rough estimate (it will vary by market, not a benchmark), a market splits roughly 3% ready to buy, 7% open to buying, 30% not thinking about buying, 30% who think they are not interested and 30% with zero intent. The large middle of about 60% is the unaware pool. That middle is why a working unaware ad is often the most scalable ad in an account. It holds up whatever the market's sophistication, and it gets around timing: even people with the problem may not feel it on the day they see your ad.

Unaware is not the same as "for everyone". You are still aiming at a particular identity, just before it has put a name to its problem.

## When to use it
- When problem- and product-aware ads have plateaued and you need a new pool of buyers.
- When the product solves something people have not articulated, or offers a new way to a familiar desire.
- **Not** when the market simply lives with the problem. People who know they have it but tolerate it are problem aware; agitate the pain or show a new solution instead of educating from zero.

## How (steps)
1. **Make the hook about identity, not product.** Use the prospect's own state of mind, a feeling they have but have not put into words. Price, brand name and claims are irrelevant this early. All the hook has to do is earn the next line.
2. **Never name the problem in the headline.** Mention exhaustion, dry skin or back pain and you are talking to someone who already knows about it, which makes the ad problem aware.
3. **Choose one of the four openers.**
   - *Hidden fear*: a shared fear nobody says out loud, told through a story, then bridged to the solution ("If your cat is older than seven, you'll want to hear this").
   - *Hidden desire*: an unspoken wish, often framed as "imagine if", narrowed to identity ("Three clues your straight hair is secretly wavy" reaches people who see no problem at all).
   - *Did you know*: a surprising fact, statistic or story that leads to a problem ("What's actually the first ingredient in most chocolate bars?").
   - *Debate*: an open question that invites people to take sides, poll-style ("Who has the better life, cats or dogs?"). The least proven of the four; start with the first three.
4. **Camouflage the ad as content.** It should look like a story, useful information or entertainment. Camouflage is a delivery style, not an awareness level: you can camouflage a product-aware ad too. What matters is what the camouflage wraps around.
5. **Walk every awareness level within the same ad:** open on identity (unaware), bring a desire or a looming problem to the surface (problem aware), lay out the kinds of fixes (solution aware), show why yours suits them best (product aware), and finish with an offer that makes buying on impulse simple (most aware). See [[Scriptwriting as a Walk Through Awareness Stages]].
6. **Borrow classic unaware headline patterns.** *Breakthrough Advertising* by Eugene Schwartz lists approaches such as: voice a dream nobody admits to, tap a fear nobody mentions, start from a universally accepted image and lead to an unacceptable problem, project a triumph the reader identifies with, or show the result of a problem so you never confront it head-on. All begin broad and avoid selling.
7. **Check AI drafts by hand.** Language models repeatedly label problem-aware headlines as unaware. If a draft names the problem, reject it and ask for replacements that lead with curiosity instead.

## Example
Say you sell an air purifier. A problem-aware headline: "The hidden reason you wake up exhausted." It names the problem, so it is not unaware. Unaware alternatives: "What actually happens in your bedroom while you sleep" or "Here's what you're breathing in tonight." Anyone can watch either. The script then reveals what is in bedroom air (a desire for better sleep surfaces), shows the ways people try to fix it, positions the purifier as the one that works overnight, and closes on a first-order offer.

## Common mistakes
- Introducing the product in the first seconds, which turns the ad into a product-aware ad aimed at people who are not ready.
- Naming the problem in the hook.
- Treating any entertaining camouflage as "unaware" without checking what it actually says.
- Writing education-heavy unaware ads for a market that already knows and tolerates the problem.
- Judging unaware openers on early hook rate alone; they win on scale, so give them enough spend to show it.
