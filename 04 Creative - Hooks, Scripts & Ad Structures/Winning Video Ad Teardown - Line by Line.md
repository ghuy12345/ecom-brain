# Winning Video Ad Teardown - Line by Line

## What it is
A deep read of a single proven video ad, one clause at a time, to understand what each line does inside the viewer's head. It goes further than the annotation in [[Studying Winning Ads You Did Not Make]]: the aim is to extract strategies you can reuse, never the lines themselves.

Change the question you ask. Instead of "why is this ad working?", ask "what is the viewer feeling here?" and "what is going through their mind at this moment?" Feelings can be recreated in countless ways; copied lines cannot.

## When to use it
- When your ads get clicks and carts but few orders, to see how winners create urgency.
- As a team exercise: several people tear down the same ad separately, then compare.

## How (steps)
1. **Pick a genuine winner.** Filter an ad-spy tool to roughly the last three to six months and sort by shares. For your own ads, add frequency to the usual metrics: a small-spend ad reaching each person around three times is mostly retargeting.
2. **Watch as a customer, then as a marketer.** First just feel it. Then go back and read the script clause by clause, leaving clips, voiceover and on-screen text for later.
3. **Give the opening seven seconds most of your attention.** A hook has two jobs, stopping and retaining; a stunt that stops people but loses them is a failure.
4. **For every clause, note what it does**: opens or closes a loop, introduces the mechanism, borrows authority, validates a pain, blames the category, raises stakes, handles an objection, closes.
5. **Ladder up with "why does that matter?"** until you reach the psychology. Example: a bold number stops the scroll because it promises that something unwanted won't happen.
6. **Validate pain points.** Search forums for the complaint the ad voices. If real people say it, the line is grounded.
7. **Find the gap.** Look for a loop that never closes or a claim never proven, and propose an iteration that fills it. Mark any invented story as invented.
8. **Lift to principle, then apply.** Write the learning abstractly ("blame the category's material, not a competitor"), then bring it down onto your product. Cross-niche steals work best. You can give written learnings and your product facts to an AI assistant to draft applications.
9. **Revisit.** Look again two days later; you will notice things you missed. Record failed transplants too.

## Example
Say the ad sells a dental gel for dogs. The beats and what each does:

1. **Juxtaposition hook that names the mechanism**: "Clean your dog's teeth in 30 seconds, no brush." Clean teeth and fast don't usually go together, so a scroll-stopping claim and an unscratched itch appear at once. Curiosity, not confusion: the viewer understands the claim but not how.
2. **Odd visual, then payoff**: an unexpected close-up, then the gel applied and a relaxed dog, which pre-empts the "my dog won't sit still" objection.
3. **A fact instead of an opinion**: rather than "good owners brush daily" (easy to reject), the ad asks whether you are among the large majority of owners who don't clean teeth daily, with a simple chart. Facts feel authoritative and create guilt without an argument. (Any statistic you use must be real and verified.) Speaking to the majority who don't also targets the bigger audience.
4. **Widen the gap**: the word "daily" sets a standard the viewer had never considered.
5. **Raise stakes**: from yellow teeth to long-term health, phrased as "could be" rather than "is", which protects belief and keeps it compliant.
6. **Absolve the viewer**: brushing is difficult, so the fault lies with the old method rather than the owner.
7. **Urgency from signs they've already noticed**: worse breath, yellowing. Familiar signs create urgency far better than abstract threats, which is how winners turn add-to-carts into purchases.
8. **The turn**: "It doesn't have to be this way", then the 30-second routine, paying off the hook's promise.
9. **Brand appears only now**, after problem and solution; the viewer moves from problem to solution to product to brand.
10. **Doubts answered through the demo**: foolproof, no toothbrush (the dog running from a brush), reaches back teeth. Answers to common questions from ad comments go at the end.
11. **Restate the main benefit** (quick, effortless) in several forms, showing it every time.

Other winners add: a trusted expert rejecting the old product (implying yours is better without saying so) and a soft, sensory call to action.

The gap: if an ad teases "discovered by accident" and never explains it, that unexplained story is your iteration.

## Common mistakes
- Copying lines instead of extracting the feeling and principle.
- Analysing visuals before understanding the script.
- Stating certainty ("you are harming your dog") where "could be" is truer and safer.
- Excusing a weak result with "we can't afford production".
