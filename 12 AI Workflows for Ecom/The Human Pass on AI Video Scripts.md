# The Human Pass on AI Video Scripts

## What it is
The step between an AI draft and a brief you can hand to an editor, creator or stakeholder. The workflow is a sandwich: AI draft, then your manual rewrite with research open, then a second AI pass to critique and polish. The model holds the information but lacks the brand's real voice, the buyer's exact phrasing and dependable facts. Your pass supplies those.

## When to use it
Every time an AI-written script is about to leave your hands. It matters most when you cannot run large volumes of ads and each brief has to count. Aim for one excellent brief a day rather than five rushed ones.

## How (steps)
1. **Set up to focus.** Phone out of reach, a dedicated desk, nothing else to do for about 30 minutes. Boredom usually pushes you into writing within ten minutes. Budget roughly a quick first pass plus a longer second pass with research open.
2. **Go through it one line at a time.** Put three questions to every line: Does it lump the brand in with competitors? Is it accurate? Which word would a buyer really use here?
3. **Swap in customer vocabulary.** Do not coin terms the market does not use. Have the model search forum posts and reviews for the phrases buyers actually use, then apply a dual register: the plain emotional phrase in the hook, the clinical term for credibility later. If buyers call low-calorie eating "torture", use that, not "difficult".
4. **Add specific scenes.** Pull concrete moments from forum research (avoiding the pantry after dinner, craving "something crunchy"). Specific detail proves you understand the pain; keep it broad enough to fit most of the avatar.
5. **Fact-check everything.** Models fill gaps and get things wrong (calling an enzyme a hormone, say). Check words like "rare" and "clinically proven", and every percentage, against the product document. Watch for figures drifting between drafts.
6. **Avoid categorising your product.** List what the audience already tried in generic terms ("supplements, suppressants, natural alternatives"), then split the problem: others only address one half, you address the half they miss.
7. **Tune the hook.** Lower-valence hooks (for example, signs that your body may be sabotaging your diet) hold attention because people want to check nothing is wrong with them. Starting partway through a thought can fold the hook and bridge together.
8. **Make demographic choices deliberately.** An implied age ("after 40") trades breadth for relevance; use it only when your customer data supports it and the mechanism really applies to that group.
9. **Add direction for the reader.** Mark pauses, emphasis and reactions. For scripts in a founder's voice, rewrite until it sounds like them and the story stays accurate; insert real customer moments you already hold, such as emails or stories.
10. **Check readability.** Run the script through a readability tool; you can knowingly keep a flagged phrase if it builds trust.
11. **Run a final AI critique** against a checklist, then add visuals beside each beat. Plenty of beats can use simple motion graphics instead of filmed footage.
12. **Bank by-products.** Unused hooks and mechanisms become new concepts in your roadmap.

## Example
Say a gut-health powder script from the model is solid but generic. In the human pass, "difficult" becomes "exhausting" because that is what buyers wrote; "you've tried everything" becomes a list of what this avatar actually tried; and the positioning shifts from "works better" to "handles the part of the problem rivals skip: support, don't just mask". A percentage in the proof line can drift between drafts; check it against the product document and fix it. For a hypothetical appetite supplement, a finished proof line might cite a 12-week study of 90 women reporting a 56% rise in GLP-1 and, on average, 24% fewer calories eaten in the first seven days, word for word as the product document states it. The pass also produces two new angles for the roadmap.

## Common mistakes
- Sending a draft to a stakeholder or editor before your own pass.
- Scripting a real founder to say things they never would.
- Coining jargon the market does not use.
- Trusting the model's science and statistics.
- Writing at a university reading level.
- Mistaking a stakeholder's approval for proof the ad will perform.
- Using strong copy as an excuse for weak execution; it can carry average footage only when it is very strong.
