# Turning Viral Videos Into Script Templates

## What it is
Short-form videos that move product without paid support frequently perform as ads as well. The valuable part is not the footage or the exact words but the structure: the order of beats, the relatable setup, the escalating tension, the reveal. This workflow uses AI to break a viral video down into that structure, then rewrites it as an original script for your product that a creator can film.

You borrow the skeleton, never the content. Do not download and re-upload another creator's video, splice their footage into your ads, or copy their script word for word.

## When to use it
- When you need fresh script ideas for creators and your own winners are running dry.
- When you want proof that a structure holds attention before investing in production.
- When your hooks get views but viewers drop off quickly.

## How (steps)
1. **Find a viral product or demonstration video** using a database of trending videos or the platform itself. Look at views, likes and comment counts, and read the comments to see how the audience actually reacted.
2. **Get a breakdown, not just a transcript.** Ask a multimodal model for a beat-by-beat analysis: each line, why it works, how relatable it is, the problem it raises and how urgency escalates, plus notes on why the whole video resonates and how it could be better.
3. **Feed the breakdown to your brand project.** Ask it to write a script for your product that follows the same structure, in your customer's language, with your mechanism and proof. Giving it only the raw transcript produces a weaker rewrite.
4. **Revise the hooks for reasons to keep watching.** Judge each hook on one question: does the viewer now need an answer? Request three hooks, each with alternatives and shot direction, then build each one out into a hold.
5. **Write visual direction per hook**, for example slow turn-over shots and close-ups on hands for a reveal, or fast cuts and tight shots of how the piece comes apart for a take-apart hook.
6. **Save working structures** into a swipe file of high-performing short-form ads.
7. **Compare your remake with the original** side by side once filmed. Check setting, hook strength and effort level; remakes usually fail because they are lazier than the original.

## Example
Say an energy-drink brand finds a viral video about the afternoon crash. The breakdown shows the beats: a relatable morning routine, an unhealthy coffee-and-sugar loop, a 2pm crash, then the cycle repeating to raise urgency. The brand's project rewrites that skeleton around its slow-release formula, with a creator in their own kitchen and office.

For a jewellery brand with a layered necklace, revised hooks shift from describing the product to creating a question: a secret hidden inside the piece paired with its price tag, a take-apart hook about why people are pulling it apart, and an emotional reveal of a hidden message. Each comes with shot direction, and stacking a price with a secret adds a stakes cue.

## Common mistakes
- Re-uploading or ripping someone else's video instead of filming your own version.
- Copying lines verbatim rather than the structure.
- Handing the rewriting prompt a bare transcript with no breakdown.
- Filming the remake with less care than the source: a softer hook, a duller setting, a flatter setup.
- Using an old version of your hook prompt after outputs have shown gaps; revise prompts as you learn.
- Hitting usage caps halfway through; split the work into smaller sessions.
