# AI Needs Human Judgment

## What it is
Language models can now research, draft hooks, write scripts and produce briefs in minutes. That does not make creative strategy automatic. Because every competitor has access to the same models, AI output becomes the new average: it lifts the floor of quality across the market but does not lift your ceiling. What separates operators is the ability to tell good from bad, explain why, and steer the tool with precise instructions.

Think of three kinds of people: those who know nothing about AI, AI specialists with no marketing skill to aim it at, and ecommerce operators who are competent at both. The third group captures most of the upside, for as long as that combination stays rare.

## When to use it
Use this as the mindset behind every other note in this section. Revisit it whenever you are tempted to buy a tool that promises "winning ads on autopilot", copy a viral prompt pack, or ship an AI draft without reading it as a customer would.

## How (steps)
1. **Learn the principle before the prompt.** Understand awareness levels, mass desire, mechanisms and why copy moves people. A prompt you do not understand cannot be improved, and outputs you cannot judge cannot be directed. See [[Getting Better at Copywriting]].
2. **Study any prompt you borrow for how it is built.** Ask what each instruction is doing, what context is missing and how you would add examples. Prompts and models change within weeks; the skill of building prompts lasts.
3. **Give precise direction, not vague requests.** Competent direction sounds like "raise the stakes in line two" or "this bridge has no pull to keep someone watching; rebuild it using these examples", not "make it better".
4. **Split the work realistically.** Models can carry a large share of ideation, brief writing, concept development and research gathering with human quality control. Final editing, clip selection and assembly are still hard to hand over. Treat any estimate of the share as perishable.
5. **Keep strategy with a human.** Choosing the angle, the mechanism, the avatar and the editorial direction is your job. AI is strong at spotting patterns, filling gaps and gathering raw material.
6. **Delegate content generation.** If you are the owner or media buyer, spend your time on strategy and briefs. Generating AI images and video belongs with your editors or designers, since that material is what they use to carry out your briefs.
7. **Be sceptical of tools that promise winners.** If one tool really produced winning ads for everyone, everyone would have the same ads and none would win. Validate any tool in your own account before you rely on it.

## Example
Say a skincare brand gives an LLM its product page and asks for ten hooks. The output is clean, on-brand and almost identical to what three competitors already run, because the model predicts the most likely words. An operator who knows the market rejects eight, notices that one hook hints at a customer-invented proxy for results, and tells the model exactly why that line has energy and to produce five more built on the same tension. The model did the typing; the judgment produced the usable ad.

## Common mistakes
- Pasting in prompts you never read, then blaming the model for generic output.
- Treating one tool's current behaviour as permanent and rebuilding your workflow every time a new model appears.
- Confusing content writing (words that fill an ad slot) with copywriting (words that set or reinforce how the reader sees you and change what they do). AI is very good at the first by default.
- Relying only on AI and templates, which guarantees you sound like everyone else and pushes you toward competing on discounts.
- Spending strategic hours mastering image and video generation tricks that belong with someone on your team.
- Treating a short-term, copy-paste skill set (clone a template, feed a tool, generate a face) as durable. It fades as soon as the trend moves.
