# Cash Flow Forecasting

## What it is
Cash flow forecasting is predicting when money will arrive and when it must leave, so growth never outruns the cash needed to fund it. A brand can show a healthy profit and still be unable to pay for its next purchase order, because the profit is sitting on shelves as inventory. Growth is capped by the cash you have to buy the next batch of stock while still serving today's demand.

## When to use it
- Before committing to a big jump in ad spend.
- When planning purchase orders, especially with long lead times or full upfront payment.
- When slow-moving stock starts piling up.
- Before peak season, when spend and inventory needs both spike (see [[Inventory Planning for Q4-Heavy Brands]]).

## How (steps)
1. **Forecast returning-customer revenue from cohorts.** Pull two to three years of monthly acquisition cohorts. Apply each cohort's historical repeat rate by months since acquisition, multiply by returning-customer AOV, and sum across cohorts. Then layer a seasonality index (a multi-year average) and known events such as launches or sales. Cohort math alone tends to land within about 10%; adding seasonality and events tightens it a lot.
2. **Forecast new-customer revenue from spend efficiency.** Plot a year of daily ad spend against acquisition efficiency (new-customer revenue per ad dollar). Remove outliers such as sale days and days below your normal minimum spend. Fit a simple linear or log curve, then back-solve the spend needed to hit your target. If the target is out of reach on the curve, name the input that would change it: a new channel, a launch, a step-change in creative, or an event.
3. **Grade every SKU.** A = fast movers, B = medium, C = slow, D = not moving. Protect A items from stock-outs; turn C and D items back into cash.
4. **Break the inventory death spiral early.** The pattern: a product stalls, its stock ages into C or D, cash gets locked up, you cannot fund marketing or new stock, and growth stalls. Run slow stock through its own funnel at breakeven or a small loss, using separate pages or other markets so the main brand's price perception is protected. Sometimes the right call is a temporary rise in marketing spend just to clear it.
5. **Map cash timing, not just profit.** Note when ad bills, supplier deposits and balances fall due against when revenue lands. The [[Cash Conversion Cycle]] note covers the full timing model.
6. **Use volume as leverage with suppliers.** As you grow, share a detailed forecast with your supplier and ask what level of risk they are comfortable with, so they define the constraint. Another option is to accept paying a little more per unit if the supplier stretches terms (for example from 60 to 90 days); in effect, margin is what you spend to gain time. Details are in [[Supplier Payment Terms and Incoterms]].
7. **Line up funding sources before you need them.** Options include partial-deposit supplier terms, shorter lead times (often at a higher unit cost), post-pay ad billing and credit lines (see [[Ad Account Billing and Credit Float]]), and inventory financing.

*General information, not financial advice:* credit lines, cards and inventory financing are debt. Interest compresses margin, and borrowed money used to fund ad spend or stock has to be repaid whether or not the campaign or the product works. Only borrow against cash you have modelled with real cohort data, and get advice from an accountant or finance professional for your situation.

## Example
Say a supplement brand has a 90-day production lead time and its factory wants full payment upfront. It wants to triple ad spend this year. Its forecast shows revenue rising, but the next three purchase orders must be paid before most of the new customers' revenue arrives, so the brand would stock out within months. Its options: negotiate a deposit plus balance on shipment, pay a slightly higher unit price for longer terms, or grow spend more slowly. Tripling with borrowed money instead could push revenue up while interest and weaker ad efficiency leave net profit close to zero.

Meanwhile its forecast flags two D-grade flavours holding a large share of inventory value. Moving them through a separate bundle page at breakeven frees cash for the A-grade bestseller.

## Common mistakes
- Treating profit as cash and scaling spend without a cash timeline.
- Forecasting total revenue as one line instead of new and returning separately.
- Extrapolating the current run rate into peak season and over-ordering.
- Letting slow stock sit because selling it at breakeven feels like a loss.
- Using financing to cover operational problems instead of fixing them.
- Assuming a supplier will never offer better terms without being asked.
