# Ad Account Billing and Credit Float

## What it is
Float is the time between spending money on ads and that money actually leaving your bank account. How you pay your ad platform decides how much float you get. With enough float, a cohort can be negative on its first order and first repeat purchases, turn positive on a later one, and still be in the black before the bill is due. Float is a working-capital tool, and it is also debt you pay for.

## When to use it
- You have proven, tracked cohort economics and want spend to grow faster than your cash balance alone allows.
- Your card limit or billing threshold is physically capping daily spend.
- You are moving from a small account toward large monthly budgets.

It is the wrong tool if you do not yet track cohort payback and company cash flow carefully.

## How (steps)
1. **Know the three common setups, from least to most float.**
   - *Pay each billing threshold from a bank or debit account.* The platform charges you every time spend crosses a small threshold. No float and no debt, but the worst option for working capital.
   - *Pay the same thresholds with a credit card.* This gives roughly a statement cycle of float. Card points are a perk for the owner, not part of the business economics.
   - *Move to invoice (monthly) billing.* Larger advertisers commonly use it. Terms are typically around 30 days plus a short grace period; plan for about six weeks and pay a little early. Invoices usually must be paid by bank transfer, so some businesses route them through a bill-payment service that accepts cards, then pay that card on long interest-free terms. Stacked, this can stretch cash-out to roughly three months or more after the spend.
2. **Price the float.** Card-funded bill-pay services charge a fee, often in the 2.5-3% range. Subtract any rewards or statement credits you reliably receive, then annualise over the days of float you actually get. Do the arithmetic yourself; do not assume rewards cancel the fee. When choosing a card for float, the length of the interest-free period usually matters more than the rewards rate, and rewards multipliers are often capped at a spend level.
3. **Remove hard caps on spend.** If a card limit is stopping a profitable campaign, a high-limit business card or a post-pay business ad account removes the ceiling. Failed payments mid-scale can get an account restricted, so keep a backup payment method on file (see [[Ad Account Protection and Payment Hygiene]]).
4. **Add a line of credit only as a planned buffer,** sized to the cash trough your cohort model predicts, not as an open tap.
5. **Track it every month.** Reconcile float balances against company cash and cohort payback; know exactly when each bill lands (see [[Cash Conversion Cycle]]).
6. **Re-check terms regularly.** Billing thresholds, grace periods, card terms and platform policies change. Verify the current terms before you build a plan around them.

*General information, not financial advice:* every float setup beyond paying from your bank is borrowing. You pay for the time through fees or interest, the balance is due whether or not the ads worked, and a bad month can leave you with ad debt and unsold stock at the same time. Only use credit to fund ad spend when you are confident, from real cohort data, that the cash will be there when the bill arrives, and get advice from an accountant or finance professional.

## Example
Say an apparel brand spends heavily on ads and its cohorts turn cash-positive around day 75. Paying thresholds from its bank account means every dollar leaves on the day it is spent, so it would need to fund roughly two and a half months of acquisition upfront. On invoice billing funded through a long-term charge card, cash leaves around day 100. If the funding fee is about 2.5% and the brand earns about 1% back in rewards, the net cost is roughly 1.5% for about 100 days, which works out to around 5-6% a year in simple terms. The brand decides that is worth paying only because it tracks cohort payback weekly and keeps a cash reserve for a bad month.

## Common mistakes
- Using float without cohort and cash-flow tracking.
- Assuming rewards always offset the funding fee.
- Treating a billing setup or card term as permanent.
- Letting a card limit silently cap a profitable campaign instead of fixing billing.
- Treating float-funded first orders as truly profitable.
- Using credit to cover a weak offer or operational problems.
