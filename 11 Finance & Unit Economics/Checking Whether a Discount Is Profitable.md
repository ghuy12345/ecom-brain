# Checking Whether a Discount Is Profitable

## What it is
A discount check is the math that tells you whether an offer will make you more money or just more revenue. Offers are powerful: they can double ROAS and lift conversion rate quickly. That is exactly why they are addictive. Every discount lowers gross margin, which raises your breakeven ROAS, so a campaign can post higher revenue and higher ROAS while earning less profit than before.

Running offers is fine for most brands, especially first brands without a truly unique product. The rule is simply never to launch one without doing the numbers first.

## When to use it
- Before any new offer, including stacked or conditional ones (a headline discount plus a second-unit deal).
- Before Black Friday, Cyber Monday and other sale periods.
- When a promo looks like it is "working" on revenue and you are about to scale it.
- When deciding how far to lower targets to buy customers during peak season.

## How (steps)
1. **Recalculate gross margin at the discounted price.** Use the price the customer actually pays. Stacked discounts multiply rather than add: 10% off and then 20% off is 28% off, not 30%.
2. **Recalculate breakeven ROAS and target CPA** from the new margin (see [[Breakeven ROAS and KPI Tiers]]), then set a new scaling target.
3. **Compare scenarios on profit.** Gross profit after ads = revenue x margin - ad spend. Model the evergreen case and the offer case side by side, including realistic spend and ROAS for each.
4. **Decide what lift the offer must deliver.** If the offer only matches your old ROAS, it loses. It has to lift ROAS or conversion enough to more than cover the margin you gave away.
5. **Prefer offers that protect margin.** Bundles raise AOV while shipping cost rises far less than units; a low-cost gift with purchase can add a lot of perceived value; free or express shipping can replace a discount in December; deep discounts can be reserved for slow-moving stock. See [[Offers Beyond Discounting]].
6. **Judge the cohort, not just the day.** Track customers acquired on each discount code and compare their 90- and 180-day value with full-price buyers (see [[Cohort LTV Modelling]]).
7. **Verify against the bank.** Re-run the numbers weekly and check them against actual profit, not only the ad tracker.

## Example
Say a kitchenware brand sells at a 60% gross margin, so breakeven is about 1.67 (hypothetical numbers). It spends $50,000 a month at 2.5x: $125,000 revenue, $75,000 gross profit, $25,000 after ads.

It launches 20% off, which cuts margin to 50% and lifts breakeven to 2.0. Revenue jumps and the team scales spend to $75,000, still at 2.5x. Revenue is now $187,500, but gross profit is $93,750 and only about $18,750 remains after ads. Revenue rose 50%; profit fell by a quarter.

The reverse case: if the offer lifts ROAS to 3.2 at the original $50,000, revenue is $160,000, gross profit $80,000 and profit after ads $30,000. Now the offer wins. Same discount, opposite verdict, decided entirely by the arithmetic.

**Q4 trade-off.** Lowering profit targets in the fourth quarter to buy revenue and list size has a hidden cost: many holiday buyers are purchasing gifts and come back less often, so each customer is worth less than in a normal month. Discount-acquired cohorts in general tend to be more price-sensitive and less likely to return at full price, even while paid metrics look great during the sale. Balance acquisition with healthy margin; a smaller day at good gross profit beats a huge day with thin profit.

**Subscriptions are the exception that proves the rule.** A very deep first-order discount can make sense when the first order is deliberately an acquisition cost and cohort data proves strong repeat revenue. Without that proof, it is just a loss.

## Common mistakes
- Scaling a discounted offer at the old ROAS target.
- Judging success by revenue growth alone.
- Adding stacked discounts as if they were additive.
- Assessing a discount only on first-order profit and never checking the cohort.
- Validating offers by email only; existing customers shop differently from new ones.
- Training customers to wait for sales by discounting all year, which leaves nothing stronger for peak season.
