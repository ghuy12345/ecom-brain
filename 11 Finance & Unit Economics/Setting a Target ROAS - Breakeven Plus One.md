# Setting a Target ROAS - Breakeven Plus One

## What it is
A target ROAS is the return at which you are comfortable raising budgets. The simple default: **target ROAS = breakeven ROAS + 1**. If breakeven is 1.5, aim for 2.5; if 1.6, aim for 2.6. At that level you are profitable on first-purchase economics at any spend.

Treat the target as a reference point rather than a rule. What you are really maximising is **absolute profit**, and a lower ROAS on a bigger budget often produces more of it than a high ROAS on a small one.

## When to use it
- As soon as you know your breakeven (see [[Breakeven ROAS and KPI Tiers]]).
- When deciding whether to scale up, hold or scale down a campaign.
- When a winner's ROAS drops as you push more budget into it.
- After any price or offer change, when the old target no longer applies.

## How (steps)
1. **Get breakeven right first.** Use gross margin only, overestimate costs rather than underestimate them, and round up.
2. **Add one.** That is your starting target. Remember this method looks only at first-purchase economics and ignores fixed overheads, so if your overheads are heavy you may need more headroom.
3. **Model spend scenarios by profit, not ROAS.** Profit = spend x (ROAS x margin - 1). Compare several spend and ROAS combinations before you commit budget.
4. **Read results over the right window.** Judge over at least three days, and closer to seven at low budgets (around $100 a day). One sale is not data; something like 50 purchases in a week is enough signal to raise budget with confidence. Expect ROAS to dip when you scale, because delivery moves to colder audiences.
5. **Ignore single-day noise.** A day at 0.4 after a day at 5.9 means nothing on its own. Act when weak performance persists for about three days, and scale down if you are sitting near breakeven and losing money.
6. **Scale for a reason.** Prefer to scale when something real changed (a new winning ad, offer or page), not because one day was good. If you sit consistently far above target, that is a signal to spend more.
7. **If you are stuck at breakeven, make better ads.** Stronger creative is usually what moves a 1.5 to a 2.

## Example
Say your margin is 65%, so breakeven is about 1.54, rounded to 1.6, and your target is 2.6 (made-up figures, per month):

| Ad spend per month | Resulting ROAS | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| $2,000 | 2.6 | ~$1,380 |
| $3,000 | 2.3 | ~$1,485 |
| $4,000 | 2.1 | ~$1,460 |
| $5,000 | 1.8 | ~$850 |

The best month is $3,000 at 2.3, below target ROAS but above target profit. Push to $5,000 and revenue grows while profit falls. The same arithmetic applies when a winning ad's ROAS sags as you scale it: ask whether the extra spend made you more money in total.

**Margin first, closer to breakeven later.**
- *Default: margin-first.* Start with breakeven plus one, judged on first-purchase profit, and be profitable, especially while you are small. It keeps every month self-funding while cash is tight and LTV data is thin.
- *Later: closer to breakeven, if your system supports it.* Once cohort data proves customers come back and pay you more within a window your cash can carry, you can run nearer breakeven to grow the customer base, setting targets from LTV-based CPA limits rather than a fixed ROAS (see [[LTGP to CAC Ratio and the CAC Death Spiral]]).
- *Subscription businesses* with proven retention can run at breakeven, or even at a loss, on the first order, because the recurring orders carry the profit.
Whichever applies, base the decision on your own financials (margins, cash position, retention and overheads), which vary from business to business, not on someone else's target.

**Sale periods are different.** During major promotions, budgets can rise much faster than in evergreen periods (see [[Intraday Budget Scaling for Sale Periods]]), but the target itself must be recalculated for the discounted margin first.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the target as a floor and refusing profitable lower-ROAS spend.
- Scaling past the point where lower ROAS outweighs the extra spend.
- Folding salaries, editors and software into product margin.
- Reacting to one day of data in either direction.
- Applying breakeven plus one blindly at extremes; with a very low or very high breakeven the rule fits poorly.
- Keeping the old target after prices or offers change.
- Running near breakeven while small, before your cohort and cash data show the business can carry it.
- Chasing the lowest possible CPA, which you can always get by spending less.
