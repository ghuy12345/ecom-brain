# Developing a Custom Hardware Product

## What it is
The end-to-end process for a fully custom physical product such as a gadget or device. Be precise about what "custom" means. Modifying an existing item, for instance changing its colour, adding a grip or designing your own box, costs relatively little. Building something with its own moulds is a major commitment, often a five- or six-figure budget and most of a year. Make sure suppliers know which of the two you are asking for.

## When to use it
- You have a proven product and want a defensible, improved version.
- Customer complaints point to a design fix no existing product offers.
- You are deciding whether a custom build is worth the time and money at all.

## How (steps)
1. **Concept.** Note what the product is for, its features and its look, then sketch it; putting it on paper reveals issues you would otherwise overlook.
2. **Design and supplier vetting side by side.** Work out shape, size and how it feels in the hand, using either an independent designer (costly) or the factory's in-house team, while you vet manufacturers. See [[Finding and Vetting Manufacturers]].
3. **Estimate the project.** Work out the development timeline, the total cost, and a firm target cost for every unit produced.
4. **Structural design.** 3D files showing where screws and adhesive go, along with finishes, materials and where the logo sits.
5. **Prototype in order.** Start with a 3D print, which is fast and cheap and tells you about size, weight and feel. Then move to a CNC-machined prototype in the real material so you can test function. Check every round against your concept list; needing two or three rounds is normal.
6. **Tooling, the go or no-go gate.** At this point you will know how good the samples are, what moulds will cost and what each unit will cost. After the moulds are made, the design is effectively frozen.
7. **Packaging.** Begin once the design is frozen, because carton die lines depend on exact dimensions. The weeks spent making moulds are the natural time to design and sample packaging.
8. **Trial run from final tooling.** Use these units to write inspection procedures (incoming parts, during assembly, before packing, and packaging checks), to complete certification on finished units, and to make samples for creators and content before full production begins.
9. **Mass production.** Know how long raw materials take, forecast the first order and how fast it will sell, and set the stock level that triggers each reorder.
10. **Post-production QC.** Agree the inspection procedure, visit the line where you can, and spot-check personally or through someone nearby. See [[Building a Quality Control System]].
11. **Reduce cost at scale.** Revisit slow-to-source materials, ask for volume pricing, put deposits on long-lead parts, arrange staggered deliveries, and share your growth plans so the factory can prepare.

## Budgets and timelines
- **Budget:** expect to spend somewhere around $50,000 to $100,000 ahead of production starting. Each prototype may cost up to about a thousand dollars, and you will need several. Moulds are usually the biggest single cost, commonly somewhere between twenty and seventy thousand dollars depending on complexity.
- **Stage your payments:** make development fees conditional on approving the prototype, which also frees you from paying for moulds if it fails.
- **Only certify final units.** Even minor changes after certification can invalidate it.
- **Timeline, conservatively 9 to 12 months:** design 6 to 8 weeks (less if you are decisive); each sampling cycle 2 to 6 weeks, mostly shipping and testing; moulds at least a month, plus a few weeks of adjustment; raw materials 4 to 6 weeks, partly overlapping with mould-making; assembly 2 to 6 weeks; export freight from a couple of weeks by air to around six by sea.
- **Order slow parts first.** Batteries can take around 45 days, compared with about 20 for plastic parts.

## Example
Say a brand selling a portable blender sees repeated complaints about a weak blade coupling. It sketches a redesigned base, has the factory produce a 3D-printed model to check grip and size, then a CNC prototype for torque testing. The second CNC round passes. With sample quality, tooling quote and unit price known, it approves tooling, uses the six-week tooling window to finalise packaging, and certifies the trial-run units before using a batch of them for creator seeding.

## Common mistakes
- Underestimating time; changing the concept after seeing a sample can add months.
- Assuming the first sample will pass.
- Overspending because development drags on, or landing on a per-unit price that cannot support growth.
- Not having branded packaging ready alongside the finished product; factories cannot pack without it, so fix a firm deadline for branding.
- Mass-produced units that drift from the approved sample; set standards early and walk the production floor in person.
- Letting the factory own the industrial design by default, which can trap you with that supplier. Discuss design rights early and take advice from a patent attorney.
