# Developing a Custom Skincare or Supplement Formula

## What it is
Getting your own skincare or supplement formula made. Buyers in these categories largely pay for the narrative, so build the brief around the ingredients and angles your marketing will use. Containers and boxes often hold everything up, so packaging starts on day one alongside the formula.

## When to use it
- Moving from a white-label product to your own formula.
- Fixing a product with scent, texture or efficacy complaints.
- Adding a new product for an existing avatar.

## How (steps)
1. **Choose the approach.**
   - *Copy an existing product:* the quickest route.
   - *Copy and add ingredients:* the route most brands take.
   - *Build from scratch:* hire an independent chemist (more control and ownership of the formula, though the factory may still adjust it), or use a formulation house that already knows which products sell, runs its own lab and can return samples within a few weeks.
2. **Brief with the marketing in mind.** Share the hero ingredients you plan to feature, the angles you will run, and anything off-limits: particular ingredients, harsh chemicals, packaging preferences, or a wish to stay as natural as possible.
3. **Give a target price.** Manufacturers request it so they can design within your budget, not to squeeze you. Work it out from your acquisition cost and the margin you need. No one can price a formula blind, because the bottle, box and ingredients all change the number.
4. **Start packaging in parallel.** Choose between a printed label on a stock bottle or printing directly onto the bottle (which feels more premium), pick a material (plastic, glass, aluminium) and a format (jar, tube, pump). Request the die lines for container and carton, produce the artwork, then decide carton finishes. See [[Raising Perceived Value Through Packaging]].
5. **Sample widely.** Approach three to five manufacturers and ask each for two to five versions, so you end up with fifteen or more samples. Rate each for smell, feel and results, rank them, and cut to two suppliers.
6. **Run extra rounds with the finalists.** Where quotes are far apart, still sample both: ask the cheaper maker to reach the better one's standard, or ask the pricier one to cut cost without changing smell or feel.
7. **Pick the manufacturer** based on how quickly and clearly it communicates and turns around new samples, how open it is (a refusal to break down ingredients can mean you are dealing with a reseller), its certifications, and finally price, which only decides things if it would stop the product scaling.
8. **Ask for an itemised quote** covering carton, container and formula (ingredients plus processing) separately. If the formulator buys packaging on your behalf, it is adding a markup.
9. **Manufacture.** Your deposit pays for ingredients and packaging. Filling normally starts only after the containers arrive and pass inspection; then the batch is mixed, filled and assembled. Request photos and video from the container supplier, the carton supplier and the filling line.

## Thresholds and timelines
- **Certificates from the start:** facility registration for the US, cosmetics GMP for Europe, and tests covering microbes, preservative challenge, shelf stability and how the formula reacts with its container. A maker unable to supply these test results is less trustworthy, whatever certificate it holds. See [[Product Compliance and Manufacturer Agreements]].
- **Sample cost:** usually a few hundred dollars from Asian formulators; premium contract manufacturers charge more yet clear retailer audits easily, useful if you may sell the business.
- **Unit cost range:** a 50 to 100 ml moisturiser can cost from under a dollar to several dollars with premium ingredients; pricey imported actives may need another sampling round.
- **Research to production start:** six to eight weeks at best; around three months if extra rounds are needed.
- **Lead times:** directly printed containers take roughly a month; stock containers with labels rarely hold things up; cartons and filling each take around two weeks. Expect the first order to take six to seven weeks overall.
- **Order roughly twice the containers you need** the first time, so the next run is quicker and fits a monthly or six-weekly buying rhythm.

## Example
Say a skincare brand wants a calming night cream built on two ingredients its ads already feature. It briefs four factories with a target unit cost, asks each for three variations, and starts bottle production in week one. A small tester panel scores 12 samples; two factories go to a second round. The cheaper one is asked to match the dearer one's texture, and does. The brand requests an itemised quote and finds the formulator was adding margin on boxes, so it sources boxes directly.

## Common mistakes
- Relying on one maker with no backup.
- Leaving packaging until the formula is finished rather than running both together.
- Skipping the target price and getting back samples that are too expensive to use.
- Accepting a single combined price that hides a markup on packaging.
