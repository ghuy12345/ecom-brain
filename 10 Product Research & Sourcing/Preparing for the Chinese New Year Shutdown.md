# Preparing for the Chinese New Year Shutdown

## What it is
A preparation plan for the annual factory shutdown around Chinese New Year. The real disruption lasts far longer than the public holiday. Line workers and office staff head home early, so output slows to a crawl in the final week or so beforehand. Production times can roughly double as staff disappear and orders pile up. Warehouses in China often shut for about a week. Afterwards, recovery takes weeks, because people come back slowly and some never return. The date shifts every year, so recalculate your deadlines annually: carriers stop accepting bookings roughly a week ahead, and upstream material suppliers such as steel mills stop even sooner.

No factory makes everything itself. The suppliers of its boxes, plastic parts and metalwork face the same staffing cliff, so ask about those inputs as well as your own order.

## When to use it
- From late Q4 onward, whenever you source from China (or from factories that rely on Chinese components).
- When stock levels are tight going into the new year.
- When product development or tooling is mid-flight around the holiday.

## How (steps)
**Production.**
1. Ask how long raw materials will take before and after the break, and how much you should buy or put a deposit on now so production can resume fast.
2. If the whole order will not be ready, agree (or pay a little extra for) a part shipment, and give the factory what it needs to hurry the remainder.
3. Find out where the factory is struggling and solve whatever you can on your side, for example ordering packaging early.
4. Where possible, visit both the factory and its main suppliers before the break to see your order is genuinely moving, and return soon after.
5. Assume staff will come back later than the official dates suggest.

**Shipping and inventory.**
1. Reserve sea freight now. Extra charges and cargo being bumped to later sailings start weeks before the holiday, and prices can rise two- or threefold.
2. Speak with your forwarder and warehouses too, not just the factory, so stock can both move and be stored.
3. Calculate days of supply: current stock plus in-transit plus in-production, rolled forward at your sales run rate through the shutdown.
4. Bridge the gap with a mix of air and sea. Fly in roughly a week of stock to cover the period when the origin warehouse is shut, instead of sending a container that is half full.
5. The aim is to keep ads running. Selling flown-in units at break-even or a slight loss can be the right call when you know your numbers. Heavy, thin-margin items deserve only a small air shipment; light items can justify a larger cushion.

**Tooling and development.**
1. Use the weeks beforehand to finalise samples and list everything that must be settled before moulds are cut. Have the mould deposit ready to pay before the break.
2. Order mould steel early; steel suppliers stop first, and this step takes up the opening week or so of a tooling process that runs about a month.
3. Engineers often have more time during this period, so design work may progress faster than usual.

**Payments and leverage.**
1. Settle any overdue invoices before the break, when factories need cash for staff bonuses.
2. If a strong Q4 left you with spare cash but you expect a slower Q1, share a forward plan covering three to eight months. Be generous now, prepaying if needed, and in Q2 ask for lower prices and better terms tied to agreed milestones. If you use several factories, weigh their offers against your yearly forecast.

**Relationships.** Join or phone into a supplier's year-end celebration if you are invited, and send small, considered gifts to the engineers and production leads as well as the owners. Strong relationships buy you priority when you are not the biggest customer.

## Example
Say a gadget brand finishes a production run ten days before the holiday but has not booked a container. The next available vessel is weeks away, and its US stock will run out mid-February. Calculating days of supply, it air-freights one week of its best-seller to bridge the gap, accepts a thin margin on those units, and keeps ads running. The rest ships by sea when capacity returns. Next year it books freight in early Q4.

## Common mistakes
- Getting goods made but leaving freight unbooked.
- Relying only on the factory's schedule rather than talking to forwarders and warehouses.
- Heading into the break with thin stock and no days-of-supply calculation.
- Asking for urgent changes mid-shutdown, when nothing will move.
- Under-communicating. In this window daily check-ins on whether work is on time are justified.
