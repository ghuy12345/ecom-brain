# Product Compliance and Manufacturer Agreements

## What it is
The paperwork and testing that make a product legal and sellable. It protects you in three ways: goods are less likely to be held at the border, you satisfy what marketplaces demand, and you limit legal liability. A manufacturer agreement adds a second layer by writing down who is responsible for quality, liability and confidentiality.

This note is a general orientation, not legal or regulatory advice. Requirements vary between countries and change frequently. Check the current rules with the relevant authority or a compliance specialist before you act, and if an AI assistant helps with research, have an expert confirm what it tells you. Never make medical or disease claims about a product; what you claim is part of compliance too.

## When to use it
- Before your first production order of any branded product.
- Before listing on a large marketplace or selling to a retailer.
- When entering a new country.
- When switching manufacturers.

## How (steps)
**1. Map the requirements for your category.** Typical examples, using the US as a reference:
- *Skincare:* registering a facility is different from having a product approved, so do not confuse the two. Expect GMP certification, tests for microbes, shelf stability and formula-container compatibility, claims that stay within the rules, and a label stating where it was made.
- *Supplements:* a registered facility, notifications for novel ingredients where they apply, GMP, testing for heavy metals and for ingredient identity, and certificates of analysis from an independent lab (these usually take about a week).
- *Electronics:* radio-frequency approval is required in the US; a recognised safety listing is usually expected by retailers even where the law does not demand it; CE marking applies in the EU; plus drop testing, battery safety paperwork, and electrical safety and interference testing.
- *Marketplaces:* barcodes bought from the official issuing body, safety paperwork, regulator letters and rules specific to each category.

**2. Budget for third-party testing.** Allow from a few thousand up to roughly ten thousand dollars depending on the category, and compare quotes from several well-regarded international labs.

**3. Verify supplier certificates yourself.** Look up registration numbers, confirm the GMP certificate has not expired, match equipment IDs, check that certificates of analysis come from independent labs, and confirm any listing marks. See [[Finding and Vetting Manufacturers]] for checking the labs themselves.

**4. Consider established suppliers for supplements.** For a first supplement, an experienced manufacturer can save time because it often has the necessary lab reports and certifications on file already. Verify those documents anyway.

**5. Write a manufacturer agreement.** Try to place liability for manufacturing faults with the factory, attach letters authorising them to produce under your brand, address confidentiality, and get signatures. On top of that, buy product liability insurance.

**6. Tie in QC and payment.** Spell out how inspections work and what level of defects is acceptable (an acceptable quality limit, or AQL), look at production waivers where they apply to skincare, and do not pay the full amount before production.

## Example
Say a brand is launching a vitamin gummy in the US and later on a large marketplace. It chooses an established manufacturer with GMP certification, then independently checks the certificate's validity and the lab behind each certificate of analysis, including heavy-metals results. It has a consultant review label claims (structure-and-function wording only, nothing about treating disease) and country-of-origin labelling. The manufacturer agreement assigns liability for manufacturing defects to the factory and sets an AQL for inspection, and the brand adds product liability insurance before launch.

## Common mistakes
- Cutting compliance corners to launch sooner; seized shipments and delistings cost far more than tests.
- Trusting a supplier's certificate without verifying it.
- Leaving the country of manufacture off the label.
- Writing ad or page claims that outrun the product's evidence. See [[Product Integrity and Claims That Outgrow Reality]].
- Paying the full order value upfront with no agreement in place.
