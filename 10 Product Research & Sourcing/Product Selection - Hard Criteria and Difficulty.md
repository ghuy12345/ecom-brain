# Product Selection - Hard Criteria and Difficulty

## What it is
A two-pass filter for choosing what to sell. The first pass is a set of hard criteria: numbers a product must clear before you spend a cent on ads. The second pass estimates difficulty. With enough work most products can find buyers, yet the effort required varies enormously, and that difficulty is fairly predictable from three factors: how strong the underlying desire is, how aware the market already is, and how sophisticated (claim-saturated) it has become.

There is no research technique that reliably produces a winner. What a good filter does is stop you testing products that were doomed by their economics or by a market you could never break into, so each test has a reason behind it instead of relying on luck.

## When to use it
- Before testing any new product, whether you found it in a marketplace, a competitor's ad or your own idea list.
- When you are a beginner and tempted to grab items from "top products" lists.
- When adding a second product and checking whether it deserves the same budget as the first.

## How (steps)
1. **Check average order value.** After bundles, aim for roughly $60 or more. Ad costs per thousand impressions are largely fixed, so a cheap item cannot absorb the cost of acquiring a buyer. As a starting band, roughly $40 to $80 is comfortable: cheap enough that purchases arrive quickly, high enough to cover acquisition.
2. **Check markup.** Price should be at least 3x your landed cost (product plus shipping), and 5x is better. Thin margins look fine at low spend and break when you scale.
3. **Check trend direction.** Look at five years of search interest in your target country. You do not need a rising trend; flat is fine. A declining trend is a no.
4. **Check weight and ticket size.** Avoid heavy or bulky items early on: freight and customs eat the margin and add risk. Lightweight categories such as skincare and supplements are easy to ship. Avoid very high-ticket items until you have the capital to survive slow learning periods.
5. **Ignore lifetime value for now.** Subscriptions and repeat purchase are a bonus, not a reason to pick a product at the start.
6. **Apply soft criteria.** Pick something you are at least not repelled by, so you can film it and talk about it. Stick to products you genuinely think deliver; test it yourself or check its efficacy quickly before committing.
7. **Estimate difficulty.**
   - *Magnitude of desire:* the more intensely people want the outcome, the higher the price they accept and the easier the sale. Mild wants (tidier desks, say) usually stay low-priced and rarely scale past organic sales. Appearance-related products can carry deep desire too. See [[Mass Desire - Channel It, Don't Create It]].
   - *Market awareness:* the more aware your buyers already are, the easier. Beginners should work at solution-aware or product-aware levels; product-aware ads alone can carry a lot of spend. See [[Market Awareness Levels]].
   - *Market sophistication:* the more claims buyers have already heard, the harder it is to stand out. See [[Market Sophistication Stages]].
8. **Pass the result to the differentiation gate.** A product that clears the criteria still needs an answer to "how will you stand out?" before it gets budget. See [[Product Differentiation - How Will You Stand Out]].

## Example
Say you are comparing two products. A desk organiser sells for $22, costs $6 landed, and solves a mild annoyance. It clears markup but fails on order value and desire strength: even a strong ad will struggle to acquire a buyer profitably. A lightweight sleep supplement sells for $45 (or $90 as a two-pack bundle), costs $9 landed, sits in a flat-to-rising search trend, and targets a strong, urgent desire in a solution-aware market. It clears every hard criterion. Its difficulty is moderate to high because the category is sophisticated, which tells you the differentiation work will matter more than the product itself.

## Common mistakes
- Treating lists of "trending products" as research rather than a starting pool to filter.
- Choosing heavy or high-ticket items first and running out of budget before the first sales arrive.
- Chasing unaware markets because they look uncrowded, when they are the hardest to sell into.
- Jumping between unrelated verticals instead of going deeper with one avatar, where knowledge compounds.
- Buying custom stock before testing traction; testing an existing product first is cheaper.
- Spending weeks researching. Time-box the search (a day of focused work is plenty), launch, and learn from the test.
