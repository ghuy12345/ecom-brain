# Supplier Types - Factories, Trading Companies and Agents

## What it is
When you buy product, you are dealing with one of four kinds of entity: a **factory**, a **trading company**, a **dropshipping or fulfilment agent**, or a **sourcing company**. None is best in every case. Which fits depends on order volume, the hours you can spend managing suppliers, and the degree of product change you want. Knowing which one you are actually talking to also tells you where hidden margin and quality risk sit.

## When to use it
- Choosing your first supplier, or moving from dropshipping to branded stock.
- When prices, lead times or quality feel inconsistent and you are not sure who really makes the product.
- When deciding whether to keep using an intermediary as you scale.

## How (steps)
**Know the four types.**
- **Factories.** Raw-material and component factories (plastics, bottles, batteries, circuit boards) feed assembly factories, which you usually deal with. Pros: lowest price, in-house engineers, real customisation. Cons: higher minimum order quantities (MOQs), fixed processes that resist your special QC requirements, one product vertical only, low priority for small buyers, often weaker English, and no visibility into their own component suppliers. Small factories are hungry but under-resourced; medium ones balance price and resources; large ones suit high volume.
- **Trading companies.** Offices that resell factory output, usually in big cities with English-speaking staff and listed on B2B marketplaces. Pros: more variety, lower MOQs from pooled orders, fewer language issues, sometimes better inspection. Cons: hidden margin on the ex-factory price, frequent posing as factories, slower customisation and slower defect resolution because of the extra layer.
- **Dropshipping and fulfilment agents.** A warehouse plus purchasing office. Pros: one contact, very low MOQs (50 to 100 units), fast lead times from existing stock, quotes within a day. Cons: speed beats quality, stacked margins, no price transparency, white-label only (logo, colour, packaging) rather than real changes, possible use of non-compliant factories, and trademark exposure on copied products.
- **Sourcing companies.** Research teams with a factory network that act as your representative: development, QC, purchase planning, payment-term negotiation. Pros: stronger due diligence and aligned incentives. Cons: you rely on them for supplier communication, and a weak one is just a trading company.

**Match type to stage.** Categories with very broad catalogues, such as kitchen tools or pet toys, usually suit trading companies, since one factory rarely makes everything in the range. A fulfilment agent is the least time; managing factories directly eats time every day; a sourcing company falls in the middle and still needs a fulfilment partner.

**Verify a "factory".**
1. Check that three names agree: the one on the marketplace listing, the one on the certificates, and the one on the building. A mismatch points to a trading company.
2. Many Chinese factory names begin with their city; a name that breaks the pattern deserves a closer look.
3. Treat a huge price concession (around 50%) as a warning: it usually exposes a middleman margin.
4. Middlemen are a normal part of the trade, so this is not a sign of dishonesty. Just price accordingly.

**Check a low sourcing fee.** If a sourcing company quotes around 5%, work out whether that could fund verification, coordination and oversight on your order size. If not, margin is likely hidden in the factory price. Ask to see factory cost and markup separately.

## Example
Say a skincare brand buys a moisturiser through a fulfilment agent and customers complain about shelf life, scent and texture. The agent has no idea which factory makes it and can only alter the label. The fix is to go to a factory network directly, sample from several makers and rebuild the formula. A separate trap: a factory making hair-removal devices will keep offering you shavers and trimmers, because factories only propose items from their own line.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming a guided tour of a site means you have seen the actual maker.
- Celebrating a halved price instead of asking what it reveals.
- Expecting the tiny MOQs and one-week turnarounds of dropshipping from genuine production; those only exist because agents sell stock that already exists.
- Passing everything through a middleman and expecting them to grasp your brand.
- Letting a factory design your product without agreeing who owns the design.
- Ignoring time zones: a six-hour gap is workable, twelve means slow iteration.
