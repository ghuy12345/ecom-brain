# Supplier Payment Terms and Incoterms

## What it is
Payment terms set the timing of what you owe a supplier: an upfront deposit, a balance when production finishes, or a delay of 30, 60 or more days after delivery. Money sitting in inventory cannot fund advertising, and switching ads off makes the platform start learning again, which can cost you a whole season. Terms are also negotiable in degrees: a supplier who turns down a straight 30- or 60-day delay may still agree to an in-between arrangement that gets you most of the benefit. The cash-cycle maths behind this sits in [[Cash Conversion Cycle]].

Incoterms are the standard trade rules that decide who handles freight and risk, and therefore when your payment clock starts:
- **Ex Works (EXW):** the price covers goods at the factory gate only. Collection, export clearance and freight are yours, and the clock runs from collection. Usually a little more expensive overall than FOB.
- **FOB (free on board):** the factory takes the goods to the port and clears them for export, and the clock starts there. Frequently a touch cheaper than EXW, and your risk only begins at the port.
- **DDP (delivered duty paid):** one price covers freight, duties and tariffs right to your warehouse, with the carrier acting as importer. The clock starts when goods arrive. Common for lightweight products.

Where goods are going also matters: stock fulfilled inside the producing country is usable immediately, so suppliers expect paying sooner than for a container spending weeks at sea.

## When to use it
- After several successful orders with the same supplier.
- When moving from dropshipping to branded stock: build the forecast, then ask for a short credit period (for example 30 days) to protect cash.
- When rising volume is squeezing cash even though the business is profitable.

## How (steps)
1. **Earn credibility first.** A supplier who has never worked with you will not extend credit because your brand is well known. Build a track record.
2. **Present two scenarios side by side:** how the business performs on today's terms (for instance 30% upfront and 70% when finished) versus with a 30-day delay. Factories tend to think in short-term profit; a forecast shows them steady line utilisation and lets them win better prices from their own suppliers.
3. **Bring a partnership plan** with milestones rather than waiting for ideas from them.
4. **Prepare documents:** sales history, profit and loss, balance sheet, and a simple explanation of what it costs you to acquire a customer, which factories rarely understand. Keep the pitch short.
5. **Let them set the risk level.** As your volume grows, ask what exposure they can live with.
6. **Offer alternative structures** when a straight credit period is declined:
   - *Rolling deposit:* leave a permanent deposit of 10% to 30% against raw materials, then pay each shipment in full, or 15 to 30 days after it ships.
   - *Pay as you ship:* put a deposit on a big batch, ship a slice each month, and call forward more if an ad takes off.
   - *Credit line:* agree a fixed credit ceiling on a 30-day delay against a larger order book, so your exposure has a known limit.
   - *Development credit:* while you pay for samples, tooling and certification on new items, request delayed payment on products already running.
   - *Trade margin for time:* accept a marginally higher unit cost in return for a longer delay, such as moving from 60 to 90 days.
7. **Look beyond the factory.** Regular freight bookings can earn their own credit period, and logistics can cost more than the goods themselves. US importers can switch duty payments to a monthly statement. Bonded or free trade zones postpone duties until goods leave the zone, in exchange for paperwork and rules. Rising tariffs are a fair reason to ask for longer terms.
8. **If refused, ask what would unlock the next level,** or negotiate faster production instead, which reduces how much stock and cash you need.

## Example
Say a skincare brand has ordered from the same factory for a year on 30% deposit and 70% on completion. It visits in person, presents a forecast showing how much faster it could grow with longer terms, and offers a rolling deposit. The factory agrees to a 20% deposit with the remainder due two months after dispatch. Keep the factory's risk in mind: a bespoke serum built on an imported ingredient it cannot use elsewhere is far riskier to fund than an off-the-shelf gadget it always stocks, so expect stricter terms on custom formulas.

## Common mistakes
- Requesting credit before any orders have been placed.
- Taking pushback personally instead of understanding the supplier's risk.
- Focusing on the factory and ignoring duty and fulfilment terms.
- Visiting during the Chinese New Year period or your own peak season.
- Accepting an intermediary's claim that terms are impossible; buyers pooling many brands often can get them.
