# Inventory Planning for Q4-Heavy Brands

## What it is
A planning rhythm for brands that make a large share of revenue in Q4. Much of how your peak season goes is settled in the six months from January. A familiar trap: a big Q4, a relaxed start to the year, a surprise stock-out, months of catch-up, and a summer that begins already behind on peak preparation. The loop repeats and eats profit you could have reinvested.

Two seasonal forces drive this. The Chinese New Year break lasts much longer than the holiday itself: upstream suppliers stop early, and factories come back short-staffed and need time to rehire, which can leave you six weeks or more without fresh materials. Freight follows a Q4-like pattern too, with higher prices and scarcer container space from about November through February. Producing elsewhere does not fully protect you, as components often still come from China.

## When to use it
- Every January, as the start of the annual cycle.
- When planning a launch in Q2 or Q3.
- From mid-year, when locking Q4 supply and ad budgets.

## How (steps)
**Q1: lock supply and negotiate.**
1. Reserve production capacity before the factories close and talk your plan through with them.
2. Prioritise your top sellers so smaller variants cannot hold up a launch. Buy components early, because seasonal demand can raise their prices.
3. Build a stock buffer and ask each supplier how quickly it expects to ramp back up after the break.
4. Kick off new product development early so the factory understands your plans for the year.
5. Place purchase orders, not just forecasts. Many factories will not buy materials without a PO, so make clear that materials must be bought before the break.
6. Arrange backup factories and backup suppliers for components, packaging included, as soon as the holiday ends rather than waiting until mid-year.
7. Get each factory's and sub-supplier's closing and reopening dates; they all differ.
8. Do your negotiating early in the year, because once orders climb in the spring your leverage fades. Share full-year volumes (noting that most falls in Q4), plan backwards to shorten peak-season lead times, ask for terms that support growth, and compare bill-of-materials costs between factories. See [[Supplier Payment Terms and Incoterms]].

**Q2: refine and launch.**
1. If Q1 performed, increase volumes and use that momentum to improve MOQs and pricing.
2. Treat any Q2 launch as a test, and remember its stock has to be produced in Q1.
3. Pinpoint which input in your category takes longest to source, and how big an order would speed it up: for gadgets that is often batteries or circuit boards; for directly printed skincare and supplements, the containers; sometimes a specific imported active.
4. Stock for spring events (Mother's Day, Father's Day, marketplace sale days), weighing the full cost of holding stock against the cost of selling out. Work back from the date: if customers receive orders in May, goods ship in April.
5. Rate suppliers on punctuality, consistency of quality and any disruptions.

**Quality through the transition.** Fix defects before you scale. After a break, confirm the same quality lead and material suppliers are still in place. Log defects for each run in a shared sheet, splitting quick fixes from design problems that need a new version, and tackle those early so an improved version can still ship before the year ends. See [[Defect Rate as a Profit Lever]].

**Q3 and Q4: size the peak.**
1. Have peak-season stock secured roughly two months ahead (by July or August), with suppliers able to cope with the volume.
2. Pull last year's Q4 revenue and ad spend month by month, compare this year's performance, and build projections.
3. Base stock and ad budgets on those projections, and avoid ordering beyond them unless you are very confident it will sell.
4. Confirm capacity with your 3PL and watch tariff and import-rule changes closely.

**Align the team.** Keep ad plans in line with actual inventory (a stock-out means pausing ads and losing their optimisation), sync sales forecasts with factory capacity, and brief customer service: an unchanged ticket rate on much higher volume still means a flood of tickets.

## Example
Say a gift-friendly kitchenware brand did most of last year's sales in November and December. In January it issues POs for its top three SKUs, pre-orders packaging, and books a backup box supplier. In Q2 it launches one new product as a test and stocks for Mother's Day. By August, Q4 stock is produced against a projection built from last year's monthly split and this year's growth rate, with a modest buffer rather than a doubled order.

## Common mistakes
- Treating the first half as a quiet period and ordering too little.
- Sharing forecasts without backing them with purchase orders.
- Having no second source for something basic like boxes, which can halt an entire production run.
- Scaling marketing without stock.
- Over-ordering on optimism and trapping cash.
