# Building a Quality Control System

## What it is
A structure of checkpoints and standards that catches defects where they start. Quality belongs to the entire chain of suppliers, not to one moment on the production line. A fault may originate in a part made weeks earlier by a sub-supplier, so one check at the end will never reveal where it came from. Without that root cause, every problem turns into finger-pointing ("it left us in perfect condition").

Good quality also eats into the factory's profit: slower output, extra headcount, more training. Their incentive therefore pulls against yours on price, which is exactly why someone independent should be checking.

## When to use it
- Before the first production run of any product.
- When defect rates rise or returns spike.
- When switching factories or sub-suppliers, or after long holiday breaks.

## How (steps)
**Choose who does your QC.**
- *The factory:* not suitable, because its interests differ from yours.
- *A trading company:* unreliable, and openness varies widely.
- *Your fulfilment warehouse:* not suitable; it earns money by shipping, and has no power to fix causes.
- *Suitable options:* a sourcing company, a seasoned employee based where production happens, or a specialist inspection firm. If you hire locally, quiz candidates on QC before hiring; several years of experience spares you a lot of training.

**Place checks along the chain.**
1. Each sub-supplier inspects key parts before they leave.
2. The assembly plant inspects parts as they arrive.
3. Inspectors check units on the line mid-production.
4. A last check happens before goods are packed.
All parties should work from one identical specification, and every sub-supplier should understand how its part fits into the finished product.

**Assess the factory's own quality set-up.**
1. Sit down with the head of quality, who should not be the person running production, and agree the specification together.
2. Ensure a golden sample exists on both sides.
3. Walk the inspection stations: check the lighting, how far inspectors hold items from the light and their eyes, and what they treat as acceptable.
4. Look for proper test equipment and staff whose only job is quality, rather than an engineer juggling everything.
5. Request failure-mode analyses for both the design and the process, plus specs for details such as screw torque, glue quantity and solder joints.
6. Confirm the paperwork is actually used: ask who changes the spec sheets when the line switches products. A sheet for a different product hanging on your line is a warning sign.

**Write measurable acceptance criteria.** When the first pilot units come off the line, classify every flaw as tolerable or critical, keep reference samples, and define limits in millimetres (for example, a scratch up to a set length and width is acceptable and anything larger is rejected). Precise limits also make it far easier to claim money back for failures.

**Category checks.**
- *Packaging and hard goods:* compare finish against reference samples for a simple pass or fail.
- *Skincare, supplements, beauty:* make a pilot batch from the same ingredients that will go into production and match texture, taste and colour against the golden sample. Before every run, confirm that a certificate of analysis exists, ingredients were re-weighed and equipment calibrated.
- *Electronics:* when batteries arrive, put them through dozens of charge cycles (around 80 to 100), then measure remaining capacity and voltage; also test every switch, button and cable.

**Approve shipments independently.** A handful of units the factory chose for you proves nothing. Use an independent inspector, occasionally without warning, sampling roughly 10 to 20 units or about one in twenty of the order, and require a written report with photos and defect rates.

## Example
Say an apparel brand sees a rise in loose stitching. Its warehouse reports every returned unit as damaged, yet when the factory re-inspects those units on camera, most turn out to be fine; the real problem is a single embroidery sub-supplier. Because the brand had incoming inspection at the factory, it can trace the batch and fix the root cause instead of arguing about blame. A quality trade-off may follow: a better sub-supplier can cost more, and that cost belongs in your unit economics.

## Common mistakes
- Trusting the supplier to police itself.
- Cutting the on-site check because launch is close; a fault found days before the ship sails leaves no time to fix it.
- Letting certificates replace judgement. GMP, ISO 9001 or ISO 13485 tell you something, but not everything.
- Explaining standards only in words; many quality managers speak little English, so show photos, measurements and demonstrations.
- Swinging between extremes: either never checking again, or inspecting every single unit. Spot checks plus built-in checkpoints keep QC manageable.
