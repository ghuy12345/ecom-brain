# Working With a Factory Over Time

## What it is
The habits that turn a transactional supplier into a partner. Factories are wary of buyers because they cannot predict when, or whether, another order will arrive. Your pricing, payment terms, lead times, place in the production queue and day-to-day treatment all hinge on how the relationship is going. Being the largest account is unnecessary; being a preferred one is enough. That means being pleasant, reliable and visibly on a growth path. When you negotiate, push for faster turnaround and better quality as well as lower prices.

## When to use it
- From the first order onward with any manufacturer.
- When briefing a new product or packaging project.
- When you cannot be on site and need quality you can trust remotely.

## How (steps)
**Send production-ready files.**
1. Hand over print files, never mockups, in whatever format the factory specifies (frequently the original design file). Mark dimensions, embed or supply fonts, outline all text, and label layers and special print effects.
2. Get the factory's list of required items, tick each one off together, and keep a clear record of the final version.
3. Keep artwork editing on your side; when factories modify files themselves, wrong fonts and images creep in.

**Brief new projects clearly.**
1. Lead with a single page covering what the product is and what you want to achieve.
2. Spell out the problems you aim to fix, and invite them to share faults they have seen in comparable items.
3. Find out which certifications apply and the time each takes, which is often one to three months.
4. Agree a QC procedure that suits what they can actually do.
5. Make your expectations on price, timing and how often you will be in touch explicit from the start.

**Negotiate in windows, not constantly.**
1. *Pre-order:* map out where every component comes from and how long it takes, then bargain on price, speed or quality. Offering to pay your deposit fast in return for a modest discount is a polite opener that often succeeds.
2. *Around the first order:* give them an honest forecast for the next quarter, half-year and year so they can line up staff and their own suppliers. Agree deposits that track that forecast, then raise shorter lead times and lower prices. Inflated forecasts wreck credibility.
3. *Once a few orders are done:* reopen payment terms. See [[Supplier Payment Terms and Incoterms]].

**Treat defects as a shared problem.** Request the factory's own return figures, send a concise defect summary every month or more often, fix issues one by one, and tie your next big order to a fix. A typical remedy is extra units in the next shipment equal to the previous defect percentage. See [[Defect Rate as a Profit Lever]].

**Run QC remotely.**
1. Create illustrated inspection sheets that show what to look at, at which stage, with specs for every critical part.
2. Agree a **golden sample**: one approved, flawless unit that sets the benchmark for all inspections.
3. Ask for photos and video when components arrive, during production and before dispatch, and insist on hearing about problems straight away.
4. Only if that breaks down, bring in an independent inspector with a full checklist. Have them examine roughly one unit in twenty, and read their report before releasing the final payment.

**Build the relationship.** Chemistry matters; if you clash with a capable factory, it can still be worth moving on. Invite suppliers to see how your business runs. Go in person when possible: issues get fixed on the spot, you notice process problems invisible from a desk, and custom samples come together much faster face to face than by chat or at trade fairs. When socialising with suppliers, leave business out of it.

**Category notes.** In skincare and supplements, ask which ingredients are worth holding in reserve so production can start sooner; ingredient costs tend to climb year on year. In electronics, some parts expire on the shelf, but with every component ready, assembly can be fast.

## Example
Say a skincare brand is about to place its second order. Instead of pushing for a lower unit price by email every week, it sends a one-page 12-month forecast, a finalised artwork pack with a checklist, and a request for photos at each QC stage against the golden sample held by both sides. Two orders later, with a track record of on-time deposits, it asks to move from a large balance on delivery to part of the balance paid 30 days after shipping, and gets it.

## Common mistakes
- Leaving expectations vague enough to be misread.
- Inflating forecasts to get attention.
- Submitting unfinished artwork and going round in correction cycles.
- Pestering about price outside the natural negotiation moments.
