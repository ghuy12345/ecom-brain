# Finding and Vetting Manufacturers

## What it is
The due-diligence process for choosing who makes your product. Your manufacturer buys the raw materials, checks quality, assembles, and gets goods out the door on schedule, and you will be in contact almost daily. That makes how well they communicate as important as what they charge. Strong marketing cannot compensate for a shaky production partner.

## When to use it
- Moving from a dropshipped product to your own branded stock.
- Adding a new product or replacing a supplier with quality problems.
- Before any tooling, formula or large first order.

## How (steps)
1. **Search broadly.** B2B marketplaces are useful but crowded with noise. Also check platforms in the local language, official product and device registries, the exhibitor lists of trade fairs, and industry contacts. Plenty of excellent contract manufacturers never list on a marketplace. Do not read too much into listings either; stock photography is common.
2. **Run a cheap filter.** Message a large batch of suppliers (dozens is normal) with one simple instruction, such as connecting with you on a particular messaging app. Anyone who ignores it is out.
3. **Cross-check answers.** Feed what one supplier tells you into your questions for the next. Within a few conversations you learn realistic prices and lead times, and who is being honest.
4. **Work out who is a real factory.** Genuine makers answer precisely and quickly, have spec sheets and certificates to hand, and talk like engineers. Polished English proves nothing on its own. Geography helps too: production for a category concentrates around certain ports and industrial hubs, so a supplier based somewhere with no relevant industry is probably a reseller. See [[Supplier Types - Factories, Trading Companies and Agents]].
5. **Score the replies.** First rank on speed and straightness of answers. Then ask about staff numbers, the size of the QC team, testing and production machinery, level of automation, who will manage your account, and whether you can speak to engineers directly. Check the certificates are real. Request a bill of materials; suppliers open about costs tend to be open about everything else.
6. **Size up the engineer.** The ideal engineer grasps what the product is meant to solve, is involved on the production floor, and writes and owns the QC procedures. Find out whether engineers or line staff are paid bonuses on quality.
7. **Compare samples head to head.** Pick three to five candidates, code them (A, B, C...) and have every sample delivered to the same address. Measure each against a benchmark, either a competitor's product or your concept. For skincare, assess scent, then texture, then results with a tester group of about twenty. For electronics, push much harder, including durability and drop testing.
8. **Talk about failures.** Ask which defects they have run into, the oddest one, how it was solved, and what will stop it happening on your item. Favour suppliers who volunteer problems; avoid ones who cover them up or apply quick patches.
9. **Check the paperwork behind certificates.** Research any lab you do not recognise, confirm the lab really tested for that factory, and see whether big retailers blacklist reports from it. Well-known international labs are the safer bet.

## Thresholds and red flags
- **Factory size:** small shops (roughly 50 to 75 people) can promise more than they deliver; mid-sized ones (100 to 200) usually combine fair pricing with decent resources and testing, which makes them the best starting point; large plants (200 and up) bring big minimum orders, long waits and little engineer time.
- **Category certificates** signal experience, for example toy safety standards or ISO 13485 for medical devices.
- **Red flags:** fuzzy answers about process, replies that need chasing, brushing off a prototype problem as something to fix later (which can turn into costly tooling errors), rigidity, and no certificates.
- **Where your time pays off:** bringing down what you pay per customer normally beats squeezing supply-chain costs, so set a sensible limit on how deep you go. Bring in third-party inspectors and compliance specialists where they add the most.

## Example
Say a supplement brand shortlists four manufacturers. One replies within an hour with a spec sheet, its GMP certificate and a candid note that a capsule coating it used last year caused clumping. Another sends a glossy catalogue, answers vaguely and needs three follow-ups. Samples from both arrive at the same address and are tested blind by a small panel. The first wins on communication and transparency even though it is slightly more expensive; the second is cut.

## Common mistakes
- Taking a slick listing or good English as evidence you have found the maker.
- Getting samples from just one supplier, which leaves nothing to compare against.
- Accepting certificates at face value without looking into the lab.
- Choosing purely on price before seeing samples.
