# Competitor Research - White Space and Weaknesses

## What it is
Competitor research is not about copying what others do. It is about finding the gaps: pains they ignore, claims they cannot make, segments they neglect, angles they have worn out and mechanisms they have already claimed. The result is a short list of decisions - what to lead with, what to avoid, how to price and which page to build.

The fixed per-competitor record format and the alternative-solutions map are in [[Competitor Records and the Alternative-Solutions Map]]. This note covers strategy.

## When to use it
- After you have a first view of your own product and the market's desires.
- Before writing landing pages or choosing an offer - better offers and pricing presentation often outperform copy changes.
- When performance plateaus against a specific rival.

## How (steps)
1. **Pick two competitor sets.** Brands selling a *similar product* inform product-aware messaging. Brands serving a *similar audience* with a different product inform solution-aware messaging. Five or six per set is plenty; stop once additional names reveal nothing new about positioning or offers.
2. **Do the core reading by hand.** An AI deep-research pass can give you a head start, but reading competitors yourself changes how you think, which is the real benefit.
3. **Profile each competitor across six areas:**
   - *Ideal customer* - who they speak to, which frustrations and beliefs, which awareness level, what language.
   - *Acquisition* - which platforms and ad formats they rely on, whether traffic goes straight to a product page or through a bridge page, and their organic and search presence.
   - *Core messaging* - the value they promise, the outcome, their tone and any mechanism they claim.
   - *Recurring angles* - an angle running three months or more is probably working; one tried once and dropped probably was not.
   - *Pricing and offer* - what the hero product costs, plus bundles, subscriptions, promotions and guarantees.
   - *Sentiment* - two- and three-star marketplace reviews (often the most honest), forum and group threads, social comments. What buyers love, you can echo louder; what they moan about is where you win.
4. **Note positioning, not just product.** A competitor framing its product as a gift or gesture of care reveals a stronger desire than "it tastes great". Being a specialist when a rival sells everything is a credibility advantage worth stating.
5. **Build a comparison grid** for your brand and the three strongest rivals, covering avatar, promise, mechanism, price, main channel, typical hook, best-loved and most-hated aspects, and the awareness stage each targets.
6. **Write the white space** - the pains, groups, awareness stages, mechanisms and kinds of proof that nobody covers well - and your differentiation in a sentence.
7. **Apply the decision rules:**
   - Worn-out angle -> drop it or position directly against it. Once shoppers have heard the same pitch from several brands, they tune it out.
   - Weakness found in their reviews -> make it the centre of your "why us" section.
   - Pain nobody addresses -> open your hook or headline with it.
   - Their mechanism -> make sure yours is unmistakably different.
   - Their price -> use it as an anchor, explaining why yours costs what it does.
   - Neglected segment -> give that group its own dedicated page.
8. **Treat weaknesses as hypotheses.** A flaw visible on a competitor's own site is a possible wedge, but if they sell well despite it, confirm with customer research before betting on it.

## Example
Imagine a soup brand selling in jars. One competitor positions its whole range as a way to show someone you care: buyers send a gift, the recipient picks the flavour, and there is a guarantee on the gift. That suggests a stronger desire than taste and an angle such as "send a jar of comfort". Another competitor ships flash-frozen; that becomes a "never frozen" wedge to validate with customers.

Now picture a supplement brand stalling against a rival that keeps adding ingredients. Rather than joining the ingredient race, it repositions around a new mechanism, frames simplicity as the benefit, sharpens the offer and launches a second version through a new delivery format (say, gummies) instead of a longer label.

## Common mistakes
- Cloning a competitor's page or message instead of learning its selling logic.
- Studying only same-product competitors and missing same-audience ones.
- Recording what competitors do without deciding what you will do differently.
- Leading with an angle every rival already runs.
- Over-reading a single competitor flaw before checking whether customers care.
- Researching from the wrong country, so you see the wrong ads and offers.
