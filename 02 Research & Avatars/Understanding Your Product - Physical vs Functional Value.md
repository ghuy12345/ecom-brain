# Understanding Your Product - Physical vs Functional Value

## What it is
Product understanding is the first research job, done before you pick desires, judge market sophistication or build avatars. Ads usually fail because of a misread of the product's place in the market, a misread of what buyers want, or weak execution. The first is cheapest to fix and most often skipped, since owners assume they know their own product.

Any product can be described twice:
- **The physical product** - what it is made of, its size and weight, who manufactures it.
- **The functional product** - the results: benefits delivered, problems removed, experiences created.

People pay for the functional product. The physical side mainly justifies the price and signals quality. Buyers will rarely make the jump from a spec to a result on their own ("stainless steel" does not automatically mean "your water stays cold all afternoon" to most shoppers), so you have to make that jump for them.

## When to use it
- When ads keep failing despite decent creative - often the desire being targeted is one the product cannot credibly deliver.
- Before choosing which desire or avatar to lead with, since each thing the product does well opens a route to a different buyer.

## How (steps)
1. **Record your own view first.** Fill in a short product brief as though briefing an outside team: your point of difference, the few things you wish every shopper knew, the top seller, the feel you want (luxury, wholesome, witty), what blocks purchases, any experts or well-known fans, strong and weak months, frequent questions, and every attractive claim you can make (guarantees, warranties, origin, testing). This written baseline is what you later compare with the market's view.
2. **List the physical attributes** - every material, component and spec.
3. **List the functional results** - what it does and every reason someone might buy it, including beliefs the market holds that you have not verified.
4. **Identify the mechanism** - link each attribute to the result it creates. The mechanism is the *how*, not the *what*. One mechanism can pay off differently by category: stainless steel keeps a bottle cold but makes a kettle easy to wipe clean.
5. **Map desire distance.** Picture the product at the centre with possible desires scattered around it. Close desires are believable but crowded. Distant desires are less contested but harder to believe. Aim for the middle ground: desires that obviously fit the product, even if that means some rivals.
6. **Test each candidate desire** - does the product plausibly deliver it, and has a trusted rival already claimed it? If either kills belief, drop it.
7. **Cold-read your own website.** Open it in a private window and read it as a stranger. Check whether each point from your product notes actually appears. Anything you know but the site does not say is a communication gap. Label every statement as a feature (a property), a benefit (the payoff for the buyer) or a claim (a differentiating assertion). Whether a newcomer would click to read more or click to buy hints at how ready visitors are.
8. **Revisit the baseline after research** and note where your perception and the market's reality differ.

## Example
Take insulated steel drinkware. Physically it is a metal container. Functionally it keeps drinks cold and carries a perceived health benefit over plastic. The problem: every competitor makes the cold claim, and the product is bought once and kept for years, so the cold-retention message weakens over time. Such a brand needs a fresh mechanism or a different, reachable desire.

Contrast that with an absurd stretch - pitching a cap as relief for a sore back. The desire is real and uncontested, but only because nobody would believe it.

Many brands avoid this problem by starting from the customer. Think of cookware made for people fed up with pans that degrade, a slim wallet for people sick of bulky ones, or chewable greens for people who hate drinking them - a format that then attracted parents buying for their kids. Use the same logic when choosing products: start from a problem people already want solved.

## Common mistakes
- Believing you already know the product and jumping straight to ads.
- Describing specs instead of results, and expecting shoppers to translate features into benefits.
- Chasing desires that are empty only because nobody would believe them. Over-reaching claims also create a treadmill: buyers who are let down make the market more sceptical, forcing a new mechanism or a new desire.
- Treating a feature you cannot turn into a benefit as a selling point. It is a research gap until you know why it matters.
- Choosing products with no clear problem in mind.
