# Researching Competitor Ads in Ad Libraries

## What it is
Public ad libraries show every ad a brand is currently running. Swipe tools add history, sorting and saving. Together they let you see what your market is being shown right now and - through ad age - which messages are probably working. This note covers searching and collecting; breaking a single winning ad down line by line is covered in [[Studying Winning Ads You Did Not Make]].

## When to use it
- During research for any brand, after desire hunting has given you terms to search.
- Before planning a new creative batch.
- When you need to discover indirect competitors you did not know existed.

## How (steps)
1. **Set the filters:** your market's country and language, every advertiser, and only ads currently live - that is what shoppers see right now.
2. **Search two ways.**
   - *Category keyword* - the whole niche, plus related terms, outcomes and adjacent products. This surfaces indirect solutions: different product types chasing the same desire.
   - *Brand by brand* - each known competitor.
3. **Find hidden brands.** If a brand will not show under its product name, open its website, find the social handle in the footer and search that. Still nothing? Go to its social profile, click through to the connected page, and search using that page's exact name.
4. **Read ad age as a winner signal.** Ads running for weeks or months are probably profitable; save and study those first. In a swipe tool, filter to your language and still-running ads, then sort by longest running. Old ads from brands that clearly spend a lot deserve the closest look. Caveat: an old ad can also simply be neglected.
5. **Save in an organised way.** Keep a separate board for each product or competitor, and select it in your saving extension first so every save is filed correctly. Annotate each save with days running.
6. **Decide what each winner is for.**
   - *Imitation* - copy the structure and swap in your desire (templatise the headline).
   - *Evidence* - read it as proof that a desire exists, even if you never reuse its wording.
   - *Iteration and ideation* are the other two modes; keep them distinct (see [[Iterations, Imitations and Ideations]]).
7. **Log what you learn immediately** in the research document: formats, pains in the market's words (visual and emotional), new problems you had not considered, visual ideas for the brain dump.
8. **Learn from the leader, do not clone it.** The biggest brand usually outspends and out-converts a challenger on its own message. Study its ads; do not copy them.
9. **Use the board as a vision board** in creative strategy sessions.

## Example
Say a brand sells a posture corrector. A keyword search for the category surfaces a lumbar-support cushion sold as a brace-free way to sit upright - an indirect competitor you were not tracking, chasing the same desire. A well-built competitor ad opens by promising each viewer the right fit for their back and desk setup, answering "is this right for me?" while naming the fear of a bulky, uncomfortable brace - evidence that advertiser researched the desire.

In a swipe tool, sorting by longest running reveals a haircare ad about hard water that has run for months. It exposes a problem you had not considered and a mechanism claim along the lines of "other formulas overlook hard water; this one is built for it". Repeating headline shapes - a long-awaited discovery ("Finally, [desire]"), a hidden secret behind [desire], or a switch story from [ordinary option] to [better one] - are templates worth adapting. Formats in the winners include raw unboxings, split-screen proof beside a demo, before-and-after shots, a presenter over a backdrop of screenshots, and reverse-effect hooks. A visual idea falls out too: a carousel where each card shows one attachment being used by a different type of buyer.

## Common mistakes
- Searching only your exact product name and missing indirect solutions.
- Overlooking ad age.
- Copying the market leader's message wholesale.
- Saving ads into one unsorted pile, so nothing is findable later.
- Collecting ads without writing down the desire, pain or format each one teaches.
