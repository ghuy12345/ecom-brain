# Forum Research for Beliefs and Hidden Segments

## What it is
Forums such as Reddit show what a market believes when nobody is selling to it. Unlike review forms or listings, threads capture arguments, doubts, recommendations and the exact words people use. Three things come out of good forum research: problems to solve, questions buyers ask, and beliefs or objections that would kill a sale. A fourth, less obvious output is **segmentation** - the discovery that one mass desire actually flows through several sub-groups with different needs.

Turning threads into a backlog of angles is covered in [[Building an Angle Backlog From Forums and Comments]]. This note is about the research itself.

## When to use it
- During desire hunting and avatar building.
- When deepening one proven angle - for example, researching the problem your best ad already leads with so a landing page can go further.
- When you suspect your audience is not one group.

## How (steps)
1. **Start from what is already working.** If an ad is winning, research its angle first. Where the account offers both a broad and a specific angle, research the specific one.
2. **Search like a buyer.** Phrase searches the way people talk: whether X is worth the money, what to buy, budget versus premium for durability, good middle options. Run the same searches on a general search engine, then again with "reddit" appended. Set a VPN to the country you advertise in so results match that market.
3. **Use each source for what it is good at.** Web search supplies data, numbers, how-it-works explanations and common beliefs. Forums give phrasing you could never invent - thread titles are often the best headline candidates.
4. **Log everything with a date.** Write down each well-upvoted belief, objection and question. Treat old threads with caution, even if they still seem current.
5. **Write killer beliefs as sentences** ("premium ones are no better than budget ones") in the objections area. Then look in the same thread for the counter-argument. You beat a belief by teaching, not by contradicting - explain the how and why the buyer has never heard.
6. **Watch for segment variables.** When a knowledgeable commenter answers a question with "depends - what's your hair type and how do you wear it?", you have found variables that split the market. Make "which traits split our customers?" a standing avatar question and draft segment lines ("Got thick hair? Here's why this helps").
7. **Read the indirect competition.** Supplements, apps, routines and other fixes for the same problem have already invested in research and claims you can learn from.
8. **Run a fact-to-claim check.** Every fact you find must be checked against your product's real data before it becomes a claim. Keep failed checks too - they are useful negative findings.
9. **Sort outputs into the research document:** verbatim and near-verbatim headline ideas, offer ideas shaped by the needs you uncovered, rough avatars, product gaps people keep mentioning, a buzzword tally, and anything surprising (prune later).

## Example
Say you sell a high-fibre breakfast cereal aimed at people with digestive trouble. Web search shows average fibre intake falls well short of recommendations; your nutrition panel shows one bowl covers around a fifth of daily needs - a supportable headline. The same check for ginger suggests a digestive-support bundle; the check for zinc fails because none of your products contain it, so that idea is dropped. Forum threads reveal frustration with doctors, a tiredness with pills ("I just want to eat real food"), and older buyers noting digestion slows with age - which hints at a mechanism and an avatar. Threads also show one sub-group on a strict exclusion diet that rules out an ingredient you use; that segment is one to avoid, not target.

In a premium hair-tool category, a heavily upvoted comment claiming "pricey ones are no better" is logged as a killer belief, and a reply doing the cost-of-ownership maths (several cheap replacements over a few years versus one durable tool) becomes the basis for education content.

## Common mistakes
- Researching without matching the target market's location.
- Using a web claim before confirming your product actually matches it.
- Stretching a source - generic advice to eat well does not endorse your product.
- Writing to "everyone" when the thread shows clear sub-groups.
- Asserting against a belief instead of educating past it.
- Stopping at the first page of comments; the best phrasing sits deeper.
