# Google Search Research - Listicles, Questions and Trust Gaps

## What it is
Search research reveals the pages and results your buyer looks at in the moments before purchase. The point is not to collect nice phrases to copy; it is to understand three things:
- **Trust** - which sellers dominate results, and therefore where shoppers already feel safe buying.
- **Saturation** - which claims every competitor and roundup repeats, and so which ones are now worn out.
- **Questions** - what people are still unsure about, which reveals objections, angles and even new avatars.

## When to use it
- Early in research for any category with meaningful search volume.
- Before choosing lead claims, to avoid the ones everyone already uses.
- When planning advertorials or product-aware ads, which lean on the questions shoppers ask while comparing.

## How (steps)
1. **See what your customer sees.** Browse through a VPN located in your selling country, in a private window,, so personal history does not skew results.
2. **Run three kinds of search:** your own brand and product (including press about you); category articles from third-party publications; and the "best [product]" query shoppers type.
3. **Read the results as a trust map.** Note which sites dominate - the category's best-known names, large retailers, marketplaces. Log that asymmetry in your objections list ("why buy from an unknown brand instead of a name I know?"), not only in competitor notes.
4. **Open the top roundups.** Copy why each "best overall" pick wins and log those reasons as competitor claims.
5. **Keep a buzzword tally.** Every time a phrase recurs across roundups and competitor pages, add a tick beside it. High-tick phrases are saturated: avoid them as lead claims or give them a genuinely new mechanism.
6. **Turn roundup headings into draft personas.** Headings like "top pick under $50", "worth the splurge", "ideal for fine hair" or "most compact" - each heading is a different buyer with a different motivation. Note each one's price and why the writer chose it. Order them roughly: everyday baseline buyer first, premium-experience buyer next, specialist or professional users last. A use case is not a persona until you know what drives that buyer.
7. **Mine questions with free question tools.** Set region and language, enter the category seed term and export the results (the visual wheel is hard to read; a spreadsheet is reusable in AI workflows). They cluster what people type by its opening word (why, can, which, how) and by connecting words (with, without, for), often showing volume. Some also offer marketplace mode (highest purchase intent), video mode and hashtag mode.
8. **Treat every question as a possible angle or objection.** Sort by volume. Questions about a feature you have become claims. Heavy comparison with a neighbouring activity hints at an adjacent avatar. "How to choose the right X" can become a long-form video or advertorial. If a question puzzles you, that is a gap in your own research.
9. **Check dates.** Rankings in roundups more than about two years old are stale, though their segmentation logic usually still holds.

## Example
Say you sell pickleball paddles. Question tools show people asking about edgeless designs, holes in the face, weight, getting wet, travel, spin, beginner choices and "which should I buy". Each implies an angle: a spin question becomes a texture-mechanism claim, a weight question becomes a "light enough for beginners" message. A cluster of questions comparing pickleball with tennis points to a new avatar - tennis players who have not switched yet.

Now imagine a mid-priced standing-desk mat. Search results are dominated by a famous premium brand and big retailers. Roundups repeat "comfortable" and "non-slip" so often that both earn a dozen ticks - neither should lead your ads. Comparing yourself directly with the premium leader may also backfire: shoppers wonder how you can match it at a fraction of the price. Anticipate that disbelief with a mechanism or proof. A review admitting nervousness about ordering from little-known websites might even become an angle about restoring trust in buying online.

## Common mistakes
- Searching from your own location and account history instead of the customer's.
- Copying phrases without tallying them, so you never build a saturation map.
- Naming roundup segments and then never analysing the buyer behind each one.
- Treating a big-name trust gap as mere competitor data instead of an objection to answer.
- Ignoring technical or odd questions because they seem minor.
