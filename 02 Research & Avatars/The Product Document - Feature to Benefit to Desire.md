# The Product Document - Feature to Benefit to Desire

## What it is
The product document is a short worksheet that forces you to connect what the product *has* to why anyone would *want* it. Each row runs feature -> benefit -> desire, and the document ends with a search for mechanisms and hidden selling points. It sits alongside the research document and feeds angles, hooks and landing-page copy. Everything from the benefits onward is labelled "assumed" until customer research confirms it.

## When to use it
- After the first product walkthrough in [[Understanding Your Product - Physical vs Functional Value]] and before market research.
- When a product page lists specs but never says why they matter.
- When you need a quick read on market sophistication before choosing an angle.
- If you want an AI assistant to help fill it in, see [[Filling the Product Document With AI]].

## How (steps)
1. **Basics.** Name the product and note its price, including any crossed-out comparison price.
2. **Product in action.** Explain what happens when it is used - the steps, the approach and the result. Then rewrite that description so a twelve-year-old could follow it and run it through a readability checker. Most shoppers read at roughly a sixth-grade level, and owners drift into jargon only insiders understand.
3. **Features.** Copy the key features, materials and parts from the product page. Research any part you do not recognise and confirm it. Drop anything you cannot explain or that seems irrelevant.
4. **Assumed benefits.** Against every feature, ask what it actually does for the buyer. One feature often supports several benefits, so give each its own row.
5. **Assumed desires.** Against every benefit, ask why anyone would care. Capture the functional desire and, where it exists, a social one (avoiding embarrassment, being admired, expressing personality). A single benefit often serves more than one desire.
6. **Assumed new mechanisms.** List what is genuinely unique. Keep the word "assumed" - founders often believe something is unique when competitors have it too. While here, check whether your differentiators actually appear on your own product page.
7. **Hidden-mechanism probes.** Work through four prompts:
   - Something it does that your marketing never mentions.
   - Parts or ingredients you use that rivals skip.
   - The "complaints" customers would have if it worked almost too well.
   - The way an ordinary customer would explain it to a mate. Ask a real friend or customer if you can. Casual, slightly hedged wording ("not sure if it's the fabric or just the fit, but...") reads as honest and is reusable as ad language.
8. **Sophistication check.** List every competitor selling the same thing, plus every business solving the same problem a different way. From that, judge how saturated the market is with similar promises, which decides whether you lead with a plain claim, a bigger claim, a mechanism or identity (see [[Market Sophistication Stages]]).
9. **Rank the rows.** Each desire is a possible route to a different avatar. Decide which one to lead with first and flag any immediate fixes, such as an unadvertised differentiator.

## Example
Say you sell a dog harness. Features: a heavy-duty clasp, straps that adjust, a chest-mounted lead clip and a custom name tag. Benefits: the dog cannot slip out, the fit is right, less pulling, the dog is identifiable if lost. Desires: the dog's safety, control on walks, avoiding embarrassment in public, never losing the dog. Probe answers: the "too good" complaints might be "my dog is obsessed with walks now" or "everyone stops me to ask where I got it" - both make playful ad hooks and social proof. The unadvertised finding: the clasp is the same kind trusted in tactical and rescue gear, which becomes an action item for the product page. The exercise also throws up an ad idea - a story about the name tag bringing a lost dog home - worth checking with real customers to see whether anything like it has happened.

A face cream works the same way: ingredients -> what each one does -> the overall results (soft all day, calmer skin, non-greasy) -> desires (confidence, relief, looking younger, an easier routine) -> mechanisms (a base oil close to the skin's own, a short ingredient list, one product replacing several).

## Common mistakes
- Guessing at materials or specifications instead of checking.
- Writing the "product in action" section in jargon the owner understands and the buyer does not.
- Stopping at benefits. Benefits explain the product; desires explain the purchase.
- Mapping each benefit to only one desire, which hides angles.
- Treating assumed mechanisms as facts before checking competitors.
- Accepting a first AI pass wholesale instead of comparing it with your own answer and editing.
