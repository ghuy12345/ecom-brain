# Review Mining - Your Reviews and Competitors' One-Star Reviews

## What it is
Reviews are the most concentrated customer language you can get. Two very different mining jobs use them:
- **Your own positive reviews** reveal the slang, inside jokes, identity statements and emotional phrases happy buyers use - raw material for hooks, headlines and stories.
- **Direct competitors' one-star reviews** reveal people who believed the category promise, bought, and were let down. What stops them now is a conviction ("products like this never work"), and your job is to find the offer and message that breaks it.

Marketplace-specific research (price tiers, customer questions) has its own note: [[Amazon Research - Price Ladder, Bad Reviews and Questions]].

## When to use it
- Early in research, alongside desire hunting.
- Whenever you need fresh hooks or a story to build an ad around.
- When targeting solution-aware or product-aware buyers who have already tried a competitor.

## How (steps)
**Part A - your own reviews**
1. **Export only four- and five-star reviews** and strip irrelevant columns (dates, emails, author names, IDs). Check the file for personal data before loading it into any tool.
2. **Sample by length, not at random.** Add a column with the character length of each review, sort descending and keep the top 200-300. Long reviews carry specific, emotional, unusual wording; "great, works well" is noise. If too few pass 500 characters, drop the bar to about 400. Split by country where markets talk differently.
3. **Run an AI pass for phrases.** Ask for around fifteen pieces of slang, inside jokes, niche vocabulary, emotional language and nerve-hitting statements, ranked by impact and relatability, plus a summary of themes. Ask follow-ups in the same conversation to get more.
4. **Read and judge yourself.** Expect mixed quality - three or four keepers from fifteen is a decent haul. The tool saves time; it rarely finds a line that careful reading would have missed.
5. **Verify every quote** exists in the actual reviews before using it; models paraphrase.
6. **Look for story starters.** A review pasted straight onto an ad rarely works because the quote alone is not interesting. Pull out the single unexpected angle hiding in it and let that phrase anchor the story.

**Part B - competitors' one-star reviews**
1. **Gather the lowest-rated reviews of your direct rivals**, mostly from marketplaces.
2. **Look beyond "it didn't work" for the reason** and sort it: product defect, durability, design annoyance, wrong or late shipment, poor service, or expectation gap. Product and service failures imply different messaging.
3. **Name the belief** each reviewer now holds about the whole category. One bad purchase makes burned buyers distrust the whole category.
4. **Answer the most common failure with part of your offer**, not just a claim - for example, a no-questions replacement if anything fails. Make the protection visible without advertising the underlying weakness.
5. **Check the business can honour it** (margin, return rate) before promising it.
6. **Log findings** in the research document under bad direct-competitor reviews and point the resulting messaging at solution- and product-aware audiences.

## Example
Say you sell a minimalist emergency multi-tool worn as a bracelet. Long five-star reviews throw up "I'm not a jewellery person but I never take this off", "it's like insurance you wear" and "dull blades are the dangerous ones". Each is a headline seed: identity ("not a jewellery person"), preparedness, and a counter-intuitive safety belief.

A vacuum review that says the product "gave me my garage back" after a rodent problem is a story starter: "Rats moved into my garage - this is how I reclaimed it." The valuable part was that one small phrase, not the whole review.

On the competitor side, imagine one-star reviews of a premium pet water fountain citing a pump that dies, a bowl that scratches after a month, a wrong item shipped and an awkward cord. The finding is not "competitors break"; it is that burned buyers believe premium fountains are not worth the risk. The response is a visible replacement guarantee plus service reassurance.

## Common mistakes
- Skipping review preparation and feeding thousands of short reviews to a tool.
- Using a phrase without confirming it appears in a real review.
- Forgetting that filtering to four and five stars removes complaint language - mine low-star reviews and forums separately for objections.
- Stopping at "it didn't work" without digging for the cause.
- Offering replacements your margins cannot cover.
- Treating length as quality - a short review can still contain the perfect line.
