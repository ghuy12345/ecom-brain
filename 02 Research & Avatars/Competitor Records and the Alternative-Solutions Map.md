# Competitor Records and the Alternative-Solutions Map

## What it is
Two structured artefacts that live in your research document:
- **The competitor record** - one entry per competitor with identical fields, so offers, prices and pages can be compared side by side. It replaces a pile of screenshots.
- **The alternative-solutions map** - a list of everything else your market already does to chase the main desire (hacks, neighbouring products, routines, DIY fixes, pricey extras), plus why each still leaves the problem unsolved.

Strategy - deciding what the records mean for your positioning - is in [[Competitor Research - White Space and Weaknesses]].

## When to use it
- During competitor research for any new brand or product.
- Before building a landing page or offer, so you know the stack you are being compared against.
- When you need fresh ad ideas grounded in day-to-day struggle rather than in other ads.

## How (steps)
**Building a competitor record**
1. **Source competitors widely** - from the competitors you already know, plus ones you find through search and ad libraries. Browse through a VPN in the country you advertise to, since sites, prices and ads vary by region.
2. **Pick the right product.** Many competitors sell several tiers. Log the one aimed at your buyer, not the professional model.
3. **Capture the entry pop-up offer** before you dismiss it.
4. **Find the ad destination.** Open the competitor's listing in the ad library, click the live ads (oldest first - longevity suggests they work) and record where each lands - product page, collection page or a dedicated landing page. That page, not the homepage, is the real conversion environment.
5. **Fill the fixed fields:**
   - Main website
   - Ad destination page(s)
   - Ad library link
   - Price range plus exactly what that money buys (sizes, quantities, accessories). Round it for readability and remember prices change.
   - Active offers, each item on its own line: trial periods, instalment payments, bonuses and what they are said to be worth, delivery terms, warranty, refund promise, first-order discounts. Together these form the **offer stack**.
   - Notes: claims (especially those with statistics), repeated buzzwords, positioning, page layout (how-to guides, videos of it in use, box contents, technical details, reviews, FAQs, delivery details, packaging story).
6. **Walk to checkout** and note first-customer offers and checkout type.
7. **Stick to observations.** A judgement like "feels high-end" is fine only alongside what they actually show and prove. Log competitor statistics as claims, not facts.
8. **Allow for seasonality** - what they are running might just reflect the month.

**Mapping alternative solutions**
1. List every other thing people do to get the main desire, drawing on research notes, swipe files and video content - creators who share tips effectively list the alternatives for you.
2. Trace each one to the next - expensive products lead to masks, masks lead to overnight treatments, and so on.
3. Note the cost of each in time or money, and why people still have the problem after doing it.
4. For each, choose to **ride alongside** it or **expose** its shortcomings. The lever is always that people try all this and still struggle.
5. Write down ad ideas immediately and flag the best. Research the technique properly before filming so any visual joke is accurate.

## Example
Say a brand sells a premium electric toothbrush. One rival's record lists a home trial, instalments, a bundled travel case with a quoted value, free delivery, two years of warranty cover and a refund promise, with claims backed by percentage improvements in plaque removal and whiteness. Its ads land on a product page showing the brush in use, the box contents and full specs. Takeaway: at a high price, listing benefits is not enough - showing the full contents helps justify the price.

The alternative-solutions map lists manual brushes, whitening strips, mouthwash, floss picks, water flossers and extra dentist cleanings. Two ideas emerge. A playful ad shows someone buried in strips, rinses and floss picks attempting every trick for a brighter smile, ending with "or just get a proper tool". Another highlights a spending mismatch: premium toothpaste and whitening strips, then a cheap brush - verify that buyers really do both before running it. Keep call-outs educational, not insulting ("stop using cheap junk" lands worse than explaining why).

## Common mistakes
- Logging the homepage rather than the page the ads point to.
- Noting a price but not what it buys.
- Recording impressions instead of facts.
- Taking a rival's statistics at face value.
- Mapping only direct competitors and ignoring the habits and hacks people use instead.
- Cloning a rival's page instead of understanding why it persuades.
