# Talking to Customers - Calls, Immersion and Seeding Feedback

## What it is
Forums, reviews and ad libraries tell you what strangers say. Talking to - and closely watching - your own buyers tells you how they actually arrived, what triggered the purchase and which desires they would never think to write in a review. This note covers four direct sources:
- **Customer phone calls** - short, warm conversations about the buyer's journey.
- **Customer immersion** - living in the content your customer consumes.
- **Post-purchase questions** - specific questions asked right after the sale.
- **Seeding feedback** - what creators say unprompted when they first receive the product.

Structured survey giveaways are a separate method; see [[Customer Survey Giveaways - When and How to Run One]].

## When to use it
- Once you have buyers, at any size of brand.
- When ads work but you cannot explain why - the journey usually holds the answer.
- When you suspect customers have desires they do not state in reviews.
- During a first wave of product seeding.

## How (steps)
**Phone calls**
1. **Choose who to call:** people with bigger-than-usual baskets, returning buyers and anyone who ordered recently. Phone numbers are usually in your store admin's order records; if you do not collect them, start.
2. **Open lightly:** introduce yourself and the brand and ask whether now is a bad time. Then stop talking. If they are busy, book a callback.
4. **Ask one question:** say thanks for their purchase, then ask how they ended up discovering you.
5. **Then be quiet.** Let the silence work. If they are unsure what you mean, rephrase gently and ask again. The ideal answer arrives unprompted: the trigger event, what they tried before, any shift from offline to online buying, the ad or touchpoint that convinced them, and their pains.
6. **Take notes as they talk** and file them in the research document.
7. **Follow naturally** - "what had you tried before?", "why us in the end?" - without turning it into a questionnaire.

**Immersion**
1. Watch the niche video content your customer watches, read the blogs and forums they read, and absorb their mindset. Deep familiarity with the customer, more than any tactic, is what makes marketing effective.
2. Note recurring worries, vocabulary and rituals in your research document.

**Post-purchase questions**
1. Ask specific questions, not "how did you hear about us?" alone - what nearly stopped you buying, what you tried before, what you hope will change.
2. Pair answers with comments on competitor review videos and niche forums for a fuller picture.

**Seeding feedback**
1. When the first wave of seeded creators posts, watch every video before giving further direction.
2. Log any themes, wants and benefits creators bring up without being asked. These are often desires customers hold but never say.
3. Use those findings to nudge creators who have not posted yet, and feed them into angle and brief work.

## Example
Imagine a weighted-blanket brand calling ten recent repeat buyers. One explains she bought after her partner started sleeping in another room because of her tossing and turning; she had already tried sleep apps and supplements; she found the brand through a video of someone falling asleep in minutes. In one unscripted answer you have a trigger event (relationship strain), failed alternatives, a desire (sharing a bed again) and the touchpoint that converted her. Three of the ten mention a partner - a hypothesis worth testing as an angle. Meanwhile, the first seeded creators keep mentioning that the blanket helps them stop doom-scrolling at night - a benefit nobody wrote in a review.

## Common mistakes
- Opening by asking permission to run through some questions - it makes the conversation feel like an interrogation.
- Firing off a list of questions about colours, pains and benefits; the conversation dies.
- Reading a rigid script.
- Filling silences instead of letting the customer talk.
- Over-directing seeded creators before seeing what they say naturally.
- Treating a handful of calls as proof rather than as hypotheses.
