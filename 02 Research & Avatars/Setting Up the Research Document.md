# Setting Up the Research Document

## What it is
The research document is the one file where everything you learn about a brand, its buyers and its rivals ends up. It combines a loose idea area with structured sections that fill in over time. Its purpose is retrieval: when you plan ads, write a page or brief a creator, the right quote, fact or objection should be one search away. When research wraps up, you tidy it and boil it down into a master brief that writers, creators and AI tools can reuse.

## When to use it
- At the very start of work on a new brand or product, before reading any reviews.
- Right before strategy work (angles, roadmap, avatars), when you consolidate it.

## How (steps)
1. **Set up the header.** Start a new document and add links you open constantly: website, social profiles, your ad library page, your ad performance report, your product notes (see [[Understanding Your Product - Physical vs Functional Value]]) and brand guidelines if any exist. Guidelines keep a growing team consistent, and buyers can tell when a brand has no real purpose - but they do not decide whether ads work, plenty of brands grow without them, so never hold research back to write them.
2. **Create an idea dump** for hooks, headlines, offers, awareness notes and visuals. Jot concrete shots down instantly ("someone rinsing foam out of their hair") instead of trusting memory.
3. **Add structured sections.** A workable set:
   - *Company and product* - what the brand stands for, physical and functional traits, features, benefits, desires, advantages, use cases, price range.
   - *Reviews* - yours, marketplace ones, the worst reviews of direct rivals, and reviews of indirect alternatives.
   - *Market language* - a positive phrase bank (language behind your main promise), a negative phrase bank (relatable frustrations), a buzzword tally, wants and needs, and explicit don't-wants.
   - *Core research* - problems matched with solutions, desires ranked by strength, other ways people chase the main desire, daily struggles, beliefs to address, and the jobs your marketing has to do.
   - *Customer* - who they are today and who they turn into after using the product.
   - *Avatars* - demographics only if relevant, plus the objections they raise and the fixes that failed them. Those two fields expose the belief system and are non-negotiable.
   - *FAQs* - every question research turns up.
   - *Competitors* - a block for each of five or six rivals; see [[Competitor Records and the Alternative-Solutions Map]].
   - *Creative audit* - the biggest-spending prospecting ads with the strongest acquisition cost or return, for the past month and past half-year, plus the best hook and click-through rates and what caused them.
4. **Keep two running lists open while writing.**
   - *Facts* - about the brand, the product, and the market (only the surprising ones). Concrete facts let readers judge quality themselves ("made with titanium" beats "high quality").
   - *Objections* - about the product, about each angle's framing, about each claim, and rooted in existing beliefs. The belief bucket feeds the other three.
5. **Fill it gradually.** Treat the sections as a guide. Add what your product needs - a "where we're weaker" list beside the advantages is useful.
6. **Consolidate before strategy.** Ten to fifteen minutes: merge duplicates, pull price range out, finish the creative audit, tidy the before/after customer section. Move missing FAQs onto product pages and treat each as an ad idea.
7. **Condense into a master brief:** target market and main awareness level; pains split into physical, emotional, social, financial and time; vivid hopes; self-image and labels they accept or reject; core promises ("get X without Y"); objections with the belief behind each and your answer; existing solutions and why they failed; competitor landscape and white space; the chosen mechanism; copy rules (tone, voice, roughly a seventh-to-eighth-grade reading level, words to use and avoid). Open every AI copy session by pasting the whole brief rather than hoping the tool remembers.

## Example
Say you are researching a hair-care brand. At first the file holds little more than links and product notes. A week later it has 60 review quotes, a tally where "frizz" appears 23 times and "smooth" 18, a negative phrase bank full of humidity complaints, and an avatar note that many buyers had tried salon treatments that faded within weeks. That failed-solutions note later grounds a mechanism angle, and the tally settles headline vocabulary.

## Common mistakes
- Trying to fill every section in one sitting, then abandoning the file.
- Paraphrasing quotes into your own words instead of storing them verbatim.
- Recording demographics in avatar sections but skipping objections and failed solutions.
- Never consolidating, so research exists but cannot be found.
- Letting brand guidelines delay the actual research.
