# Amazon Research - Price Ladder, Bad Reviews and Questions

## What it is
A big marketplace is more than a list of competitors. It shows the full **price ladder** of a category - the full run of competing prices, cheapest to most luxurious - and what buyers on each rung actually pay for. It also exposes thousands of reviews and buyer questions you will never see on a brand's own site. Mined properly, it tells you how to film, which objections to answer, which features shoppers now expect as standard, and how sophisticated the market is.

## When to use it
- When your product sits in a category with many marketplace listings, especially if your price sits well above the typical shopper's.
- Before writing problem-aware ads that contrast you with cheap alternatives.
- When building or auditing product-page FAQs.

## How (steps)
1. **Map three tiers.** Search the category and deliberately note products below you (budget), alongside you (similar price) and above you (premium). Write down each tier's price and how many times more expensive each step is; the top tier can cost ten times the bottom.
2. **Profile each tier.** For each, record problems, pros, cons, usage scenarios and the buzzwords in listing copy. A review-summarising browser extension can summarise the main pros, cons, reasons for buying and situations of use across thousands of reviews in minutes. Treat its percentages as directional, not exact.
3. **Go for volume.** Run the analysis on the number one listing and on a mid-ranked one - you want the largest sample, not the best rank.
4. **Use usage scenarios to plan filming.** If the typical buyer uses it in one room straight after one daily habit, film there.
5. **Mine cheap-product failures.** List how budget alternatives break (overheats, catches hair, smells of burning, dies within weeks, feels flimsy). Tag each as ad-ready problem-aware material and log it under bad comparator reviews.
6. **Group buyer questions into themes** (fit with other gear, how heavy it is, wattage, plug type, safety marks, extras) and count each theme.
7. **Read the sophistication signal.** If questions cluster on specs and technical features rather than outcomes, the market already accepts the outcome and is shopping for the mechanism that delivers it. Lead with mechanism, not bare promises.
8. **Note feature expectations.** Cheap products set the baseline. When a premium product lacks something the cheap ones all have, shoppers raise it as an objection - and marketplaces surface this where your own site never will.
9. **Read your own listing** and check whether your copy simply repeats everyone else's buzzwords.
10. **Act on it.** Put answers to the common questions on the product page and work them into ads, technical ones included. Use competitor imagery for creative inspiration.

## Example
Imagine you sell a premium hair dryer. Reviews of budget dryers praise how cheap they are - their buyers' motivation is low risk ("if it breaks, I've lost very little"). So "great value" in your copy slides right past that shopper, who sees your price as expensive. Your job with that segment becomes education: cheap dryers may cost more over time through heat damage and replacement. Treat that as an assumption to validate.

The same research might show that "lightweight" is the most-cited pro in the category while every ad leads on power - a gap worth testing. The nearest-priced competitor's top con, "slow drying", is an opening. The question tally reveals many buyers asking about voltage and whether a cool-shot button is included; if yours lacks one, that is an objection to handle. And if most reviewers use their dryer in a small bathroom straight after showering, that is where your ad is filmed.

## Common mistakes
- Looking only at listings priced like yours and missing what budget and luxury buyers reveal.
- Assuming a phrase like "great value" means the same thing at every price rung.
- Assuming power is why people buy without checking what reviewers actually praise.
- Waving away technical questions as unimportant when many people ask them.
- Treating a single summary tool's numbers as exact.
- Keeping FAQs in the research document and never putting them on the product page.
