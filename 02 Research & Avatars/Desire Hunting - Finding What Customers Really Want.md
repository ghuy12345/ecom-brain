# Desire Hunting - Finding What Customers Really Want

## What it is
Desire hunting is the focused, hands-on job of combing the web for what people crave and the problems they want gone. The output is a sheet of desires stated in the customers' own words, backed by quotes, plus rough ad ideas. It is deliberately not fully automated: the best ideas tend to be small details buried deep in a thread or a long comment, and doing the reading yourself deepens your feel for the buyer - which later shows up in every hook and headline you write.

Finding a desire is only half the work. The market also has to believe you can deliver it, which is where [[Market Sophistication Stages]] comes in.

## When to use it
- After the product document, before choosing angles or avatars.
- Whenever ads feel generic or copy "does not land".
- Periodically on running brands - research is ongoing, and re-reading old notes a day later surfaces things you missed.

## How (steps)
1. **Set up a desire sheet** with four columns: desires (plain "I want..." and "I don't want..." statements), verbatim language snippets, review snippets, and a parking lot for weak ideas.
2. **Hunt in a rough order of richness:** forums such as Reddit, then YouTube videos and especially their comments, then TikTok, then marketplace and on-site reviews, then question-aggregator tools. On forums, type the simple question a buyer would ask and open the threads with the most replies.
3. **Read the signals.** Upvotes show what resonates; long comments mean you have hit a rich vein; post dates tell you whether a belief is still current.
4. **Ladder every complaint.** Ask "why is that a problem?" to reach the desire, then "why does that matter?" to reach the instinct (health, status, control, belonging, safety). One complaint can fork: joint pain while lifting leads to health *and* to status through personal records. Treat the deeper layers as guesses until research backs them up.
5. **Correct your vocabulary.** Re-read each quote and replace your wording with theirs. Search your source text for the word you chose; if nobody used "hydrated" but dozens said "smoother" or "less dry", use theirs.
6. **Capture side intelligence:** competitors and products people name (someone converted them), ingredient or feature knowledge they already have (a read on sophistication), beliefs about timelines ("it takes three months to work"), and who it does not work for.
7. **Use AI as a helper, not a replacement.** If laddering stalls, paste raw comments into an assistant, explain what you mean by mass desire, and request surface wants, the core desire and the instinct behind each. For deep-research runs, do your manual reading first or in parallel - see [[AI Market Research With Live Web Access]].
8. **Pull it together.** Note which instinct keeps coming up, then immediately ask whether the market has heard it a hundred times. A desire every competitor sells is saturated; look across adjacent categories for under-discussed benefits.

## Example
Imagine a collagen supplement. Collagen's results are invisible on camera, so buyers invent their own proof. A forum comment mentions trimming nails far more often since starting it. That proxy becomes a testable angle: "If your nails aren't growing faster, your collagen might not be working." It lets viewers self-diagnose, gives new hope to people who think the category failed them, and opens a pool of past users who blamed a brand rather than the ingredient. Compare that with a no-pull dog harness, where the proof is simply filmed.

The same research might show complaint themes - huge tablets, bad taste, bloating, price, scepticism that it is "just protein". Each is a technical problem desire: "the collagen that doesn't make you gassy", smaller capsules, a plausible explanation for sceptics. In an audience going through hormonal change, the comments might ladder up to wanting to feel like their old self, with sub-desires such as feeling normal, being in control of my body and enjoying life rather than enduring it. If every brand already says "take control", that word is saturated and you need a sharper entry point.

## Common mistakes
- Automating everything and never reading a thread yourself.
- Labelling desires with words the market never uses.
- Treating one dramatic comment as truth rather than a hypothesis to test.
- Stopping at the surface want and never reaching the instinct.
- Confusing "found a desire" with "found a winning angle" - believability still has to be solved.
- Making claims the research cannot support. Hedge ("may", "might"), check any physiology you cite and never fake visual proof.
