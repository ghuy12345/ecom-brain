# Reverse-Engineering the Avatar From a Winning Ad

## What it is
Every ad that wins is speaking to *someone*. Reverse-engineering means tracing a winner - yours or a competitor's - to the angle it uses, the desire underneath, the behaviour or experience the viewer has, and therefore the person it actually reached. That inference becomes a **hypothesis**: an educated guess about why the ad works. A hypothesis earns its keep only if it leads to the next winner.

Saying a winner worked because of a good hook teaches you nothing. Explaining it as "this reached people who already tried X and want Y" tells you what to make next. Line-by-line teardown mechanics are in [[Winning Video Ad Teardown - Line by Line]]; this note is about the avatar.

## When to use it
- When one ad starts pulling most of the spend in an account - especially a **super winner** (sometimes called a breakthrough), which takes most of the budget *and* raises return, allowing budget to grow.
- When studying a long-running competitor ad.
- After a multi-angle test, to decide where to go deeper.

## How (steps)
1. **List the chain for the ad:** angle -> possible desire -> possible behaviour or product experience -> alternative, narrower readings -> the likeliest person.
2. **Read the comments and responders.** Who is actually engaging, and what do they talk about? Revise your reading if the audience differs from the one you aimed at.
3. **Ladder the desire.** Keep asking "why does that matter?" until a surface want turns into a core one. Remember the product can only credibly satisfy so many of the desires you uncover.
4. **Note technology-problem desires.** A complaint about how products in the category are built (plastic parts, noise, mess) often creates a new desire (a version without that flaw). A feature-led, new-mechanism ad still targets a person - read who.
5. **Write the hypothesis** in one or two sentences and log it with the ad.
6. **Check how the angle was born.** If it came from research and the ad failed, suspect execution before abandoning the angle. If it was based on a product feature, a failure is ambiguous - the feature itself may simply not matter to anyone.
7. **Scale with the breadth-to-depth sequence:**
   - *Breadth:* test several angles at once (three is a workable start). A direction emerges.
   - *Quick depth:* make short-term iterations on the winning angle in the same format - new images, new subtext - for a fast spend lift.
   - *Real depth:* spend a week or so researching *why* people care about the winning angle or mechanism, then make a leap into a richer format (for example a voiceover story video that gives more context). This step often unlocks more scale than quick iterations, yet it is the one most often skipped.
8. **Do not over-analyse.** Save deep breakdowns for the ads that matter - focus on clear winners and super winners. Expect the first month or two of hypotheses to feel uncertain.

## Example
Imagine a water-bottle brand running three angles: "designed by a dietitian", "easy to clean", and "no plastic touching your water". The plastic-free angle wins most of the spend. Reverse-engineering it:
- Angle: a bottle without plastic in the water path.
- Laddered desire: no plastic in the air -> no microplastics in my lungs -> I want to be healthier.
- Behaviour / experience: people who have already swapped plastic bottles and containers and read about microplastics.
- Likeliest avatar: health-conscious buyers actively removing plastic from their home.
- Hypothesis: the ad wins because it extends a swap these people are already making.

The "designed by a dietitian" angle - chosen because the product had it - might matter far less than expected. You would first iterate the winning static with new images (a modest lift), then research why this group fears microplastics and make a voiceover story ad walking through a parent's decision to clear plastic out of the kitchen. That richer format could scale further than the static.

## Common mistakes
- Copying a competitor winner without understanding who it speaks to - it brings short-lived luck at best.
- Crediting surface elements (hook, colours, music) instead of avatar and desire.
- Building angles around features instead of around people.
- Settling for small iterations and skipping the research that enables a bigger format jump.
- Deciding an angle is dead when the real problem may have been how it was made.
- Testing blindly, with no written hypothesis to confirm or reject.
