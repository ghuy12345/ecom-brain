# Avatar Fundamentals - Beyond Demographics

## What it is
An avatar (or persona) is a particular kind of buyer whose situation gives a problem or desire its own flavour. A useful one covers the product, who needs it, the problems in their way, the beliefs they hold and the fixes they have tried before. "Women 35+" or "busy mums" are demographics, not avatars - they say nothing about why someone would stop scrolling.

Avatar depth matters more as you grow. On modern ad platforms the creative itself does much of the targeting, so audience settings can stay broad while the *ad* does the selecting. The more precisely an ad speaks to one person's situation, the better the right people find it. Many brands that stall at a spend plateau are not short of budget; they are short of message specificity and conversion rate.

## When to use it
- Before writing any ad, landing page or creator brief.
- When research has produced a long list of possible personas and you must pick a few.
- When scaling stalls and new avatars are needed to unlock new pockets of demand.

## How (steps)
1. **Start from the desire, not the demographic.** Begin with the people you can genuinely help and how, rather than with a product looking for buyers.
2. **Choose the condition before the persona.** The condition is what the prospect wants to gain or get rid of (for a collagen product, that might be younger-looking skin, digestion or thicker hair). Choose it based on claims you can already back up, how strongly it ties to a mass desire, whether it is topical, and how mature, funded and risk-tolerant the account is.
3. **Then list personas** who experience that condition in different ways, and flesh each out by asking about their typical day, their fears, the failed fixes behind them and the outcome they are really after. Add the labels people use for themselves ("this is so me" in comments is a goldmine) and, where useful, their emotional state - struggling, motivated, or close to giving up.
4. **Score each persona on urgency and stakes.**
   - *Urgency:* must they solve it next year, next month or right now?
   - *Stakes:* would solving it be nice, impactful or life-changing?
   High on both = high leverage. Low on either = likely to convert poorly, even if it is a valid audience.
5. **Name and define the winners:** a memorable name, age, gender, the main problem or goal, and the deeper want underneath.
6. **Go narrow, then broaden.** A narrow avatar is a smaller market but resonates harder, so it usually scales better at first. Widen it as the brand becomes better known.
7. **Decide how many to run.** This depends on stage and execution:
   - Small brands: one core avatar, so learnings compound. Many brands stay on one well-executed core well into seven figures a month.
   - Growing brands: add a second or third primary once execution is strong and the first avatar's market starts limiting growth - rarely more than one to five in focus at once. Stage-by-stage guidance is in [[Building Core Avatars From Desires]].
   - Even with several avatars, let the platform sort audiences inside one structure and only split campaigns by avatar at very high spend.
   - Around big sale periods, stay with your one to three proven primaries.
8. **Keep testing new avatars** through tight feedback loops; each ad that wins tells you which avatar it actually reached (see [[Reverse-Engineering the Avatar From a Winning Ad]]).

## Example
Take a hair-growth supplement. Persona A: a self-assured woman in her mid-twenties who is happy with her hair but would like a bit more body - nice-to-have stakes, low urgency, low leverage. Persona B: a mother in her thirties losing hair after giving birth, who feels her confidence and sense of self slipping - life-altering stakes, needed now - high leverage. Both are valid; only one deserves early budget.

Or take a chicken product that is hard to overcook. A beginner cook who has given up on chicken after embarrassing dinners and an experienced chef seeking flavour are different avatars with opposite headlines - write to one at a time.

## Common mistakes
- Using demographics as personas.
- Picking a persona before deciding the condition.
- Pouring budget into big segments that would merely like a fix someday.
- Running many avatars at once too early, so no learning compounds.
- Splitting campaigns by avatar at small spend instead of letting the creative do the targeting.
- Responding to a plateau with bigger budgets instead of sharper avatars and better conversion.
- Treating an underperforming avatar as dead without checking the three usual causes: thin research, weak execution, or a product that genuinely cannot bridge to that problem.
