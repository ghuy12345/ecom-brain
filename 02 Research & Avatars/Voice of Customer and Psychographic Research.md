# Voice of Customer and Psychographic Research

## What it is
Psychographic research maps what goes on inside the buyer's head: their values, fears, hopes, identity, the forces they blame and the language they use. Voice-of-customer (VOC) mining is the collection side - gathering what customers literally say so your copy can say it back to them. Together they answer two questions you should settle before writing anything: *who is this for* and *why do they care*.

Copy that converts sounds as though the prospect wrote it about themselves. That cannot be done from assumption; once the research exists, the writing becomes far easier.

## When to use it
- Before writing headlines, hooks, landing pages or creator briefs.
- When defining or refreshing an avatar.
- When copy is technically correct but flat - usually a sign it is in marketing language instead of customer language.

## How (steps)
1. **Collect 50-100 verbatim quotes** from forums, marketplace reviews (yours and competitors', with special attention to two- and three-star reviews, which are often the most balanced), video and social comments, groups, Q&A sites, and success or horror stories.
2. **Tag what each quote reveals:** the words they use for the problem and for the outcome they want, past failures, and the emotional weight of the problem.
3. **Group pain points** into physical, emotional, social, financial and time-related. The more specific the pain, the more it resonates.
4. **Map objections** against the usual suspects: doubt it suits them, been burned by similar products, price, lack of time, suspicion it is too good to be true, wanting to mull it over, wanting someone else's opinion, and expecting to find it cheaper elsewhere. Record the exact online phrasing that signals each one and the belief behind it.
5. **Run a structured deep-dive question set** (manually or with a deep-research AI) covering:
   - *Demographics and worldview* - attitudes, hopes, wins and setbacks, who or what they blame, biases, and their core beliefs summed up briefly. Unlocks tone, register and which authorities they trust.
   - *Existing solutions* - what they currently use, its pros and cons, bad experiences, and whether they trust it to work. Unlocks the mechanism of the problem, oversaturated angles and the trust gap.
   - *Curiosity* - unusual past attempts, forgotten older remedies, a hidden reason old solutions failed. Unlocks hooks that feel exclusive.
   - *Corruption* - the sense that the problem is newer or worse than it used to be, what outside forces made it so, and a group spared because it avoids those forces. Unlocks an outside villain the buyer already half-blames.
6. **Define the avatar by desire, then sharpen.** Start from the desire, then add experiences and behaviours (which expose beliefs and pains), then emotions, then demographics only if they matter.
7. **Answer six questions before any headline** - the reader, their desire, their experience, the feeling it left, what they did about it, and why it matters to them. Once written, re-check that the line speaks to that reader and that they would care.
8. **Write the output into your brief** - pains, hopes, how they see themselves, words to use or avoid, core promises, top objections and the existing fixes that fall short (see [[Setting Up the Research Document]]).

## Example
Take a juicier, higher-fat chicken cut that stays moist even when overcooked. For a beginner home cook, the desire is to impress the family; the experience is dry, overcooked chicken; the emotion is embarrassment; the behaviour is avoiding cooking chicken; the belief is "I can't cook chicken". The headline writes itself: "Never overcook chicken again." For a professional chef, the desire is a remarkable flavour experience, so "the wagyu of chicken" fits. Swap them and both fail - the chef does not care about overcooking, and the novice is intimidated by luxury framing. (This pair is illustrative, not drawn from research.)

On language: a reviewer complains about pouring money into products that never delivered. Do not polish that into "Tired of ineffective solutions?" Keep it close to how they said it: "You've already thrown money at stuff that didn't work. Here's why this is different."

## Common mistakes
- Building avatars from demographics alone.
- Polishing customer phrasing into corporate copy.
- Listing pains vaguely ("they feel bad") instead of specifically.
- Ignoring objections until the landing page, when they should shape the angle itself.
- Assuming one headline can serve several avatars - a line that pulls one in can push another away.
