# International Expansion and Worldwide Targeting

## What it is
Expansion can mean three different things: letting a worldwide campaign find which countries respond, deliberately launching from a small home market into a much bigger one (often the US), or growing a small home market through more products. Each needs different preparation. Breaking into a new market is usually harder than selling a new product where you already operate, so expansion should be a deliberate choice, not an escape from a plateau.

## When to use it
- A worldwide or multi-country campaign shows unexpected traction somewhere.
- Your home market is small and you have proven, durable winners.
- Effort to test a new country is small (duplicate a campaign) and the downside is limited.

## How (steps)
1. **Grow at home first in small markets.** Adding products for your existing avatar usually beats chasing new avatars or countries.
2. **Discover with worldwide targeting.** A worldwide campaign, with exclusions for countries you cannot serve, shows which markets respond. Prune it to profitable countries over time. When one country stands out, give it its own campaign, a localised site and ads written in its language.
3. **Localise the whole journey.** Ads in one language landing on a site in another convert poorly. Translate winning ads and pages using native speakers or, at minimum, a native proofreader. Match accents too: some accents transfer between markets better than others, so re-voice before writing off a winner.
4. **Treat a large market as a second business.** Copied campaigns, converted prices, home-market shipping and split focus are how brands fail in the US. Rough readiness signals: meaningful home revenue (often several million a year) and several winning ads with large lifetime spend. Launch one new country at a time.
5. **Model the economics.** Larger markets often have much higher CPMs. CAC = CPC / CVR and CPC = CPM x CTR, so doubled CPMs at the same CTR and CVR double CAC. The answer is usually pricing rather than conversion tweaks: price for the new market's expectations. Model first-purchase gross profit, including shipping, fulfilment, returns (often higher) and tariffs.
6. **Read as a local brand.** Shoppers worry about customs, shipping time and currency. Show local delivery times, hold local inventory where possible and avoid advertising foreignness.
7. **Watch cheaper regions closely.** Regions with much lower CPMs can be attractive, but payment methods (local wallets, cash on delivery), purchasing power, AOV and customs are what actually limit you.
8. **Settle tax and entity questions first.** Your store platform may not warn you about every country's tax threshold; check each region and consider registering for VAT early. US sales tax varies by state, and heavy spend concentrated in one state can cross its threshold. Low-tax jurisdictions have residency and entity conditions; get professional advice before acting. Tariff changes are a real risk; plan with your logistics partner.
9. **Mind seasonality** in opposite hemispheres; start with products that sell year-round.
10. **Expect cash strain:** duplicate inventory, lower early profit, more creative spend and diluted focus.

## Example
Say a UK skincare brand's worldwide campaign shows Germany and Australia profitable. Australia shares the language and accent, so it gets a dedicated campaign with local pricing and delivery times within a month. Germany waits until ads and pages are translated by a native speaker. The US, where CPMs would be roughly double, waits until the brand has more proven winners and a US warehouse plan.

## Common mistakes
- Opening many countries at once and multiplying logistics and tax work.
- Converting prices instead of re-pricing.
- Ads and site in different languages.
- Expanding abroad to escape a plateau at home.
