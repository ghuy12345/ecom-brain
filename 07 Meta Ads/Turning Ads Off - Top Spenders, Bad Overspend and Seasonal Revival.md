# Turning Ads Off - Top Spenders, Bad Overspend and Seasonal Revival

## What it is
Meta's delivery chases the largest number of conversions at the lowest cost; ROAS is not its goal. So its top-spending ad often shows a lower ROAS than a small ad, yet it is usually the one bringing in the most purchases and doing the prospecting that feeds everything else. Turning it off because its ROAS looks mediocre is one of the most common ways to destabilise an account. At the same time, a genuinely bad ad can win the auction with cheap clicks and burn a large budget overnight. This note covers both sides.

## When to use it
- Whenever you are about to switch off an ad with significant spend.
- When a new ad grabs most of the budget at a poor return.
- When planning seasonal periods where old winners may come back.

## How (steps)
1. **Use frequency to see where in the funnel the ad sits.** Around 1-2 suggests mostly prospecting; about 3+ with pricier clicks suggests heavy retargeting. Turn off a prospecting ad and the retargeting it was feeding dries up too.
2. **Check click-based versus view-through data** before trusting its ROAS.
3. **Look at the account before the ad.** With the account at KPI, individual ads swinging is expected.
4. **Ask the counterfactual question:** if you shifted this ad's budget to your other top ads, would they return more? Usually not: those ads are small because delivery cannot find more buyers for them, and a high-ROAS ad on small spend rarely survives three times the budget. Occasionally yes: 4.6x on $2,500 against 1.7x on $3,900 favours the first.
5. **Do not assume fatigue.** Some ads run profitably for many months. Don't remove a tiring former winner until something better exists.
6. **Leave the unspent tail alone.** Allow roughly three times target CPA of daily spend per ad set. Ads that get no spend cost nothing, so leave them running rather than switching them off or forcing budget onto them (see [[Campaign Types - An Account Map]]).
7. **Use spending limits on large accounts.** At roughly $10k+/day, give new test ad sets a daily cap you could afford to lose (for example $500), and remove it once the ad proves itself. Meta blocks some first-day kill rules on campaign-budget campaigns, so a spending limit is the practical safeguard.
8. **Diagnose clickbait.** Add a landing page views column beside link clicks; a wide gap means people click and bounce, the signature of an ad winning cheap, low-intent clicks.
9. **Prepare seasonal revivals ahead.** Flag past seasonal winners in your log and switch them back on a little before their season starts, holding everything else steady. Treat it as a cheap option, not a strategy.

## Decision rules
**Define "top spender" by spend level,** not by gut feel: for example the ads in your top 1% or top 5% of spend over the period. These are the ads the rules below protect most carefully.

- **Within KPI: leave it on.** As a general rule, an ad that stays within KPI keeps running, even if a newer ad shows a higher ROAS on smaller spend.
- **A dip of about a week: do not turn it off.** Short dips are normal swings; check the account as a whole and wait.
- **Consistently over KPI for a couple of weeks: reduce budget first.** Lower its spend (for example by moving it to a smaller fixed budget) before cutting it, and see whether it recovers at the lower level. Decide in advance how far over target you will tolerate.
- **CPA spiking plus signs of creative fatigue (rising frequency, falling click-through): cut it.**
- **Drastically over KPI: cut it.** This includes a new ad grabbing most of the spend far below target. Below about $10-20k/day, allow a day or two for lagged conversions; above that, use rules to stop the burn. Also cut when the counterfactual check or a clickbait gap clearly argues for it.

## Example
Say your target is 1.5x. Ad A is in your top 1% of spend at 1.6x with frequency of 1.3: within KPI, so it stays on. Ad B, new, has run 0.8x on heavy spend for three days: drastically over KPI, so you turn it off and its budget flows to ads running 1.8-3.0x. Ad C, a long-running top spender, drops to 1.3x for five days: a dip, so you leave it. Ad D has sat around 1.3x for two weeks, so you cut its budget by half first; when its CPA then spikes alongside rising frequency and falling click-through, you cut it. In May, last summer's best static is flagged in the log and switched back on just before June.

## Common mistakes
- Switching off the top spender for a minor ad's higher ROAS, then watching account ROAS fall a week later.
- Killing an ad over a week-long dip, or cutting a consistently over-KPI ad outright without first reducing its budget.
- Remembering seasonal winners only once the season has already started.
- Pushing budget onto an ad showing a huge ROAS from one purchase.
- Using revivals as a reason to slow new production.
