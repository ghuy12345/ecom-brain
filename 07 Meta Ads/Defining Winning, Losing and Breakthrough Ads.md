# Defining Winning, Losing and Breakthrough Ads

## What it is
"Winner" means different things to different people, so teams optimise inconsistently and hit-rate claims become meaningless. A useful definition judges every ad on three axes together:
- **Spend share:** how much of the campaign or account budget it pulls.
- **KPI:** whether it hits your target CPA or ROAS.
- **Time:** how long it sustains both.
Winning is a sliding scale across all three, and the only kind that really matters is the one that lets you raise the budget.

## When to use it
- Before every weekly review, so the whole team labels results the same way.
- When setting creative team targets or bonuses.
- When deciding which ads get isolated, iterated or killed.

## How (steps)
1. **Sort every tested ad into four tiers:**
   - **Breakthrough:** meets the spend-share and KPI thresholds for the required time. It lets you scale the account. Scale it and iterate it.
   - **Spend winner:** takes spend but misses KPI. It keeps you level. Iterate to improve conversion, and throttle it.
   - **KPI winner:** hits KPI but gets little spend. Iterate to make it more appealing so it earns spend.
   - **Loser:** neither. Turn it off and record why.
2. **Use spend-share thresholds over about seven days.** Rough guide: around 2% of spend means no real data; around 10% is the minimum to take seriously; 30-50% at KPI is probably a winner. On small accounts (up to a few thousand a day), a true winner often takes the majority of its campaign's budget, sometimes around 80%, at or above KPI. If no ad of yours has ever done that, a true winner is probably still ahead of you.
3. **Apply the breakthrough test.** The ad's own KPI should beat the campaign's KPI, and overall campaign ROAS should improve when it arrives. An ad that absorbs budget without beating the campaign average is only a spend winner; delivery tends to pull money from it eventually, and it rarely lifts the account. Breakthroughs also tend to show stronger one-day-click returns, because they convert people quickly.
4. **Watch the trajectory, not one day.** Genuine breakthroughs show spend and return climbing together when you check at days 3, 7, 14 and 30. Day-to-day volatility is normal; read three-day averages. Many breakthroughs take a large share for about a fortnight, then ease off while the campaign remains healthy.
5. **Segment thresholds by account size.** A fixed rule like "10% of spend" means different dollars at $3k a month and $3M a month. Write separate thresholds for spend bands.
6. **Use a "super winner" definition for scaling.** For team morale, any ad that survives, earns some budget and meets KPI can be called a winner. For scaling decisions, count only ads taking a big share of spend at KPI. These are rare: a rough estimate, not a benchmark, is about 3-4% of tested ads, with another ~10% meeting KPI without scaling.

## Why fixed seven-day bonus rules mislead
Paying a fixed bonus for every ad that "wins" within seven days rewards ads that may be dead by day ten, and it inflates hit rates, especially if ads are switched off after a week. A fairer approach looks back at new ads after 30 days and rewards them according to the spend they actually helped add. Treat finding breakthroughs as the baseline expectation of the role, not a bonus event.

## Example
Say an account spends $100/day. Ad A takes $70 of the week's $700 and gets a purchase: about 10% share, so it is not scaling the account. Ad B takes 55% of spend at a CPA below target, and the campaign rises from $100 to $300/day over the next fortnight while holding KPI. Ad B is the breakthrough; Ad A is a loser, however good its single purchase looks.

## Common mistakes
- Labelling an ad a winner because its ROAS is high on tiny spend.
- One threshold for every account size.
- Getting excited about the best variant of a concept that still took 2% of spend.
- Hit-rate reporting where ads were switched off before they could prove longevity.
