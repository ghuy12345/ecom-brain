# One Broad Campaign - Spend as the Signal

## What it is
In a single broad campaign with the budget set at campaign level, Meta decides how to split spend between your ads. That allocation is itself the test. An ad that earns a meaningful share of spend at your target return is very likely a real winner; an ad that gets almost nothing is, for practical purposes, a weak ad. As a rough estimate, around 99% of ads that fail to win spend in a campaign-budget setup would not have beaten the winner anyway. You do not need separate testing and scaling campaigns, interest audiences or retargeting splits: the broad campaign prospects and retargets on its own, and your only campaign-level lever is budget.

## When to use it
- As your starting structure by default: one broad campaign with campaign budget is the most efficient setup for most brands, up to several thousand dollars a day and often far beyond. Adjust it to your account and its KPIs.
- When you want new ads tested against your best existing ads, not in a protected sandbox.
- When you produce enough concepts to keep feeding the campaign.

## How (steps)
1. Create one broad campaign: sales objective, campaign budget on, location, age and gender only.
2. Separate at the ad set level by angle or avatar: launch each new concept as its own ad set with three creatives, each carrying two primary texts and two headlines, so delivery has combinations to choose from instead of you pre-picking.
3. Keep a champions ad set of proven ads; new concepts must out-earn it at KPI to count.
4. Do not set minimum spend per ad set. Minimums tend to buy spend at worse efficiency without creating winners.
5. Watch the daily spend breakdown. Promote ads that win spend at target and treat them as your better ads. Leave ads that earn little running rather than switching them off or forcing budget onto them; they cost almost nothing while delivery passes them over.
6. Run everything in one consolidated campaign rather than several parallel automated campaigns. Ads competing in one pool lets delivery reach the best performer and keeps management simple at high creative output.
7. Judge any structural change over about 30 days, not on a hot day or two.

## Why forcing spend misleads
When you pull an ignored ad into a fixed-budget campaign and force money through it, delivery finds the easiest buyers available: people already close to purchasing. Those sales are collected rather than created, so the ad looks like a winner on platform ROAS while adding little. Forced-spend tests of this kind usually return far below the main campaign, and the occasional good result tends to fade within days. The emotional hurdle is accepting that most of your ads will earn little spend, and that this is a verdict on the ads, not a broken algorithm. The platform earns more when you sell more, so it has no reason to starve a genuinely good ad.

## Adding a winners ABO on top
One broad campaign is the starting point, not a ceiling. Once you have proven winners, moving copies of them into a separate campaign with fixed ad-set budgets gives you more flexibility to scale them (see [[Isolating Winners in a Dedicated ABO]]). Whether and when to do it depends on your starting point:
- **Stay with the single campaign** while spend is under roughly $1-2k/day, while winners are rare, or when you lack time to manage extra ad sets. Here isolation mostly adds complexity.
- **Add the ABO** when you regularly find breakthroughs, a new winner keeps crowding out older, still-profitable ads, or one winner collapsing could drag the whole campaign down.
Either way you can keep adding new ads to an ad set, whether it sits in the CBO or the ABO. The core rule holds throughout: the ABO is for ads that already earned spend in the main campaign, never for ads that did not.

## Example
Say a supplement brand at $2,000 a day launches four new concepts a week into its main campaign. One concept takes 35% of spend within five days at a return above target, and the campaign's overall ROAS rises. That is a winner. Another concept looks great on CTR but spends $40 in a week. The brand leaves it running untouched, writes down why it might have failed, and does not move it to a forced-spend campaign.

## Common mistakes
- Forcing budget onto ads the system ignored and then trusting their platform ROAS.
- Switching off ads that get no spend, as if they were costing you money.
- Declaring a winner from a small test campaign where spend was a tenth of the scaling campaign's.
- Assuming engagement (likes, comments) predicts spend.
- Applying this below a workable budget, where too few purchases exist for delivery to decide.
- Changing a working structure because of a new theory instead of making better ads.
