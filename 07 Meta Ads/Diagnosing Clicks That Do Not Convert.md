# Diagnosing Clicks That Do Not Convert

## What it is
It is tempting to celebrate an ad with a 4% CTR or a 50% hook rate. But Meta prioritises purchase intent over attention: an ad that grabs eyes without driving purchases will not be scaled, and if it is, it burns money. The core distinction is between attention and good attention. A broad, dramatic or curiosity-bait hook can win huge click-through from people who never had a reason to buy. Fewer clicks with more intent usually win.

## When to use it
- An ad has a very high CTR (sometimes well into double digits) or hook rate but few sales.
- An ad gets lots of engagement but little spend.
- An ad gets spend but misses KPI.
- Your store's revenue per session is low even though traffic looks healthy.

## How (steps)
1. **Did it get spend?** High engagement with no spend usually means delivery predicted low conversion. If you genuinely suspect a delivery glitch, a short test with a minimum budget can gather data, but that is the exception.
2. **Check the hook for the wrong crowd.** Most "bad hold rate" or "no sales" complaints are really hook problems. A clickbait or overly broad hook pulls in curious people who are not the avatar. Align the hook with what the body and product deliver. A hook rate of 25% among likely buyers is worth more than 50% from everyone.
3. **Check for a belief problem.** If the right people are clicking but not buying, they do not believe the claim. Write down what your avatar currently believes and work through those beliefs one by one, adding mechanism, proof or authority.
4. **Check ad-to-page congruency.** Does the page deliver exactly what the ad promised, in the same words and product name? A full story-led ad landing on a plain product page loses people. An ad aimed at category newcomers that lands on a page with dozens of options will confuse them; a simplified page fixes it.
5. **Check the offer's realism.** A rock-bottom price with a freebie attached can seem too good to be true; try a higher price or another offer.
6. **Check the ad type.** Fascinating-fact videos can hold attention while giving no urgency and no reason to purchase. Make sure the ad ends on why to buy now.
7. **Check clicks against landing page views.** A large gap means people click and bounce, the signature of misclicks or bait.
8. **Use revenue per session.** RPS = AOV x conversion rate. When RPS is low and CPC is high, suspect the creative (low-intent clicks), not the page. For example, AOV $140 at 1.2% gives about $1.68 per session; at a $1.50 CPC, that is close to break-even before product costs, so fix ad intent. AOV $65 at 4% gives $2.60, which is strong; if CPCs are high there, the ads lack the relatability or curiosity that earns cheap clicks.
9. **At small budgets, stay product-aware.** Below roughly $1k/day, information-heavy problem-aware ads often create clicks without purchases; niche to one sub-avatar and use offer ads for site visitors.
10. **Review ads and funnel together,** not just the numbers.

## Example
Say a posture-corrector brand runs a video opening on a shocking spine X-ray. CTR is 3.8% and hook rate 52%, but CVR is 0.4%. Breakdown shows a young, broad audience. Landing page views are 70% of link clicks. The team rewrites the hook to call out desk workers with neck pain, sends it to a page that opens with the same promise, and sees CTR fall to 1.6% while CVR triples.

## Common mistakes
- Treating hook rate or CTR as the success metric.
- Working on hold rate when the real issue is a hook that drew the wrong people.
- Lecturing strangers with broad facts that never connect to the product.
- Redesigning the page before checking whether the message matches.
