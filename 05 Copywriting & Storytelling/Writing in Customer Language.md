# Writing in Customer Language

## What it is
Most weak copy is written in copywriter language: thesaurus words, category jargon and polished adjectives that no real person would say out loud. Customer language is the opposite. It uses the exact phrases, slang and self-descriptions your buyers use in reviews, forums and comments.

It does two jobs at once. It builds trust, because sharing their dialect marks you as an insider. And it grabs attention, because familiar words trigger a "these are my people" reaction before the reader has decided whether to care.

A sharp form of customer language is the **label**: a name a group uses for itself, such as "night owl", "light sleeper", "stay-at-home mom", "history nerd" or "grinder". Labels reward the reader's identity and sense of community, and they make the message instantly easy to process.

## When to use it
- As a proofing pass on any finished draft.
- When writing hooks that need to call out a specific segment.
- When building landing page sections and you need each claim to feel earned rather than invented.
- When copy will not come: usually a sign you need more research, not more effort.

## How (steps)
**1. Bank the words before you write.** Pull phrases from your own reviews, competitor reviews, forums, comment sections and your research document. While building avatars, write down any term you do not recognise; unfamiliar slang is often the most valuable. An LLM can help search slang and label sources, but vet what it finds. Keep this bank somewhere you can grab from when stuck (see [[Voice of Customer and Psychographic Research]]).

**2. Find or create labels.** Look for existing labels the niche uses about itself. You can also coin a label tied to your mechanism, for instance a texture word between "creamy" and "crunchy", but invented labels only work once the market adopts them. Labels usually sit in the hook, sometimes in the body. They will repel people outside the group, which is fine; just remember a label earns the stop and nothing more, so the next line has to carry the viewer forward (see [[Writing the Bridge After the Hook]]).

**3. Try the doorstep test.** Would you say this sentence to someone standing at their front door? Nobody says "revolutionary all-in-one solution" on a doorstep.

**4. Swap words after drafting.** Look for polished, generic words and swap in the buyer's own phrasing:
- "stressed out" → "totally fried"
- "seeking a solution" → "desperate for anything that works"
- "lose weight" → "finally lose this muffin top"
- "revolutionary product" → "a lifesaver"

**5. Trace every claim to research.** Every claim on an ad or page should trace to a finding from research, a customer review or a true founder story. If you want to say "gluten-free" or "zero added sugar" and have not checked how buyers feel about it, do that research first, even a quick search. Research never truly closes.

**6. Lead with what matters most to the buyer.** On a page, rank benefits by their importance to the buyer, not by the template's default order. Cut or relocate any section that fails to justify itself. Settle the messaging before choosing imagery; imagery then sharpens it.

**7. Feed new customer behaviour back into copy.** If reviews show buyers using the product in a handy way you never planned for, rewrite the copy around it and use those reviews as proof.

## Example
A healthy-snack brand drafts a page with the headline "A revolutionary, nutritious snacking solution." Its reviews instead keep repeating phrases like "finally a snack my picky kid will eat" and "I keep a box in my car for school pickup." The rewrite leads with the parent's problem in their own words, then orders benefit blocks by what reviewers mention most: real ingredients, high fibre, then "zero added sugar" (which lands harder than the softer "no added sugar"), with "ready to eat" moved up because of the car-snack behaviour.

The review section is built the same way: one distinct review per buyer type (a gift buyer, someone avoiding certain ingredients, a parent of a fussy eater, someone who arrived from an ad), one review that mirrors the page headline, with the most powerful phrase of every review in bold. Generic "great product!" reviews are cut.

## Common mistakes
- Writing to sound smart instead of sounding like the buyer.
- Using internet slang the niche does not actually use; test labels the audience genuinely owns.
- Inventing claims without checking them against research.
- Following a page template robotically and keeping sections that do not fit.
- Using generic reviews when specific, buyer-matched ones exist.
- Assuming native wording always wins; it usually converts better, but treat that as a hypothesis and test it.
