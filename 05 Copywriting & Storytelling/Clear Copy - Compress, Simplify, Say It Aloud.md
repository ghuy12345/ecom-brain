# Clear Copy - Compress, Simplify, Say It Aloud

## What it is
Long copy is fine when the message needs the room, yet most long copy is simply unedited. Clear copy comes from four habits:
- **Compress**: edit with the delete key. Any word that is not helping is hurting.
- **Simplify**: aim for about a sixth-grade reading level. No reader has ever complained that an ad was too easy to follow.
- **Say it aloud**: write lines you could say to someone's face.
- **Draft in the real environment**: write hooks and statics inside a visual replica of the placement, because text that looks fine in a document can look clumsy on a phone.

Short writing takes more work than long writing. The old remark about lacking the time to write a short letter still holds.

## When to use it
- Every brief, script, static and page section, before it ships.
- When a hook wraps to four lines on screen or gets cut off.
- When a long sales video script starts to sound like a lecture.
- When a draft feels fine but nothing in it hits hard.

## How (steps)
**1. Draft, stop, step away.** Finish, then take a break and come back fresh. Much weak copy is a first draft nobody revisited.

**2. Cut with the Jenga test.** Pull each sentence, phrase and word in turn. If meaning and flow survive without it, delete it. A hook or headline is done when removing anything else would make it collapse. Run this on your winners too; they can usually lose something. Out of everything available to say, one point usually deserves the space.

**3. Check readability.** Run briefs and scripts through a readability tool and target grade 6 or lower. Swap long words for short ones ("alleviate" becomes "ease"), fix flagged sentences and split dense blocks. Layout matters: one paragraph can score grade 13 and, broken into short lines, grade 5. AI can help simplify; check what it returns. Long-form scripts tend to drift up toward an eleventh-grade level and lose people. Grade 6 is a default, not a law; very technical or luxury audiences may tolerate more if tests say so.

**4. Use the doorstep test.** Picture saying each line to a stranger at their front door. "A revolutionary fitness experience" fails because it never says what the product does. "Plug it in and aim it at the sky" passes. Every ad is spoken in some sense: a creator reads it, a voiceover reads it, or the reader hears it internally on a static. Spoken copy also nudges you toward demonstrating rather than asserting (see [[Show, Don't Tell - In Words and Visuals]]).

**5. Draft inside a replica of the placement.** Skip plain documents and task tools for ad copy. Use a design or whiteboard tool with cards that imitate each scene.
- Build a card for the hook, showing exactly how it will sit in the feed. Never skip this.
- Copy the card for the bridge and each later scene (a new line usually means a new slide) and add notes on pacing and visuals for the editor.
- For videos several minutes long, hand pacing decisions to the editor instead of building every scene.
- For statics, write with the picture in mind, place the words into a layout template, then tighten again when the finished design comes back.
- Keep a library of layouts taken from winning ads; they often spark concepts of their own.

Seeing the layout forces compression. Most great headlines are good headlines with words removed. A mock-up is no cure on its own; it can just as easily reveal that the copy itself needs work.

## Example
Say a back-pain brand's first hook is a long sentence about years of struggling with back pain and finally fighting back. On a vertical card it wraps to four lines and buries the point. Cut step by step, it becomes a five-word line promising that years of pain stop here, and the team tests it on one line versus two. A second hook, telling viewers their pain is not their fault, gets the same layout test.

In the body, "Our formula helps alleviate discomfort and fuel your progress" fails both readability and the doorstep test. Rewritten: "It eases the ache so you can get through the school run." Shorter, simpler, sayable, visual.

## Common mistakes
- Treating "long copy can work" as permission never to cut.
- Filler such as "fuel your progress" and needlessly formal words.
- Writing to sound clever; there is nothing to gain by sounding smarter than the reader.
- Drafting hooks in a text field and only discovering on screen that they are unreadable.
- Lecturing strangers; you interrupted them, so get to the point.
- Assuming a mock-up or a readability score replaces judgement about what to say.
