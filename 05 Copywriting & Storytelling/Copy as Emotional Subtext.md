# Copy as Emotional Subtext

## What it is
Two headlines can make the identical claim and produce completely different results. The difference is subtext: the unwritten message the reader infers, and the feeling and desire that inference sets off. Emotion responds to implication, not to the dictionary meaning of the words. Good copy is therefore less about clever phrasing and more about engineering what the reader concludes on their own.

A practical way to name that subtext is the four core emotions that sit under almost every persuasive line, each with an amplified version that multiplies its pull:
- **New**, amplified to **only** (nothing like this exists)
- **Easy**, amplified to **anybody can do it**
- **Safe**, amplified to **predictable** (others got the result, so will you)
- **Big**, amplified to **fast** (a large result, soon)

This framing comes from Kyle Milligan's book *Take Their Money*, which also treats copywriting as a language learned through pattern recognition rather than templates. The same four ideas appear as claim frames in [[Copywriting Fundamentals - Rules and Frames]].

## When to use it
- When a draft "reads fine" but you cannot say what the reader should feel after it.
- When you need variations of a winning ad and want them to be true variations, not cosmetic rewrites.
- When you study other people's copy and want to understand why it works, not just what it says.
- When a section stalls and you do not know what comes next.

## How (steps)
**Decode before you write (active reading).**
1. Read one strong piece of copy a day with a pen in hand. Mark patterns rather than memorising lines.
2. For every section, name the emotion it aims for and the core emotion (of the four) behind it.
3. Note any novel line that could spark an idea for your own product.
4. Keep at it until patterns recur enough that you can guess what comes next, the way you can predict the plot of a formulaic TV series after a binge.

Reading actual copy beats reading about copy, but bingeing only helps if you have this decoder running.

**Write for the inference.**
1. Before drafting a section, decide which core emotion it must communicate.
2. Find the specific claim, image or detail that makes the reader feel it without being told (see [[Show, Don't Tell - In Words and Visuals]]).
3. Reread the draft and ask what it communicates, what it makes the reader feel and which desire it touches. If the emotion or inference has no name, rewrite. "Conversational" alone is not enough; the line has to carry something like intrigue, novelty, ease, safety, scarcity or urgency.

**Build variations from subtext, not wording.**
When a winner needs fresh versions, ask three questions of the original:
1. What is the hook's message?
2. Which feeling does it leave?
3. What desire does it recognise?
Then say the same thing with new words. If the new version conveys a different message, or none at all, treat it as a new test rather than an iteration. Run this check across your live ads too: what feeling does each one leave?

## Example
A classic mail-order headline from John Caples, the line about people laughing as you take your seat at the piano, never mentions status. Its message is being doubted and then proving the doubters wrong. A flat rewrite such as "Learn to play piano like a pro" says roughly the same thing and triggers nothing.

The car-rental ad "We're only No. 2, so we try harder" worked the same way. Its candour signalled an underdog identity and gave a logical reason to expect better service. A boastful "we'll be No. 1" framing centred the brand's ambition instead of the customer. Later executions changed every word but kept the underdog feeling, and gathered a following of buyers who also saw themselves as underdogs.

Applied to a hypothetical skincare brand: "Our serum contains 2% niacinamide" says something. "The first serum my sensitive skin didn't fight back against" communicates New (first), Safe (my skin is like yours) and a recognised frustration, in fewer extra words.

## Common mistakes
- Leaning on headline or email templates without understanding the choice behind them; templates give phrases for one context, not the system that generates them.
- Treating "write conversationally" as the whole job.
- Reading books about copy but rarely reading real copy.
- Reading passively without marking patterns.
- Calling a reworded ad a variation when its emotional message has changed.
- Direct-response copy has little room for softening, so a line that communicates the wrong thing in few words does disproportionate damage.
