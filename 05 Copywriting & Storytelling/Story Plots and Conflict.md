# Story Plots and Conflict

## What it is
A plot is a story's shape: where the emotion begins, where it dips and where it finishes. Plots hold content; they are not the content. A story still works because of change, withheld details, causality, stakes and tension (see [[Story Building Blocks]]). Choosing a plot on purpose, though, gives you a dependable way to structure an ad and, more valuably, a systematic way to iterate on a winner.

In direct response the emotional line runs more or less one way and has to finish high, because the buyer needs hope when deciding. Many useful arcs come from Christopher Booker's *The Seven Basic Plots*, with a few additions that suit ads.

Every story also needs conflict, and conflict needs an enemy.

## When to use it
- Structuring a new story-led script.
- Iterating a winner: keep the angle, change the plot.
- When every ad has collapsed into "problem, agitate, solve".

## How (steps)
**1. Choose an arc that finishes high.**
- **Overcoming the monster**: trouble, struggle, victory. Essentially problem-agitate-solution; common and hit-or-miss. A stronger variant keeps escalating: one problem, then another, then a worse one, then a single fix for all. With no lull, attention never drifts.
- **Rags to riches**: rock bottom, effort, breakthrough, success. It is about becoming someone new, and it relies on viewers recognising themselves in the protagonist. One exception: a protagonist who starts even worse off than the viewer and still wins can create belief without relatability.
- **In media res**: open on the decisive moment, then the discovery, then a win that confirms it. Practical trick: rewatch your finished ad, mark the first moment it delivers what viewers came for, delete everything earlier and open there.
- **The quest**: an aim, setbacks, a discovery, success. The hook sounds aspirational rather than desperate.
- **Voyage and return**: ordinary life, upheaval, insight, a better ordinary. The narrator went to the extreme so the viewer can skip it, which builds authority with doubters.
- **Rebirth**: stuck, a wake-up call, transformation. No villain and no struggle, only realisation; well suited to unaware audiences.
- **Confusion**: a mistaken belief, an insight, then clarity (the narrator misread the situation and has since corrected it). Useful in sophisticated markets and for working through objections.
- **The two paths**: where the current road leads, and a better road. It projects the future instead of dramatising present pain; a natural fit for explainers, voiceovers and talking-head ads.

**Skip tragedy.** A decline with no redemption is built to frighten or prevent, not to sell; it belongs to public-health and charity campaigns. The one exception is an ad that deliberately leaves the tension open because the click resolves it on an advertorial or listicle.

**2. Use plots to iterate.** Work out which arc a winning ad follows, then rewrite the same angle in a different arc and test it. Over time you learn which shapes your avatar responds to. Resist forcing every script into "problem, fight, win".

**3. Name your enemy.** Strong brands usually define something they are against. Ask three questions:
- **A, approaches**: what other method of solving the problem are you up against? A separate path to the result, not merely a rival product.
- **B, beliefs**: what belief do you champion or oppose? This works through identity, since people's purchases reflect who they think they are. It is the widest category.
- **C, competitors**: which brands sell the same thing in your category? The riskiest choice, because claiming superiority without a concrete difference falls flat, and direct claims about named rivals bring legal risk.
The categories overlap, and a well-picked enemy sharpens positioning; the strategic side is covered in [[Positioning Exercise - Map, Hole, Enemy]].

## Example
One hypothetical energy supplement, several plots:
- Overcoming the monster: the mid-afternoon slump, the battle with coffee, the fix.
- Rebirth: years of waking up drained without questioning it, a wake-up moment, clear mornings.
- Voyage and return: a month of extreme biohacking, burnout, then a simple routine that worked.
- Confusion: the narrator assumed they lacked energy, then realised the real issue was something else.
- Two paths: where another year of four coffees a day leads, versus the alternative.

Enemies in practice:
- Approach: a gummy greens brand framing powdered greens as the wrong way to get your greens; a slide board pitched against cycling as a workout.
- Belief: an apparel brand campaigning against mental-health stigma; Liquid Death opposing plastic waste and po-faced wellness marketing, retelling one rebellious story across many ads.
- Competitor: a dog-crate brand mocking noisy, ugly wire crates. A clean enemy, though the ad jumps straight to product, so it is a weaker story.

## Common mistakes
- Letting the plot replace change, stakes and tension.
- Using tragedy, or any arc that ends low, in a conversion ad.
- Protagonists the viewer cannot identify with.
- Claiming superiority over rivals without giving a reason.
