# Show, Don't Tell - In Words and Visuals

## What it is
When you tell someone a benefit ("you'll have more energy", "it's high quality"), you activate the logical, skeptical part of their brain. They already expect a seller to say the product is better, faster and easier, so the claim bounces off. When you show the experience, a behaviour change or a demonstration, the reader reaches the conclusion alone, and conclusions people reach on their own are the ones they believe.

The working rule: let the prospect say the benefit to themselves. You supply the picture.

Showing happens on two levels:
- **In words**: concrete, visual language that describes the experience instead of naming the benefit.
- **In visuals**: demonstrations and imagery that make the claim unnecessary.

## When to use it
- On every benefit line in an ad, script or page.
- When a claim is being met with doubt in comments or on surveys.
- When your category seems "hard to show"; it usually is not, the habit of stating benefits is the real obstacle.
- When choosing imagery for a landing page section.

## How (steps)
**1. Audit each line: telling or showing?** Go line by line and mark every stated benefit.

**2. Rewrite benefits as experiences.** Describe the moment the reader will live through.

| Telling | Showing |
|---|---|
| You'll feel energised | You'll feel ready to charge through a brick wall |
| A premium-quality wallet | A wallet machined from titanium |
| A confidence boost | Nights out stop feeling like something to dread |
| More energy | Waking before the alarm, walking past the 3pm vending machine without a thought |

A concrete fact (titanium) often beats a quality adjective, because the reader infers the quality. See [[Facts Over Opinions]].

**3. Convert abstract words into ones you can picture.** "Productive routines" is abstract; "up at 6am writing before the house wakes" is concrete. Rewrite on paper, again and again, until every abstraction turns into something you can see. An AI model makes a good proofing pass: ask it to flag every abstract noun, verb, adjective, adverb and cliché, then rewrite each flagged item yourself.

**4. Ask "how could we show that?" and delete the claim.** If footage can prove the point, drop the sentence. A caption announcing that a famous portrait is smiling adds nothing. Half the words, twice the examples.

**5. Make sure the demo proves the real claim.** A dramatic stain-removal stunt is persuasive but does not necessarily prove whitening. Choose demonstrations that match the claim you actually make.

**6. Give each page claim a picture that proves it.** On landing pages, each benefit block should have a visual that does the work: ingredients cut and blended for "real food", the product beside its source fruit for "no added sugar", a short prep clip turned into a GIF rather than a static photo. Keep image sets consistent (same framing, same label treatment); consistency matters more than polish. Build copy and imagery together, because an image choice can change the line beside it.

**7. Keep it real.** Never pull pictures from search engines; well-informed shoppers spot them. And a founder story only works if it happened. If there is no genuine origin story, lean on how well you understand the buyer rather than inventing a past.

## Example
Visual swaps that replace a stated claim:
- "Calms a crying baby": a wailing infant, the product is added to the room, the tears stop; the only on-screen text says it is lavender scented.
- "Easy to clean": a rival dish rack being scrubbed with a toothbrush, then yours simply going under the tap.
- "Easy to put on": feet sliding into shoes with no hands.
- "Better fit": a side-by-side of a baggy tank top and a well-cut one.
- "Your diet is wrecking your gut": pour a day's real intake (chips, coffee, energy drink, doughnuts) into one container.

A grooming brand can go further and write the desire as an exaggerated experience, then disarm it by admitting the exaggeration, which keeps the image while protecting credibility. In some formats, such as animated or AI-generated creative, abstract benefits can be made literal in ways live footage cannot.

## Common mistakes
- Stating benefits and adjectives instead of experiences and facts.
- Explaining what the visual already shows.
- Using a demonstration that is impressive but does not prove the actual claim.
- Polished but inconsistent imagery; a plain page that answers what the reader came for beats a beautiful, shallow one.
- Stock or scraped images that savvy buyers recognise.
- Fabricated founder stories.
- Assuming a creator-delivered line will work with anyone; sometimes the person on screen is part of what is being shown.
