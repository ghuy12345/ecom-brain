# Handling Objections in Copy

## What it is
Every cold buyer arrives with doubts. Ideal copy keeps a doubt from ever taking shape; failing that, it answers the doubt at the very moment it surfaces, before the reader leaves.

Objections fall into three types:
- **Product or market**: "I've tried this kind of thing" or "nothing in this category works."
- **Claim**: "That can't be real," even from someone who likes the category.
- **Avatar or belief**: "Not for someone like me." This is about the buyer, not the product.

What buyers doubt also shifts with market sophistication. Young markets wonder whether it works at all (answer with proof, a clear mechanism, authority). Middle-stage markets want to know what makes yours different (a new mechanism, plus showing past disappointments were never the buyer's doing). Mature markets ask whether it is meant for them (identity: describe the buyer so precisely there is nothing left to object to). See [[Market Sophistication Stages]].

## When to use it
- Body copy, scripts and page sections after the hook.
- When comments argue, or clicks are healthy but purchases are not.
- When price is the main sticking point.

## How (steps)
**1. Gather objections verbatim.** Pull them from low-star reviews, forum threads, comments and videos you already collected. This is synthesis, not new research.

**2. Turn each into a problem to solve.** "Customers think it's a cheap copy" becomes "How do we prove quality despite the lower price?" Answers rarely arrive at once; research further and let it sit.

**3. Pick a strategy that fits the type.**
- **Facts** (product and claim objections): give them something they cannot disprove. "Delicious cookies" invites argument; a cookie that has sold out dozens of times does not. Never invent facts. See [[Facts Over Opinions]].
- **Reversals** (product and avatar objections): concede the biggest objection, then show the product was made for exactly that person (results even if you slip up on weekends; firming that works even on badly damaged skin).
- **Because** (every type): give each claim a reason. "Simple to clean, since it's dishwasher-safe." Stronger reasons make stronger claims.
- **Reassigning blame** (product and avatar objections): people let one bad experience condemn a whole category. Point to the real culprit (capsules destroyed by stomach acid before they work; filler grains in "premium" food). Stay on the buyer's side: your frustration was fair, but the cause was this, not you. Avoid lecturing or piling on statistics; once the effort shows, the reader is gone.
- **Analogies** (all types, especially price): reframe through something familiar. See [[Metaphors, Similes and Analogies]].

**4. Build a chain of agreement (gradualization).** Keep the reader nodding so they never stop to doubt:
- Promise, proof, promise: every promise is followed at once by evidence.
- Parallel statements: let something already accepted carry the next claim.
- Repeated proof: several experts or sources back to back, so authorities carry the message instead of you.
A worked order: an accepted truth, a step built on it, the real objection named before they think it, a fact showing the category is to blame, a promise to fix it, the mechanism, specific proof, reassurance at the point they wonder whether they will keep it up, then the problem restated as solved. The idea comes from Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (see [[Breakthrough Advertising Ch 9 - Gradualization - Part 1]]).

**5. Reframe price instead of defending it.**
- Break it down into a small daily amount. Be careful comparing it to a habit people love, such as their morning coffee.
- Compare upward: to a pricier alternative, or to what the unsolved problem costs (procedures, vet bills, stress).
- Reframe the desire: they are buying the outcome or identity, such as finally wearing the clothes at the back of the wardrobe. Use this deeper in the copy, not in the hook.
- Some brands openly admit they are expensive and explain why. A "no brand markup" argument helps, but never as the only reframe.

**6. Plan demonstrations for the hardest doubts.** Timers, side-by-sides and before-and-afters answer "does it really work faster?" better than any sentence.

## Example
Say a dish-rack ad (hypothetical) stacks agreement: if you cook daily, you likely own a dish rack, and you likely dread scrubbing it. The presenter mentions having gone through three brands, which turns experience into authority and answers "why trust her?" before anyone asks. Only then does the product appear, with its easy-clean claim attached to a reason.

Say a budget rival to a market leader faces "too good to be true", and research reveals two camps ("they're all the same" and "avoid this type entirely"), so each gets its own message.

## Common mistakes
- Telling buyers they were wrong instead of reassigning blame.
- Lecturing or stacking statistics until it sounds desperate.
- Defending price instead of reframing what is being bought.
- Invented facts or one message for every objection camp.
- Repeating a once-fresh answer after every competitor has adopted it; find a new way to deliver the same reassurance.
