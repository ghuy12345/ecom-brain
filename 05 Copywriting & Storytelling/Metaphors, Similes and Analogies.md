# Metaphors, Similes and Analogies

## What it is
Figurative language explains something new by pointing at something the reader already understands. It does four jobs in ad copy:
- **Context**: gives the brain something familiar to hang a new idea on.
- **Simplification**: makes complex science or a new mechanism easy to grasp.
- **Reframing**: makes an old solution look absurd, which explains why it failed.
- **Borrowed credibility**: compares your product to something already respected.

People prefer clarity, and a good comparison makes the customer feel smart rather than lectured.

The three forms differ in how hard the reader has to work:
- **Metaphor** declares one thing to *be* another (a shoe described as a glove for the foot). The reader makes the leap themselves, so it does not feel like a claim, but it takes more processing and can be harder to read.
- **Simile** compares directly with "like" or "as" ("fits like a glove"). It is clearer, but it reads as a claim and can raise skepticism.
- **Analogy**, in practice, extends the comparison to a relationship ("fits your foot the way a glove fits your hand", or "an enzyme that works like the alternator in your car"). The textbook four-part analogy form almost never sounds natural in an ad.

As always, judge the line by what it communicates, not what it literally says (see [[Copy as Emotional Subtext]]).

## When to use it
- Products that need a lot of explaining or involve friction, where the buyer must understand how it works before believing it.
- New categories with no existing mental shelf.
- Explaining why past attempts failed without blaming the buyer.
- Reframing price or value objections.
- When a mechanism is real but sounds technical.

## How (steps)
1. **Name the thing that is hard to grasp.** The mechanism, the category, the old solution's flaw, or the value.
2. **List familiar systems with the same shape.** Cars, kitchens, the body, sport, money, nature, everyday chores. You want a reference the audience instantly recognises; an obscure comparison costs more attention than it saves.
3. **Choose the form.** Use a metaphor when you want the reader to make the claim themselves; a simile when clarity matters more than subtlety; an extended analogy when you need to explain a process.
4. **Use comparison to reframe old solutions.** Make the incumbent approach sound absurd. Traditional pee pads become "like walking barefoot through a jungle" for a dog.
5. **Borrow severity or authority.** Likening a risk to something everyone already treats as serious (running ads without fundamentals is like piloting a plane with no licence) carries that seriousness across.
6. **Pair with visuals where you can.** A visual metaphor can do the work without a word of copy.
7. **Test against a literal version.** Run the plain claim against the figurative one; sometimes the literal fact wins.

**Where to look for inspiration.** Older great ads are full of these techniques. Today's biggest brands mostly reinforce a position they already own, which makes them weak templates for a challenger explaining something new.

## Example
Famous patterns:
- Red Bull's "gives you wings": wings stand in for energy, a pure metaphor.
- A fast-food line saying a burger tastes like a million dollars but costs one: a simile plus a juxtaposition.
- A roofing-tile ad comparing a roof without its tiles to a hospital without doctors: an analogy that borrows seriousness.
- An early personal computer invited people to "test drive" it, borrowing the premium feel of buying a car.
- A ketchup brand showing a sliced tomato with a line about nobody growing ketchup like them: a visual metaphor that also claims category leadership.
- A small car ad boasting it had as many wheels as a luxury saloon and more boot space than a sports car: borrowed authority played for laughs.

Applied to direct-to-consumer brands:
- A digestive supplement explaining its enzyme as the alternator that keeps the car's battery charged.
- A made-to-measure clothing app described as "a tailor in your phone", collapsing a new, high-friction process into a familiar one.
- A mid-priced durable product reframed against price objections: it is not a luxury, it is the reliable everyday car, not the sports car.

## Common mistakes
- Comparisons the audience does not recognise, which add confusion instead of removing it.
- Mixing several metaphors in one line until none land.
- Similes so obviously promotional that they trigger the same skepticism as a plain claim.
- Overusing one analogy across every ad until it becomes fatigue.
- Modelling on market leaders' brand-reinforcement ads rather than on challenger-style explanation.
