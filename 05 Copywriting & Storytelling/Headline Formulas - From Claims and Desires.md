# Headline Formulas - From Claims and Desires

## What it is
A headline expresses a message; it does not invent one. You reach a headline from one of two starting points:
- **A claim.** You know what you want to say. Run it through transformations to find its most striking form.
- **A desire.** You know the deep want your product channels. Pair it with something shifting in the culture, and the headline forms around that meeting point.

Pre-sale pages such as listicles and advertorials add a third layer: proven formulas that also shape the page. In every case the headline carries the most weight; if it neither grabs attention nor promises something specific, nothing beneath it gets read.

## When to use it
- A strong claim sits under a flat headline.
- You need a batch of headline tests for an ad or pre-sale page.
- The category is crowded and you need a different desire to approach from.

## How (steps)
**Route 1: transform a claim.** State the claim plainly, then express it through whichever of these fit (adapted from the claim chapter of Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*; see [[Breakthrough Advertising Ch 4 - 38 Ways to Verbalize a Claim]]):
- Put a number on the size or speed of the result.
- Compare it ("less than a coffee a week").
- Make it sensory or metaphorical ("melts away").
- Dramatise it with a small story, reversal or before-and-after.
- Phrase it as a paradox, a question, or a question with its answer.
- Remove a limitation ("no surgery"), or set one anyone can clear ("if you can count to ten...").
- Attach an authority, a group the reader identifies with, newness or exclusivity.
- Condense it by naming what the product replaces ("a whole repair kit in one tube").
- Link the mechanism to the claim, or surprise by contradicting how readers expect it to work.
- Warn about the cost of doing nothing, or call the claim almost too good to believe.
- Challenge a belief that is holding the reader back.
Keep the structure of older examples and refresh their dated specifics.

**Route 2: build from desire.** Dig under a benefit by repeatedly asking what it would give them, until a feeling appears. Then:
1. Choose one force behind desire: a mass instinct (health, status, attraction, belonging, control, comfort) or a problem modern technology creates (complexity, overwhelm, fragility, maintenance, incompatibility, obsolescence).
2. Pair it with one force of change: a style trend, or something the public has recently learned.
3. Write one headline that serves both. Instinct plus newly learned information often yields the biggest breakthroughs.
4. Try elevation: pitch one product at several levels of desire (comfort, control, status, health) and see which converts. It has to ring true, or it reads as manipulation. Never pull luxury goods down to convenience.
Avoid filling the grid mechanically. A better habit is to take headlines that already work and name the desire each one triggers. See [[Forces Behind Mass Desire - Instincts, Technology Problems and Change]].

**Route 3: pre-sale page formulas.**
- Listicle shapes: number plus benefit, number plus problem solved, number plus audience plus promise, number plus emotional trigger plus promise, number plus timeframe plus promise. General form: [number] ways to [outcome] without [unwanted method].
- Advertorial shapes, written to read like editorial: discovery, personal story, expert exposé, hidden truth, contrarian, warning, suppressed knowledge.
- A three-layer hook: why care (the result), who it is for, then an unexpected angle that opens curiosity.
- By awareness: problem-aware readers get the problem first with a discovery twist; solution-aware get differentiation; product-aware get proof that busts objections. Questions suit problem-focused pages, statements suit solution-focused ones, and numbers usually beat none.
- Each list item's sub-headline must stand alone; draft five per item and keep the best.
- Calls to action that feel like starting something ("find out if it suits you") tend to beat transactional ones ("buy now").

**Test in batches.** Draft 10 to 15 variants across types (fear, curiosity, problem, story, revelation, authority) and test two or three at once: ad click-through first, page conversion second.

**Headline versus image.** Forced to choose, prioritise the headline. In image-led native placements a striking photo may earn attention first, so the headline must reward it. A broad headline needs very explicit imagery so the message stays clear.

## Example
Say a dog-harness brand tests two near-identical headlines. One combines the health instinct with new information (collar pulling can injure a dog's windpipe) and asks owners why they still do it. The other calls collars outdated, which leans on status. If the health version wins, understanding why is the real takeaway. Another pairing: fragility with buy-it-for-life ("the last harness you'll need").

## Common mistakes
- Hunting for a headline before the claim or desire is clear.
- Trying to manufacture a want nobody has; you can only channel one.
- Defining the audience by age and gender rather than desire.
- Claiming celebrity, expert or vet use that is untrue or unsupported.
- Fear-driven headlines that fail tone or ethics checks.
