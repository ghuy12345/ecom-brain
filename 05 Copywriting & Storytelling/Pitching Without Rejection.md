# Pitching Without Rejection

## What it is
The pitch is where the product first appears as the answer, after the ad has explained, informed or demonstrated something. In most cold-traffic ads it works like a call to action. Pitch too early and the viewer feels sold to and leaves. Pitch at the right moment, in the right voice, and it feels like a helpful tip.

Two conditions must be true before you pitch:
1. **The payoff has landed.** Every reason the hook promised has been given, the curiosity gap is closed and the setup has been honoured. Pitching before this breaks trust; the viewer was waiting for something and got an ad instead.
2. **You have established belief.** Relatability, a new mechanism, proof, authority, and objections prevented or answered. Different people need different amounts: one buyer is satisfied by relatability and a mechanism, another wants more proof or an expert. In more sophisticated markets the copy must also supply identity, so the buyer feels "this is for people like me".

Offer and sale ads aimed at the most-aware audience are the exception; they can pitch directly because belief already exists.

## When to use it
- Any educational, story-led or problem-led ad where the product appears partway through.
- When watch time is healthy but click-through or conversion drops after the product appears.
- When writing the transition line that moves a script from teaching to selling.
- When planning a founder-led or creator-led ad.

## How (steps)
**1. Check the payoff.** Reread the hook. List what it promised. Confirm every promise is delivered before the product shows up.

**2. Check belief.** Ask which belief elements this audience needs: relatability, mechanism, proof, authority, objection handling, identity. Fill gaps before the pitch, not after.

**3. Consider a flash-forward.** Direct response can open with the payoff: reveal the result up front and use the remaining time to explain the route there. Once viewers glimpse the result, they stay to learn the method. A detergent experiment revealing harsh residue in the opening seconds, or a dog using a pad correctly every time before you explain how, both work this way.

**4. Pitch as a recommendation, not a sales line.** Shoppers are wary online and like being pointed to things by someone they trust. A founder or creator delivering it feels like buying from a human. Except in offer ads, act as if you are passing on information rather than speaking for the brand.

**5. Use a transition line.** Lines that move naturally from teaching to recommending:
- "Now here's the thing..."
- "Not sure where to begin? ..."
- "The only catch..."
- "Here's what I ended up doing..."
- "So many of you asked me about this..."

**6. Pick the pitch style that fits the speaker and urgency.**
- Not from the company, no urgency: an expert pointed me to it; here's my go-to and two or three reasons; lots of people switched because of one specific thing. Experience-based authority ("I've bought several of these, this one's the best") beats a vague "I did my research", which nobody believes.
- Not from the company, with urgency: it keeps selling out; there's a sale on and I don't know how long it lasts.
- From the company, no urgency: why the brand was started; the guarantee.
- From the company, with urgency: the sale runs while stock lasts; it has sold out before.

Only use urgency that is real. See [[Founder Ads]] for the founder-led version.

## Example
A pet-hygiene brand's ad opens on a dog walking to its pad and using it perfectly, the payoff shown first. The creator then explains what she tried that failed, why the old pads confused the dog (reassigning blame), the scent mechanism that guides the dog, and a quick clip proving it on a new puppy. Only then: "If you're wondering which pads I switched to, it's these. Two reasons: the scent strip and the leak-proof edge." No hard sell, no "buy now".

Great payoffs also earn shares. Viewers feel they got real value, feel they discovered something first, and think of someone it could help.

## Common mistakes
- Pitching before the hook's promise is delivered.
- Pitching before belief is built, then blaming the audience or the offer.
- Speaking as the brand in an ad that is supposed to feel like a recommendation.
- Generic authority lines ("I did tons of research").
- Fabricated scarcity or urgency, which erodes trust once noticed.
- Using the same amount of belief-building for every audience, regardless of sophistication.
