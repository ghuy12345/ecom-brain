# Raising the Stakes

## What it is
Stakes are whatever the reader could win or lose. The larger it feels, the more attention they pay. A flat ad describes a product doing its job; a high-stakes ad makes you feel the situation, the way you would feel a marriage proposal in front of a full stadium.

Stakes can be positive (what you gain) or negative (what inaction costs you). They can appear in the hook or at any later point. The goal is drama the buyer recognises as true, not invented danger.

## When to use it
- As a second-pass edit on any finished ad or script.
- When an ad gets clicks but viewers do not feel a reason to act now.
- When carts fill but checkouts stay rare, a common sign that urgency is missing.
- When a product solves a "nice to have" problem and the copy treats it that way.

## How (steps)
Reread the draft and ask: how can I put more at stake, add drama, make this feel like a big deal? Then work through five levers. Each comes with a question to ask.

**1. Make it bigger.** Can the problem or result be shown in a more extreme, more vivid form?
- Dog accidents become ruined hardwood floors.
- "More energy" becomes "ready to charge through a brick wall".

**2. Add witnesses.** Who sees it happen, ideally someone with authority or someone you love?
- The dog has an accident in front of the landlord.
- Your children are watching when you finally tackle it.

**3. Make it urgent.** What makes it matter today?
- The damage is happening today; each accident strengthens the habit.
- "This is the ideal age, and it only gets harder from here" is stronger than a vague "now's a good time".
- One alarm word such as "emergency" changes how the brain reads a line.
- Urgency from symptoms the buyer has already noticed (yellower teeth, worse breath) beats abstract threats.

**4. Make it permanent.** What disappears, or arrives, for good?
- Decades of dental trouble, or decades of people complimenting your smile.
- Scars that never fade after a cheap razor.
- A wallet that ends up in your grandchildren's pockets.

**5. Make it cost more.** Count the true price in money, hours, relationships or feelings?
- Afternoon crashes that steal hours every day.
- Snoring that sends a partner to the spare room.
- Procedures that cost thousands if the problem is left alone.

**Check the emotion.** If you can feel the situation when you read it back, the stakes are up.

**Stay on the right side of fear.** Raise stakes with things the buyer already experiences or can verify. Use "could set you up for" rather than "you are causing". Absolve the buyer by blaming the old method rather than the person ("brushing a dog's teeth is genuinely hard"), then pivot to relief: things can be different.

## Example
Low-stakes to high-stakes rewrites, all hypothetical:
- A DNA kit: "Learn your heritage" becomes "Find out what your genes might say about your health" (bigger).
- A custom-song gift: "I bought my wife a personalised song" becomes "My wife was sure I'd forgotten her birthday" (costs more, adds a witness).
- A dental powder for dogs: "Cleans teeth" turns into "Skip this and your dog could face years of mouth problems" (permanent).
- A holster: "Draw fast" becomes "Draw fast when it's an emergency" (urgent).
- A fishing drink sleeve: rather than describing a can holder for anglers, a short story in which a police officer catches you casting a line beneath a bridge. The product is barely discussed, yet attention holds because something is at stake (witnesses).

In a pet-care ad structure, stakes escalate in sequence: a surprising hook, a fact that widens the gap, a shift from cosmetic to health consequences, absolution of the owner, symptoms they have already seen, then the turn to an easy, fast fix.

## Common mistakes
- Inventing dangers the buyer does not recognise; it reads as fearmongering and erodes trust.
- Accusing the viewer directly instead of blaming the method.
- Stopping at "bigger" when witnesses, permanence or cost would land harder.
- Raising stakes with no turn to relief, leaving the reader anxious rather than motivated.
- Unsupported health or safety claims; stakes still need to be true.
