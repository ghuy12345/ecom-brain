# Why Stories Sell

## What it is
An ad exists to get a purchase, today; teaching is at most a means to that. Education feels like the obvious route, but it hits two walls:
- **Familiar information** (things people already know, like "sleep and water help with weight") earns a nod and no action.
- **New information** does better, because novelty brings hope and shifts the blame for past failures to a fresh cause (the real issue is stress hormones, not overeating). Yet people can still take it in and do nothing. If knowing were enough, they would already have acted.

What actually blocks the sale is psychological: what people believe, feel and expect about the outcome. Moving those beliefs is the writer's real job, and stories are the most practical tool for it.

**How a story moves belief.** The viewer steps into a character's shoes. The character's belief changes. The viewer's changes alongside it, so they persuade themselves. Because nobody is told they are wrong, the "agenda detector" stays quiet, the reflex that assumes a brand only shares facts to make a sale. Stories also lower defences, work through emotion rather than scrutiny, and stick in memory. A believable story can even feel like evidence, which is exactly why it must be honest.

## When to use it
- An ad educates well but does not convert.
- You are scaling, which means reaching colder people.
- The category has invisible results and skeptical buyers, such as supplements and skincare.
- You have no visible, demonstrable mechanism to lean on.

## How (steps)
**1. Know which buyer you are losing.** Splitting buyers into "logical" and "emotional" is too fuzzy to be useful. A sharper split is how much information someone needs first:
- **Low-information buyers** need little; they are almost ready.
- **High-information buyers** need far more persuasion and decide slowly.
A sensible working assumption, worth checking for your market, is that high-information buyers make up the bulk. Early winners mostly reach the ready few. As budgets grow, the platform must find people who need more, and results fall when the ad cannot give it to them. When performance collapses on scale-up, suspect the creative before the auction.

**2. Wrap the information in a story.** Left alone, high-information buyers piece things together over weeks from many places. An ad hoping to shortcut that has to carry the information itself, yet raw facts fired at a scrolling stranger fail because the product is nowhere in their thoughts. A story is the wrapper that wins attention and keeps it while the information lands.

**3. Make it relatable, not just about you.** Having a story and having a gripping one are different things. A common first mistake is believing a story is gripping because it is about you. A mishap involving a luxury car will not land with someone who cannot imagine owning one. Your fascination does not guarantee the prospect's.

**4. Balance education and relatability by market stage.** The two are not rivals; the mix changes with sophistication. Treat the split below as a rule of thumb, not a law: where your product sits depends on your audience and market, so test both an education-led and a relatability-led version before settling on one:
- **Less sophisticated markets, or products with an obvious, visible mechanism**: education and new information can carry the ad. A material that visibly wipes clean needs little story.
- **Sophisticated markets, or invisible results**: education no longer differentiates because everyone is doing it. Relatable stories and identity outweigh product facts.
- **In between**: let a story deliver the new information, so the mechanism arrives through a character's experience rather than as a lecture.

**5. Aim for a breakthrough, not a small lift.** Education converts some people. It rarely produces the standout ad that absorbs most of the budget.

## Example
A hypothetical sleep supplement runs an education ad stating that magnesium deficiency is common and disrupts sleep. Viewers nod and scroll. The story version follows a nurse on night shifts who tried every sleep hack, assumed she was simply a bad sleeper, then learned her schedule was draining a mineral her body needed for deep sleep. The same fact now arrives through her change of belief, and viewers who recognise themselves in her reach the conclusion on their own.

Old Spice shows relatability winning over product facts at scale. Rather than arguing about ingredients, its famous campaign spoke to the women who often buy body wash for their partners, with a playful line about how their man could smell.

## Common mistakes
- Treating information as persuasion.
- Blaming bids or account structure when the ad cannot inform colder buyers.
- Brand- or founder-centred stories the prospect cannot relate to.
- Lecturing about a mechanism instead of letting a character discover it.
- Dropping education in early markets where it still sets you apart.
- Assuming where your market sits instead of testing education-led and story-led versions against each other.
- Dishonest stories; anything that feels like evidence must be true.
