# Facts Over Opinions

## What it is
"Best-tasting protein powder" is an opinion. Anyone can disagree with it, and a skeptical buyer will. "Six ingredients, against the 32 in the leading brand" is a fact. The reader cannot argue with it, and they draw the conclusion ("cleaner, simpler") on their own.

Facts work for several reasons:
- **Nobody can dispute them.** Any opinion can be disputed.
- **They communicate more than they say.** A horsepower figure tells you about speed, engineering and status; "unmatched power and performance" tells you nothing.
- **They stack credibility.** After a run of facts, the reader is primed to accept the occasional opinion you do make.
- **They guarantee you said something.** Vague praise is easy to write and easy to skip.
- **They often hide a story.** Behind most facts is a reason, an origin or a decision you can build copy on.

Facts are not only statistics. Reaching for a percentage every time is the lazy route. Firsts, physical properties, ingredient counts, materials and comparisons are all facts.

## When to use it
- Whenever you write a claim, especially for skeptical, high-sophistication markets.
- When ads get attention but comments argue with the claim.
- In hooks that need to make an accusation or point out a problem without sounding preachy.
- Any time you are tempted to write "best", "amazing", "premium" or "high quality".

## How (steps)
1. **Flag every claim** and check whether it is just a judgement.
2. **Recast it as something checkable**: ingredients, a number, a study result, a "first" claim, a material, a physical property, a test.
3. **Pick one strong fact about your product** and build a piece around it. Be honest about its limits; a candy shell keeps chocolate off your fingers compared with a bare bar, not compared with a blowtorch.
4. **Consider letting the fact stand alone.** An ad that just shows your ingredient count beside a competitor's lets the reader conclude "better" without you saying it.
5. **When an opinion is unavoidable, follow it quickly with a fact.** Softeners like "could" are fine when tied to a real figure.
6. **Turn accusations into facts.** "You're a bad owner if you don't do this" gets dismissed. Asking whether the viewer is among the 96% of owners who skip it daily makes the same point, creates mild guilt and borrows authority, because facts feel authoritative. Keep the fact relevant to the hook.
7. **Verify every number.** Check borrowed statistics and study figures against your product facts or the original source before launch. An AI assistant is a handy sanity check for "is this line fact or opinion?" but not for whether the fact is true.

## Example
Rewrites across categories:
- A rug brand: a line promising dogs will adore the rug (opinion) becomes "Dog-proof, machine-washable rugs" (fact).
- A teen supplement: "Takes them from grumpy to happy" becomes a note that low levels of certain vitamins are linked to moodiness, followed by the nutrients the product contains.
- A kettle (hypothetical): "Premium build" becomes "The only kettle with no plastic touching the water."
- A toothpaste: "Fights cavities" becomes a specific reduction figure, but only if a study supports it.

A pet-care video ad shows the method inside a hook. It opens on a surprising pairing (clean teeth, fast), then states a fact with a simple on-screen chart: most owners do not clean their dog's teeth daily. Adding the word "daily" quietly sets a standard the viewer never considered, widening the gap without lecturing. The stakes then rise with a hedged "this could lead to problems" rather than an accusing "you are", which protects belief and compliance.

## Common mistakes
- Equating "fact" with "percentage" and ignoring firsts, materials and comparisons.
- Using made-up or unverified statistics; a false fact destroys more trust than an honest opinion.
- Facts that are true but irrelevant to the hook or the buyer's desire.
- Overstating certainty ("you are damaging...") where "could" is both truer and safer.
- Burying the one strong fact in a paragraph of adjectives.
