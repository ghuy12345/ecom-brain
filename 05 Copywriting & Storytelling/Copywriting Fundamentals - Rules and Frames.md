# Copywriting Fundamentals - Rules and Frames

## What it is
Copywriting turns what you know about a buyer into words that move them to act. Research decides what to say; copy decides whether it lands. This note gathers the rules, frames and structures underneath every ad, script and page. Treat it like learning a language: early drafts sound stiff, and fluency comes from repetition, not from a template or prompt.

## When to use it
- Before writing any ad, script, email or page section.
- When a strategist or a test batch underperforms and you suspect the words, not the visuals or targeting.

## How (steps)
**1. Follow six working rules.**
- *The reader only cares about themselves.* Frame each line around their problem, desire or outcome. "For two decades we've helped people like you fix X" beats a line about how long the company has existed.
- *The market is the judge.* Do not debate copy; test it. To sharpen your instincts, predict the winning variation before launch and examine why when the prediction misses.
- *Write like people speak.* Contractions, short sentences, lines opening with "And" or "But", roughly a sixth-grade reading level. "Makes your body burn fat rather than store it" beats jargon about metabolic function. See [[Clear Copy - Compress, Simplify, Say It Aloud]].
- *Length is fine; boredom is not.* Interested readers keep going while it stays engaging; still, match length to the job (see awareness below).
- *Each sentence sells the next one.* Finish lines on intrigue, lean on open loops, and leave no comfortable exit before the call to action. "Three ingredients. The first is something no rival formula contains" pulls harder than a vague line about ingredients working together.
- *Research is most of the job.* A workable ratio is two or three hours of research for each hour spent writing. If the next line will not come, go back to research.

**2. Run a quick value check.** For any ad or page, ask whether it promises a bigger result, makes that result more believable (specific proof, guarantees, demonstrations), gets there faster (how fast it works, not shipping speed) and asks for less effort or sacrifice (results while keeping what they enjoy).

**3. Stack claim frames.** Buyers quietly ask: is it new, can I manage it, what happens if it doesn't work, will anything really change? Each question maps to a frame:
- New and only
- Easy, and suitable for anyone
- Safe and predictable
- Big and fast
Weak copy answers one ("lose weight fast" is only speed). Strong copy stacks several, and never builds a claim around a trivial benefit.

**4. Do not place yourself in someone else's category.** "Like X but cheaper" or "better than other collagens" positions you as a mere alternative and surrenders the new-and-only position. Lead with a mechanism that makes you the first of something. Comparisons work mainly when a genuinely new mechanism stands behind them (see [[Unique Mechanism - Problem Cause and Solution]]).

**5. Write body copy in objection, claim, proof, benefit cycles.**
1. Objection: voice what the reader doubts.
2. Claim: your counter, ideally a mechanism.
3. Proof: a study, a demonstration or a testimonial.
4. Benefit: the emotional payoff, shown rather than told.
Repeat per major objection. Open with objections, since a cold market carries more doubt than belief, and skip clichés like "I know what you're thinking".

**6. Match length to awareness.** Unaware readers need the most copy (what is going on, why it matters, what alternatives exist, why yours). Problem-aware is long, solution-aware educates on your approach, product-aware differentiates, most-aware just needs the deal. Choose the entry stage and walk the reader through each later stage instead of leaping from "you have this problem" to "buy now". Early on, concentrate on problem-aware through most-aware; reaching the fully unaware is usually work for larger accounts. Detail is in [[Market Awareness Levels]].

## Example
A sleep-products brand starts with "Sleep better with our blackout blinds": one implied frame, no proof. A hypothetical rewrite with stacked frames: "The first blackout blind that stops every sliver of light, fitted in five minutes without a drill, and thousands of parents say their toddler slept through by week one." That covers new (first), big (every sliver), easy (no drill), fast (week one) and safe (other parents got it). The body then cycles: "Blinds never fit our windows" (objection), "these press into the frame" (claim), a short fitting demo (proof), "the first morning you wake when you choose" (benefit). Verify any figure before it ships.

## Common mistakes
- Writing about the company, its awards or its mission rather than the reader.
- Judging by personal taste, or blaming the platform when the ads simply fail.
- Formal, essay-style English.
- Trimming copy on the belief that nobody reads.
- Telling benefits ("more energy") instead of showing them.
- Single-frame claims and "alternative to X" category language.
