# Getting Better at Copywriting

## What it is
Copywriting is a skill, not a template, and it is learned like a language: awkward at first, fluent after enough repetitions. This note covers how to build it on purpose: judging your own work, what to practise daily, where original ideas come from, and a simple process that stops you skipping steps.

The skill matters more now, not less. When everyone writes with AI, AI output becomes the average, and copy that once looked good becomes ordinary. The floor has risen. What sets top performers apart is people who use AI *and* recognise good copy, because only they can steer and judge the output.

## When to use it
- Starting out as a copywriter, strategist or founder writing your own ads.
- High output, few breakthroughs.
- Your copy sounds increasingly like your competitors'.
- You depend on prompts or templates and cannot say why a line works.

## How (steps)
**1. Calibrate taste with results.** Take ego out of judging copy. Preferring one headline is a guess until the market votes, so let data close the loop. Liking an ad because it converts is fair; calling an ad great while it fails to convert is a contradiction. Drill: write down your predicted winner before each test and study the misses.

**2. Practise three things a day,** ideally for about 90 days, or whatever stretch you can genuinely keep up:
- **Read** one piece of copy closely. What does it communicate, and why is it easy to grasp? Weak copy teaches as well. See [[Copy as Emotional Subtext]].
- **Write** a page with intent: an ad, a page section, a script, a post.
- **Generate one ad idea.** Draw on customer research, other brands' ads, your own and teammates' ads, marketing books, or anything that halted your scroll. Prompts: what made me stop? What would I change? What tipped me into buying? If the whole niche says one thing, what happens if we say the opposite? Which objection does nobody address, and could we open with it?
Analysing, executing and ideating are the three activities behind ads that scale. Keep a swipe file of hooks and winners along the way.

**3. Guard the hour.** Set aside a focused hour every day when your mind is sharpest, with distracting apps and the ad dashboard blocked. Some days it flows and some days it is a grind; write regardless. Readers never see how long it took.

**4. Leave room for boredom.** Distinctive ideas come from your own mind, not from endless consumption. Boredom switches on the brain's default mode network, which links unrelated memories and ideas, and those links are original because they are yours. Most people now avoid that state: in a widely cited experiment, given only a button that delivered a painful shock as the alternative to sitting alone with nothing to do, a reported 80% pressed it. Habits that help: delay the phone in the morning, no screens at meals, walk without the phone, use app blockers, sit quietly for a few minutes a day. The aim is to stay with hard thinking longer.

**5. Rework headlines over several days.** Draft it, tighten it with fewer and stronger words, return the next day asking what it must achieve, and rework again.

**6. Write prose first, script second.**
1. Research for ideas.
2. Decide the awareness stage, which shapes the format.
3. Write the argument as prose, moving from problem to solution.
4. Convert that prose into a video script.

**7. Write copy, not content.** Content fills the ad slot with something informative or entertaining. Copy aims to plant or strengthen a position in the buyer's mind and change what they do. Hitting a weekly creative quota is a reasonable start; depth is what turns content into copy.

**8. Study the right examples.** Brands that scale through direct response with no visible mechanism to demonstrate have to excel at story and relatability, so they make the best teachers. Products with obvious visual proof barely need copy; supplements and skincare depend on shifting beliefs. Older direct-response ads and the classic books are rich sources: Claude Hopkins' *Scientific Advertising*, Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*, *Ogilvy on Advertising* and Kyle Milligan's *Take Their Money*. Native-level English is not required if the writing is clear.

## Example
A creative strategist at a skincare brand writes fifteen scripts a week by feeding a template into an AI. Results are average and sound like every competitor. They switch to a 60-minute daily block: annotate one classic ad, write a page of prose for a live concept before scripting it, and log one idea from reviews. A couple of months later their predictions are sharper and their ideas come from reviews and quiet walks rather than competitor swipe files.

## Common mistakes
- Debating copy instead of testing it.
- Leaning on templates, cloned formats or AI without understanding why lines work; borrowed skillsets fade fast.
- "Me-too" copy from cloned funnels, leaving price as the only reason to choose you.
- Constant consumption with no room to think.
- Scripting before the argument is clear.
