# Common Ad Messaging Mistakes

## What it is
When an ad flops, the cause is usually the message rather than the targeting or the account structure. The same handful of errors turn up again and again, and almost all of them trace back to two roots:
- **Arguing with logic when the reader needs to feel something.**
- **Staying vague when specifics would land.**

This note lists eleven of the most frequent messaging errors and the fix for each. Use it to audit ads that are failing and as a pre-flight check on a new batch.

One idea runs through several fixes: buyers instinctively look for the biggest result for the smallest effort, as soon as possible.

## When to use it
- Auditing a cold-traffic ad that gets impressions but no traction. Check it for logic-first selling and for telling rather than showing before anything else.
- Before shipping a new concept.
- Reviewing scripts from creators, editors or an AI draft.
- When you cannot tell whether a test failed because of the idea or the execution.

## How (steps)
**1. Selling with logic.** Cold, top-of-funnel copy should lead with emotion. Features and reasoning do their job further down, once the viewer cares and knows the product, by justifying the purchase. Fix: before writing, map four layers of desire for the persona: functional (make the problem stop), emotional (the feeling they are after), identity (how they want others to see them) and existential (the fear underneath it all).

**2. Chasing a desire nobody has.** Copy can only channel a want that already exists at scale. If no one wants it, the problem is product-market fit, not wording. See [[Mass Desire - Channel It, Don't Create It]].

**3. Leading with features.** Translate each feature into what it does for the buyer before it goes near a script. Detachable shorts on a jumpsuit become "quick, easy loo trips at a festival".

**4. Making it sound hard.** Present the effort as small while keeping it true. Three capsules daily for three months becomes "a seven-second morning habit".

**5. Pointing at the far future.** People treat their distant future self almost as a different person. Move the quickest benefit forward: count in hours or days rather than months.

**6. Overcomplicating the language.** Keep the reading level around fifth or sixth grade, however educated the audience. Read scripts out loud and mark any line a child could not follow straight away. See [[Clear Copy - Compress, Simplify, Say It Aloud]].

**7. Being too clever.** Clarity wins over cleverness. Wordplay that needs decoding spends attention you have not earned.

**8. Telling, not showing.** Use literal or metaphorical visuals, sensory language ("smooth", "silky") and concrete scenes ("taking the stairs without a wince" rather than "knee pain goes away").

**9. Staying vague.** Detail makes viewers feel understood and sparks curiosity. "Down 7 lb in a week" beats "lost weight", and precise hooks tend to improve hold rate and click-through.

**10. Selling several things at once.** Keep to a single big idea. Check: could the persona sum up the ad in a handful of words? Write that one-sentence idea down before shipping. If you cannot, simplify.

**11. Reinventing the wheel.** Novelty for its own sake is a trap. Look at which pains, benefits and objections keep working for competitors, take apart how they phrase them, and build from there.

## Example
A hair-supplement brand targets new mothers dealing with postpartum shedding. Its first ad lists ingredients and a twelve-week timeline: logic, features, a distant payoff and visible effort.

Mapping the four desire layers changes the brief. Functional: stop the shedding. Emotional: feel attractive again. Identity: be seen as a confident, put-together parent. Existential: the fear that the woman she was before the baby has gone.

The rewrite opens on the emotional and existential layers (a mother noticing hair in the shower drain and missing her old self), shows a concrete near-term win (less hair in the brush within the first weeks, stated only if the brand can support it), reframes the routine as "two gummies with your morning coffee", and holds one idea throughout: getting her hair, and herself, back. Ingredients move to the landing page, where they justify the decision.

## Common mistakes
The eleven above are the mistakes. Two habits make them worse:
- Reworking campaign structure or targeting when the message is at fault.
- Fixing all eleven in one test, so you cannot learn which change mattered.
