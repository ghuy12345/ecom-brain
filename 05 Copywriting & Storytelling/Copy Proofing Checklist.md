# Copy Proofing Checklist

## What it is
A checklist of 14 questions to run over a finished draft so weak copy gets caught before launch. Use it to proof, not to write. A strong ad will not tick every item, and some winners skip several, but copy that fails most of them is rarely worth running.

The 14 questions fall into five passes: who it is for, how it opens, how specific and believable it is, whether it is distinct from rivals, and how it reads.

## When to use it
- After cutting and readability edits, before a brief goes to an editor or designer.
- When reviewing a batch of statics or hooks from a teammate or an AI draft.
- When an ad gets attention but no sales and you suspect the claim is generic or unbelievable.
- Not for typos and layout on landing pages; that belongs to [[Landing Page QA Before Launch]].

## How (steps)
**Pass 1 - Audience and purpose**
1. Exactly who is this for: which sub-avatar, holding which beliefs?
2. Why should they care, and why does this message suit them rather than everyone?
3. Is the opening aimed at a specific group, or is it broad?
4. What is the mechanism, the reason it works?

**Pass 2 - Opening**
5. Does the opening create a gap the reader wants closed, spark an emotion and carry real stakes?

**Pass 3 - Specificity and believability**
6. Is there a concrete detail, number or scene where an abstract phrase used to be? Replace "high quality" with the fact that makes it quality.
7. Could a sceptic check the claim? Opinions ("the best", "amazing") invite doubt; observable facts let the reader reach the conclusion themselves.
8. Where does authority come from? Authority moves beliefs more than almost anything else. It matters most in crowded, sophisticated markets, when you lack a clear unique mechanism, and when results are slow or hard to see, as with supplements. Sources include an expert, a mechanism people can see working, deep category knowledge shown in the content, a comparison device, or a large volume of creators and repeat buyers vouching for it. One ad can skip it; the funnel as a whole cannot.

**Pass 4 - Distinctiveness**
9. Could a rival paste their product into your line and have it still work? If so, it is generic.
10. Search the exact headline in quotes. If several brands already use it, rewrite.
11. Does it imply rather than assert, leaving the reader to draw the conclusion?

**Pass 5 - Delivery**
12. Are emotional valence and intensity right for this audience?
13. Is it near a sixth-grade reading level, and could it be shorter without losing meaning?
14. Would it sound natural spoken to a stranger, and is it advertising rather than a lecture?

Two quick drills sharpen the habit:
- **Recall test**: write a few abstract lines and a few concrete ones, return a day later and note which you remember.
- **Silent-pitch test**: imagine presenting the product with no words, only by showing one physical fact. Whatever you would hold up is usually the line to write.

## Example
For a hypothetical camping-stove brand, "The best stove for outdoor lovers" fails most passes: it is broad, unprovable and any rival could say it. "Boils a litre of water in under three minutes at altitude" is specific and checkable. Adding "in a stove that folds flatter than a paperback" gives a picture and an angle few rivals can copy. The first line is an opinion; the second lets the reader decide it is excellent.

A second case: a luggage brand's "built to last" becomes "survived 40 conveyor-belt drops in our own test" - a claim a reader can picture and a rival cannot borrow without running the same test.

## Common mistakes
- Writing to the checklist rather than proofing with it.
- Opinion headlines and claims every competitor also makes.
- Abstract superlatives instead of pictures.
- Assuming a mechanism that convinces one avatar convinces another; an easy-clean material means nothing to a buyer shopping on looks.
- Lecturing in long-form content and forgetting it is an ad.
- Treating a "hold rate" problem as the issue when the hook is attracting the wrong viewers.
