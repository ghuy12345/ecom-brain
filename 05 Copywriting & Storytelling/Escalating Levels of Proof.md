# Escalating Levels of Proof

## What it is
Most consumers are deeply skeptical of advertising. A cleverer hook does not fix that; layered, escalating proof does. Think of proof as a ladder with six levels. Each rung is harder to fake and more convincing than the one below. Greater skepticism, which usually comes with a more sophisticated market, means climbing higher.

The six levels, from weakest to strongest:
1. **Tell me it works.** A claim with numbers: backed by studies, loved by customers, made by experts.
2. **Show the receipts.** Put the evidence in view: the actual paper on screen, the specialist in their setting, captured reviews, buyers with the product in hand.
3. **Let others say it.** Genuine social proof: creator content, talking-head testimonials, experts in a podcast-style setting, founder ads.
4. **Show the result.** A transformation you can see: before and after, a tidier room, a meter reading that drops. It also future-paces, helping viewers imagine their own outcome. Side-by-sides with competitors, and even drawn before-and-afters, belong here.
5. **Demonstration.** The product doing the thing on camera: a repellent working live, a razor gliding without irritation. Also use demos to disprove an objection, such as a tanning lotion leaving white sheets spotless.
6. **Extreme demonstration.** Take the demo somewhere absurd, so viewers reason that if it copes there it will cope at home: a knife slicing a steel hammer, a product surviving conditions far beyond normal use.

## When to use it
- Planning proof for a new concept or landing page section.
- When ads stop the scroll but comments say "doesn't work" or "fake".
- Entering a crowded, high-sophistication market where everyone already claims the same thing.
- Choosing which ad formats to brief next.

Where proof sits on a landing page is covered in [[Proof Types and Placement on Pages]].

## How (steps)
1. **Place the market.** Estimate the sophistication stage (see [[Market Sophistication Stages]]). In early-stage markets, levels one and two are usually enough. From stage three onward, skeptics need demos or extreme demos, backed by social proof from real creators.
2. **Make level-one claims precise.** Precise figures signal that a number is real; round numbers feel invented. Use the exact customer count or study count, and only figures you can back up. Never make up a "precise" number.
3. **Put the evidence on screen.** Whenever you mention proof, show it. Narrating proof without visuals wastes it.
4. **Mix in real people.** Viewers increasingly notice synthetic AI presenters, and those ads can feel fake. Build a bank of footage from real creators, even from a single shoot, to blend into AI-assisted ads.
5. **Build at least one demo ad and one before-and-after ad** for any category where results are visible. Testimonials alone are not enough.
6. **Use demos to kill objections, not only to show benefits.** Ask what the buyer most doubts, then design a demo that answers it.
7. **Sanity-check extreme stunts.** An extreme demo must be safe and must actually prove your claim. Stunts with real-world risk can backfire badly: if the stunt exposes you, your staff or your customers to harm or fraud, any failure becomes the story instead of the product.

## Example
A hypothetical insect-repellent brand selling into a crowded, skeptical market:
- Level 1: "Tested on over 2,000 hikers" (only if true).
- Level 2: the lab report on screen, with the key line highlighted.
- Level 3: a camping creator talking through her week in the field.
- Level 4: before-and-after arms after an evening outdoors.
- Level 5: an arm with the product placed into a container of mosquitoes, live, beside an untreated arm.
- Level 6: the same demo after a swim and a long hike, showing it still works.

Early on, the brand might get away with levels one to three. As competitors copy the claims, the demo and extreme demo become the ads that still convert.

A skincare brand could use level five to answer an objection rather than show a benefit: rubbing the product onto white fabric to prove it does not stain.

## Common mistakes
- Hoping a sharper hook will overcome doubt that only stronger proof can fix.
- Narrating proof with nothing on screen.
- Going fully synthetic with creators.
- Using only testimonials in a visible-result category.
- Round, made-up-sounding numbers, or worse, invented precise ones.
- Extreme stunts that are unsafe or that do not actually prove the claim.
