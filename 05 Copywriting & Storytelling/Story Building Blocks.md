# Story Building Blocks

## What it is
A story is conflict that produces change. Add even a little conflict to flat writing, sometimes a single word that makes the reader wonder "why?", and it starts turning into a story. This note covers what an ad story is made of, a quick test for whether you have one, and where to find stories worth telling.

Three terms:
- **Positioning**: what you do and your place in the market.
- **Story**: the reason behind it, which strengthens the position.
- **Storytelling**: planting that reason in the buyer's mind.

One prerequisite comes first: real category knowledge. Know the problem well enough that you could fill a book. Writers short on knowledge fall back on adjectives, and no technique or AI tool fixes that. If the words will not flow, research more.

## When to use it
- Writing story-led scripts, native ads and long image ads.
- Turning a winning claim or a review into a narrative.

## How (steps)
**1. Build in five elements.**
- **Change.** Every story comes down to something shifting. People notice change because it may mean danger or opportunity. Show one precise moment of change and suggest more is on the way.
- **Withheld information.** Reveal most of the story and keep a slice back, roughly 80/20. Curiosity peaks when readers half-know what is happening; it fades when they are lost or certain. Create it with a question or contradiction, by breaking the category's pattern, or by hinting at a secret about to be revealed. Open on an unexpected change, an information gap, or both.
- **Stakes and drama.** People are pulled toward the unusual, and higher stakes make a better story (see [[Raising the Stakes]]).
- **Tension and suspense.** Open tension, hold it, then release it. Link lines with the logic of "but" (a turn) and "so" (a consequence) rather than "and"; a chain of "and" reads like a list. The words themselves can stay unwritten; they describe the connection.
- **Cause and effect.** Readers want the reason someone did something reckless, risky or remarkable. Clear causes let them bring their own memories and wants into the story.

**2. Run the seven laws.** Break one and you probably lack a story:
1. Without conflict there is no story.
2. Without change there is no story.
3. Without stakes there is no story.
4. Without turns and consequences ("but", "so") there is no story.
5. Without people there is no story. A customer review is the easiest entry point.
6. Without the ordinary there is no story. Begin with something small and familiar and show it growing large; do not lead with a giant number.
7. Once you start explaining, the story is gone. Replace the explanation with an example.

**3. Find a story to tell.** Ask of your product:
- What does everyone wrongly believe about it?
- What about it might frustrate people?
- Which story proves you differ from rivals?
- What angered you enough to make it?
- What would competitors rather customers never learned? Transparency angles live here.
- Who should not buy it? Naming them builds identity and draws the right people.
- What is going on in your category at the moment?

A single fact has three possible framings: a flat brand statement ("all-natural ingredients"), a market story (a surprising truth about the category, such as what really tops the ingredient list of most chocolate bars), or a brand story about your own origin or struggle. Only the last two spark curiosity.

**4. Mine reviews for a phrase, not a quote.** Pasting a review onto an ad rarely works because the quote alone is seldom gripping. Export your reviews, ask an AI model to score them for story potential, then pull the single striking perspective from the best ones and build outward. Review mining itself is covered in [[Review Mining - Your Reviews and Competitors' One-Star Reviews]].

**5. Fit the product into the story.** Ask where the product sits in the avatar's life, and in a story they already care about.

## Example
A garage-cleaning vacuum's review mentions sorting out a rat problem and getting the garage back. The mined phrase is "got my garage back." The story: rats took over, and one vacuum reclaimed the space.

A memory-blanket brand illustrates the people law. A blanket stitched from old T-shirts is a product; a blanket stitched from a late son's shirts is a story. It also illustrates the ordinary law: inviting buyers to send a few old shirts beats boasting about millions processed.

A luggage brand obeys the explaining law by filming a pen forcing a zip open rather than describing weak zips.

## Common mistakes
- Writing stories before you know the category.
- Joining lines with "and" until it becomes a list.
- Opening with big numbers instead of a small, relatable moment.
- Explaining instead of showing an example.
- Pasting raw reviews onto creative.
- Treating prompts as a stand-in for craft; you need a good story, not merely a story.
