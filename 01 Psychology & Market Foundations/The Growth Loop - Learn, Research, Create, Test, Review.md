# The Growth Loop - Learn, Research, Create, Test, Review

## What it is
Ecommerce growth comes mostly from marketing and ad creation, not from media buying or finding a magic product. The whole job fits in one loop:

1. **Learn** - build a knowledge base so you can see what you currently cannot.
2. **Research** - find out what people want and why.
3. **Create** - ads, pages, offers or products that show how you deliver it.
4. **Test** - launch in one campaign; spend is a sign of promise, no spend is a sign it is not.
5. **Review** - make educated guesses about *why* each result happened and write them down.
6. **Repeat** - either go back to research or use what you learned to produce the next batch.

Many operators only cycle create and test, sometimes research, and skip the review. Closing feedback loops is one of the most reliable habits behind brands that keep growing. The loop is also the cure for overwhelm, which usually comes from absorbing too many concepts at once. Instead of consuming more, find which step you are on.

Detailed ad-review methods live in [[Feedback Loops - Learning Why an Ad Worked]]; this note is the overall system.

## When to use it
- Starting out and unsure what to do each day.
- Feeling overwhelmed by tactics.
- Going weeks without finding a winning ad.

## How (steps)
**Run the tight loop by default.** Create, test, review, repeat. Pick a reasonable test structure, commit to it and run it rather than redesigning it every week.

**Step back only when the loop stops producing (diagnostic ladder)**
1. *No winners:* spend more time on review. Most failures are execution flaws, and review is where you find them. Put your ad next to a genuinely good one and find the difference.
2. *Still no winners:* go back to research. Do competitors have a better product? Are you speaking to the right person? Is this the right channel? Is the offer broken? If the offer is broken, even excellent ads cannot save it.
3. *Still nothing:* go back to learning. Re-reading foundational material after real experience reveals far more than the first pass did.
For product tests specifically: if one product fails, try a different product; if that fails too, research a different category.

**A 30-day beginner routine**
1. Get the ad account and business manager configured correctly.
2. Create a single offer strong enough to convert.
3. Make one fresh ad concept per day on three to five days each week. A concept means a genuinely different idea, not a small tweak.
4. Review every ad: the result, the lesson and the change you will make next time.
5. Study ads that are clearly winning to calibrate your eye. Borrowing how proven ads are *structured* is a fair way to learn at the start.
6. Spend roughly 90% of your time away from the ad dashboard, on research, creative and offers.
7. Keep going. A winner in the first month is possible but not the norm; success in month one means knowing what to do and then doing it.

## Example
Say a home-fitness brand launches five concepts in a week and none spends. Instead of switching campaign structures, the founder reviews each against a competitor ad that clearly works and notices the competitor opens with a specific frustration while theirs opens with a product feature. The next batch fixes that. If two more weeks produce nothing, the founder steps back to research and discovers the core buyer is not gym-goers but parents with no time, which reshapes the next round of creative.

"Winning" is relative: any ad that tips you into profit or lifts you to the next level, say from 10k to 15k a month, counts.

## Common mistakes
- **Studying without acting.** Consuming content all day feels productive but is not; only action generates feedback.
- **Treating learning as one-off.** Familiar material reads very differently once you have real experience.
- **Constantly switching tools or structures** instead of committing.
- **Demanding both more depth and more simplicity at the same time.**
- **Looking for answers in media buying** when the issue is creative, offer or research.
- **Avoiding discomfort.** Difficulty is a sign of growth, and it keeps competition thin.
