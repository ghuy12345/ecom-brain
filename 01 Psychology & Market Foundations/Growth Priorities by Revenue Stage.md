# Growth Priorities by Revenue Stage

## What it is
Each revenue or spend level needs a different skill set. The jump from a small to a large daily budget is rarely about new tactics; it is about executing the known ones (ads, offers, pages, team) much better, and about changing *which* lever you pull. The broad sequence is:

**volume, then intent, then systems, then team, then mindset.**

The exact revenue boundaries vary by category and business model, so treat the ranges below as approximate. This note follows on from [[Staged Growth for a New Brand - Offer, Ads, Then Journey]]; creative-system specifics by spend are in [[Creative Strategy by Spend Stage]].

## When to use it
- Setting quarterly priorities.
- Checking whether you are working on the right problem for your size.
- Deciding what to hire or delegate next.

## How (steps)
**Check the prerequisites for large scale first**
- Unit economics must work. Very low order values make paid acquisition extremely hard; on Meta in the US, an order value in the region of $60 or more gives you room.
- The market must not be in decline. You do not need a special niche, a problem-solving product or repeat purchase, though repeat purchase helps.
- Word of mouth changes the maths: if each customer brings another, your effective acquisition cost roughly halves. That makes customer experience a growth lever in its own right.

**Stage priorities (rough estimates only, not benchmarks)**
1. **Starting out to roughly low six figures a month - volume and skills.** Run enough product and creative tests for something to break through. Do not waste tests on low-value questions (bid strategy minutiae). Learn the fundamentals: awareness, desire, research. Log every creative, why you made it, who it targets and what you learned.
2. **Low six figures to low millions a month - intent.** More volume is no longer better. Compare, hypothetically: 30 creatives at a 20% hit rate produce six winners; a 1% hit rate on 100 gives one. Every creative needs research and a hypothesis behind it. Track winners per month and cut volume if hit rate drops. Useful levers here: landing pages built for specific winning ads (a winning page can lift spend like a winning ad), seeded creator content turned into retained creators (reported hit rates run about 15% for raw seeded content against about 5% for produced images and videos), and formats that already earn organic engagement in your niche carrying your own message.
3. **Around half a million to a few million a month - systems.** Formal tracking and feedback loops, small pods (media buying, CRO, creator content) that share learnings in one loop, better team or agency choices (vet heavily), and careful expansion into related products for the same customer.
4. **Several million a month and up - team, focus and mindset.** Lean into whatever only you can do and hand off everything else. Recruit and hold on to top performers, tie their incentives to the brand's goals, develop the solid middle, hire on culture and expect new people to take time to ramp up. At the top end the constraint becomes limiting beliefs, focus and a written plan.

**Avatar depth by stage**
- Commit to one core avatar until roughly the low millions a month, so every learning builds on the last. Bring in more avatars when addressable market genuinely caps growth.
- Hold off on separate per-avatar campaigns until spend is very large; let the platform sort delivery before that.

**A simple decision filter.** Anything that does not move the current goal forward gets dropped.

## Example
Say a supplement brand at a mid six-figure month is producing 100 creatives a month with a low hit rate and feels busy but flat. Under the stage view, it is in the intent stage: it cuts to around 30 creatives, each with written research and a hypothesis, logs every result, and builds two landing pages matched to its best ads. Winners per month rise while workload falls. Only later, once spend plateaus because the core avatar is saturated, does it add a second avatar.

## Common mistakes
- Aiming at targets several stages ahead and copying what far bigger brands do.
- One-dimensional growth: only working on creative, or only on media buying.
- Not tracking, so successes and mistakes cannot be repeated.
- Producing junk volume once intent matters more.
- Getting distracted after early wins by untested tactics and noise.
- Blaming the landing page by default when growth stalls; new creative and new offers usually drive the biggest jumps.
