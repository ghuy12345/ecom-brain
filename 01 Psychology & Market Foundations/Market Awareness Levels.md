# Market Awareness Levels

## What it is
Market awareness describes how much a prospect already understands about their problem, the possible fixes, and your product specifically. The model comes from Eugene Schwartz's Breakthrough Advertising and splits prospects into five levels:

1. **Unaware** - the problem or desire is not on their radar. At most there is a vague irritation with no known cause (stairs feel harder than they should).
2. **Problem aware** - knows something is wrong ("my knee aches on the stairs") but has not looked into fixes.
3. **Solution aware** - knows categories of fixes exist (braces, physio, surgery) but has not settled on one, or cannot yet see why yours wins.
4. **Product aware** - familiar with your kind of product, possibly your brand, but is not yet convinced it will work for them.
5. **Most aware** - knows your product and what it does but has not bought. They may be waiting for the right price, or they may simply not trust you yet.

The level decides where your copy has to start. It does not decide where it ends: every ad should carry the prospect from its entry level through the remaining levels to the offer.

## When to use it
- Before writing any hook, headline or page, to choose the opening.
- When reviewing an ad that gets attention but no sales: often the copy starts at the wrong level.
- When planning which audiences to go after first. Higher-awareness prospects are the easiest sales; most brands do not need unaware prospects until serious scale.

## How (steps)
1. **Diagnose the entry level.** Ask what the viewer must already know for your first line to make sense. A useful test: copy that states the problem outright assumes a reader who is at least problem aware.
2. **Do the job for that level:**
   - *Most aware:* sell. Make a strong offer, lower the risk, make buying easy, then get out of the way.
   - *Product aware:* put your product in the best light. Build trust, stack benefits, answer remaining objections and future-pace how easy the result will be.
   - *Solution aware:* show the benefits you offer that other approaches lack. Where another approach promises the same thing, show how yours does it better, backed by reviews and stories.
   - *Problem aware:* describe the problem so precisely that the viewer thinks "that's me", then introduce the fix. Go narrow and specific, but do not dwell so long that people relive the pain; mix in relief early and test how long to stay there.
   - *Unaware:* find the market through a core emotion, name that emotion, explain the problem behind it, then present the solution, product and offer. The goal is to make them problem aware.
3. **Match length to level.** The less aware the viewer, the more ground you must cover, so the copy runs longer. Most-aware copy can be very short.
4. **Walk the rest of the ladder.** Wherever you enter, move through each remaining level inside the same piece.
5. **Use comparison to set the level deliberately.** Comparing your product with others of the same type speaks to product-aware buyers; comparing it with another kind of solution altogether (a balance board versus an exercise bike) speaks to solution-aware buyers.

## Example
Take a sports-bag brand. A problem-aware ad spends its first stretch stacking small, familiar frustrations: gear that goes missing, a forgotten skate, a damp sock, the smell. Then each frustration is paired with the feature that fixes it. Now imagine a brand selling easy-on footwear for people with limited mobility running a genuinely unaware ad: viewers know their condition but have never considered that struggling with shoes could be fixed. The ad raises that problem slowly, then the fix. As a general rule, comments like "I had no idea these existed" are a signal you reached unaware people.

## Common mistakes
- **Mislabeling problem aware as unaware.** A market that simply puts up with a known problem is problem aware. Agitate the pain or show a new solution instead of writing a long education piece.
- **Assuming people know less than they do.** "Are you tired?" is rarely an unaware opener; most people already know sleep products exist.
- **Forcing a level.** Write naturally, then check where the hook starts. A good follow-up question for a working ad is how it would read one level earlier.
- **Stopping at the entry level.** An ad that only describes the problem, or only states the offer, leaves the prospect stuck partway.
- **Over-trusting the boundaries.** Solution aware and product aware blur in practice; pick the closest fit.
