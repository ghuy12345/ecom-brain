# Core Human Desires and Layers of Desire

## What it is
Two linked ideas about what people actually want.

**Core wants.** Drew Eric Whitman's Cashvertising groups buying motives into eight biologically wired wants and a set of weaker learned ones. The eight core wants: staying alive and living longer; good food and drink; escaping fear, pain and danger; a sexual partner; comfortable surroundings; winning or at least keeping pace with others; protecting the people you love; and the approval of others. Because these are biological and never switch off, appeals rooted in them hit hardest. Learned wants (knowledge, curiosity, cleanliness, efficiency, convenience, quality, style, saving money and getting a deal) are useful supporting levers but rarely the main hook.

Desire works as tension: an unmet need creates tension, tension creates desire, desire drives action. People choose with their feelings and defend the choice with reasons. Watching someone else get what they wanted is satisfying in itself, which explains much of the pull of stories and testimonials.

**Five layers of desire.** Picture an iceberg. Visible surface wants sit on top; deeper, mostly unconscious fears and hopes sit underneath. The same product can be sold at several layers, and the right one depends on the persona's awareness and skepticism.

This note does not cover trigger words (see [[Power Words and Emotional Trigger Words]]) or mass desire (see [[Mass Desire - Channel It, Don't Create It]]).

## When to use it
- Choosing which want an ad should hit first.
- Building a full set of angles for one persona instead of a single message.
- Making copy vivid enough that the buyer imagines owning the product.
- Deciding whether a fear-based message is appropriate.

## How (steps)
**Run a persona through the five layers, from surface to deep**
1. *Practical* (the surface ask): "I want my hair back". Best for problem-aware audiences in low-skepticism markets, where a direct claim is enough.
2. *How it works*: they want a particular route to the result (more keratin, a healthier scalp). Suits solution-aware buyers in mid-sophistication markets who distrust generic claims.
3. *Feeling*: the feeling once it is solved (no more frustration or embarrassment). Works at any level and becomes more important as skepticism rises and hope fades.
4. *Self-image*: the person they hope to become, or to be seen as. Raises the stakes; useful for reaching unaware audiences and reviving hopeless, late-stage personas.
5. *Deepest stakes*: decline, safety, whether change is even possible. Reserve for unaware audiences or the most jaded personas.

**Install a mental movie**
1. Name the want the product satisfies.
2. Swap vague words for concrete, sensory ones, so a named action happens to a named object.
3. Add vivid detail until the reader can see the scene without effort.
4. Test: could a stranger sketch what you wrote? Buyers always try a product in their imagination before they try it for real.

**Build a fear appeal (all four parts, or none)**
1. Make the threat concrete and personal enough that the reader genuinely worries.
2. Give a specific recommendation that removes it.
3. Show the recommendation is effective.
4. Convince the reader the step is within their power: simple, affordable and available.

## Example
Take a skin-soothing balm for razor bumps on the bikini line. Practical: clear the bumps. How it works: a calming formula that works on the irritated follicle. Feeling: feel confident in swimwear this summer. Self-image: be a woman who feels attractive and at ease. Deepest stakes: feeling attractive enough to be loved. A brand could test the first three on cold traffic and keep the last two for audiences that have given up on the category.

## Common mistakes
- Opening with your credentials or specs rather than what the buyer gets.
- Fuzzy copy that gives the reader nothing to picture.
- Hooking on a learned want when a core want could do the job.
- Fear without a credible, doable fix; too much fear paralyses instead of motivating.
- Inventing new fears instead of tapping existing ones. Reach for fear only when your product really removes the threat, and never inflate the harm. Deadlines and scarcity are gentle versions of fear; they only work when the offer shows an obvious way to avoid missing out.
- Stopping after belief. People still need an easy next step to act.
