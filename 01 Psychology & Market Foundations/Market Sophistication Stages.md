# Market Sophistication Stages

## What it is
Market sophistication measures how much your market has already been exposed to: the number of comparable products people have come across, the promises they have been told and the products they have tried for themselves. As exposure piles up, skepticism rises. When people call a market "saturated", they usually mean it is highly sophisticated.

The model comes from Eugene Schwartz's Breakthrough Advertising. It describes the market as it is; you cannot split-test your way to a different stage, and it is not the same thing as awareness (how close one person is to buying).

When every option looks alike, buyers pick almost at random. An option that clearly differs, whether in branding, mechanism or presentation, becomes the natural pick, like one black shirt on a rail of identical white ones. Knowing the stage tells you what "different" needs to look like.

## When to use it
- Before writing claims for a new product or a new market.
- When ads with a strong-sounding hook still do not sell.
- When one country or segment performs and another does not; the weaker one may simply be more sophisticated.

## How (steps)
**The five stages in order**

| Stage | Market history so far | Typical claim |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing like it exists yet | Make the plain claim, clearly and simply |
| 2 | Has heard the claim from someone else | Enlarge the claim: bigger, faster, more |
| 3 | Has heard every version of the claim | Bring a new mechanism: a fresh route to the familiar result |
| 4 | The mechanism is familiar now | Enlarge the mechanism: easier, faster, cheaper, fewer drawbacks |
| 5 | No longer believes claims at all | Identification: sell the identity and ideal life, mostly through visuals and emotion |

The key distinction: stage 2 makes the *claim* bigger, while stage 4 makes the *mechanism* bigger. Stage 1 is rare; newer categories often sit at stage 2, and most ecommerce sits between 3 and 5.

The cycle loops. A late-stage market returns to stage 3 as soon as a genuinely new mechanism appears, or resets to stage 1 with a truly new claim or product.

**Diagnose your stage**
1. Take stock of your own product: its mechanisms, the problems it addresses and anything distinctive about it.
2. Write down what each direct competitor offers and promises.
3. Write down the other routes people take to the same result, beyond rival products. For body composition that includes gyms, personal trainers and surgery.
4. Collect competitor ads and ads for those alternative routes in one place, so you see the feed as the prospect sees it and notice which brands pop and which disappear.
5. Scan the collection for claims that keep repeating. A claim many brands already make is spent; you cannot win by saying it louder.
6. Decide the stage, then make a point of looking unlike everyone else.

Exact stage placement matters less than recognising whether you are in an early or a late-stage market. For strategy in late-stage markets see [[Responding to High Market Sophistication]].

## Example
The mobile phone's history shows all five stages. The first device that could send email simply named that ability. Rivals then enlarged the promise around connection. The touchscreen arrived as a new mechanism. Later generations enlarged the mechanism with lines like "twice as fast at half the price". At the luxury end, watch and sportswear brands barely feature the item and sell the person you become; an ad that gives you goosebumps while hardly showing the product is a stage 5 ad.

A smaller hypothetical: imagine a heated eye mask brand launching on "soothes tired eyes in minutes" when several competitors already say the same thing. The same mask had an auto shut-off timer, an indicator light for heat setting and a washable cover; any of those could have been named and built into a mechanism story.

## Common mistakes
- Going live with an appealing idea before checking what rivals already claim.
- Believing your claim or mechanism is original before reviewing competitor ads.
- Confusing sophistication with awareness, or trying to "test" which stage you are in.
- Over-analysing the exact stage instead of acting on the obvious read.
- Claiming a format as a mechanism (a gummy, a spray). Buyers care what it does, not its shape.
