# The Psychological Layers a Converting Ad Must Pass

## What it is
Most ads lose at the level of psychology well before budget or media buying come into play. Think of a converting ad as a sequence of seven gates. The viewer has to get through each one, in order, before the next can work. Weak ads often clear only the first couple; strong ads clear most of them.

1. **Camouflage** - it does not look like an ad.
2. **Relatability** - the viewer thinks "this is for me".
3. **Pain over pleasure** - it leads with what they are losing.
4. **A precise emotion** - one specific feeling, landed fully.
5. **Identity** - it shows the person they become.
6. **Mechanism** - it answers "why this product?"
7. **Risk reversal** - it takes away the final reason to refuse.

Because the gates are ordered, an early failure makes everything after it irrelevant. Gates 1 and 2 are where most ads die.

## When to use it
- Auditing your own ad before launch or after it underperforms.
- Breaking down a competitor's winner to see which gates it clears.
- Giving feedback to editors or creators in a shared vocabulary.

## How (steps)
Ask seven yes/no questions, in order:
1. **Did it camouflage?** Having been let down before, viewers instinctively scroll past anything that feels like advertising. Typical giveaways: glossy studio lighting, flawless product shots, an opening logo, runway-style models and voiceovers that sound obviously recorded. Raw, creator-style footage often wins mainly because it is not flagged as an ad.
2. **Does it speak to one specific person?** Stack relatability on several dimensions: someone who looks like the buyer, everyday language, pain described at the granularity they feel it, their real setting and their life stage. "Woman in her 30s" is generic; "mother with two small children, both under five, on her third coffee after months of broken sleep" is a person.
3. **Does it lead with pain?** Losing something hurts more than an equal gain pleases. A hook about what gets worse every day you wait usually beats one promising results in 30 days, because it frames the choice as normal life versus actively losing.
4. **Is the emotion precise?** Choose a single emotion, such as loneliness, frustration, embarrassment, resentment or pride, and make each scene and line serve it until the viewer thinks "that's exactly how I feel". Angle types to choose from include practical payoffs, feelings, who the buyer wants to be, and the cost of ignoring the problem.
5. **Does it show the person after?** People buy the future self who has the product. Show the transformation, not just the item.
6. **Is the mechanism visible?** The rational mind asks why this one. Spell out how cause leads to effect. It need not be exclusive, only shown: Claude Hopkins famously turned a brewery's ordinary purifying and filtering steps into a winning campaign simply by describing them. Giving a reason works even when it is modest; psychology research on requests found that adding "because" raised compliance from about 60% to about 94%, even when the reason was weak. Naming the mechanism makes it feel proprietary.
7. **Is risk reversed?** Stack three tools: specific social proof (an exact review count, not "customers love it"), authority (credible expert endorsement or press, only if true) and a money guarantee (refunds, a generous trial or free returns).

Fix the earliest failing gate first; polishing gate 6 is pointless if gate 1 fails.

## Example
Say a skincare brand's ad opens on a studio product shot with the logo, then lists "natural, dermatologist-formulated, third-party tested". It fails gate 1 (looks like an ad) and gate 6 (those are table stakes, not a mechanism). A rebuilt version opens with a creator in her bathroom describing the exact moment she noticed her skin barrier getting worse (gates 1 to 4), shows her confident without makeup weeks later (gate 5), explains in one plain sentence how the formula repairs the barrier (gate 6), and closes on a precise review count and a 60-day guarantee (gate 7).

## Common mistakes
- Feature-led ads that speak to logic when emotion is doing the deciding.
- Generic emotions such as "make her happy".
- The product appears, but the change in the person never does.
- Proof that is too vague to trust.
- Treating the gates as a checklist of add-ons rather than a sequence that has to be passed in order.
