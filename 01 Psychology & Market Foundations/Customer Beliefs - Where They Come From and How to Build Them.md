# Customer Beliefs - Where They Come From and How to Build Them

## What it is
A belief is the sense of being sure what something means, and that sureness can attach to things that are false. Beliefs form the customer's picture of reality, and every claim you make passes through that picture as a filter. An ad lives or dies on whether the customer is ready to accept it, not only on how big its promise is; the strongest claim is not always the strongest headline.

Avatar work usually captures desires, behaviours, emotions and demographics, but often skips beliefs, which is where conversion is won or lost. Beliefs are at least as powerful as desires. Work with one and people will accept surprisingly bold claims that fit their worldview; brush against one, even slightly, and any promise becomes worthless. Beliefs also protect people's sense of not looking foolish.

One way to split the jobs inside an ad: desire decides the content, identity decides the visuals, belief decides how the argument is developed.

## When to use it
- Before writing any claim, so you do not write a great promise nobody believes.
- When a strong offer or claim still fails to convert.
- When the market has been burned by previous products.

## How (steps)
**Understand where beliefs come from**
- *Experiential*: formed by living through something (one bad meal and the restaurant is "bad"). The strongest kind.
- *Adopted*: from parents, peers, culture and marketing.
- *Intuitive*: internal, often unconscious, quietly steering behaviour.
- *Memory-based*: recall; the least important.
Beliefs often start intuitive, firm up through influence and harden through experience. They run in a cycle: belief, thoughts, feelings, behaviour, action or inaction, experience, which then reinforces the belief. This is also why sophisticated markets are hard: past experiences have hardened beliefs about the whole category.

**Find the beliefs**
1. You cannot simply ask. Read them indirectly from what people do, report and feel in reviews, forums and comments, then trace back to the belief underneath.
2. With every comment, ask: what is this person sure of, and what does their picture of the world look like?
3. Maintain an ongoing list ("it's about how much you eat, not what"; "restricting always leads to binging").
4. Before writing, answer: what does the market believe in general, what does it believe about this product, what are the common objections, and which promises would strike them as unbelievable?

**Earn belief with four techniques**
1. *Agreement ladder:* open with questions the reader answers "yes" to. It filters non-buyers and builds momentum, then you handle objections in turn.
2. *Symptoms:* describe their exact experience from inside their situation so they feel understood, instead of listing features.
3. *Authority:* state the mistaken belief plainly, recognise why people hold it, then overwhelm it with evidence and credentials. Match the weight of authority to whatever built the belief; one built by years with a trainer needs more than one ad.
4. *Logic chain:* every step leads unavoidably to the next until the product is the only sensible conclusion.

Reach for the ladder and symptoms when you need relatability, authority to override a false belief, and logic when a mechanism needs explaining.

## Example
A sleep-mask logic chain: light is what wakes you; even a thin leak of light disturbs sleep; past a point, pressure on the eyes is uncomfortable; so the ideal is total darkness with no pressure; judging that by trying masks on is nearly impossible; so a mask with contoured cups that block light without touching the eyes is the obvious answer. The reader reaches the conclusion themselves.

A diet example of the belief cycle: someone wants to slim down, yet believes "I just don't have the discipline", which leads to "there's no point", a defeated feeling, never starting, more weight gain, and a stronger belief. Unless the ad handles the willpower belief, the weight-loss promise does not land.

## Common mistakes
- **Attacking a settled belief directly.** People will doubt almost anything except their own convictions. Move the belief with evidence rather than argument.
- **Over-claiming.** Stacking sleep, focus, joints, energy, mood, skin and immunity dilutes the ad even when each claim holds up. Build one promise and strengthen its believability. If you lack proof, narrow the claim rather than adding more.
- **Too good to be true.** Even a genuinely great offer fails if it sounds impossible.
- Skipping belief research and relying on desire alone.
