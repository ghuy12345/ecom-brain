# Mass Desire - Channel It, Don't Create It

## What it is
Advertising runs on desire. A **mass desire** is a personal want held by enough people to add up to a market. Such wants are shaped by social, economic and technological shifts that dwarf any advertising spend. Copy cannot manufacture them; it can only point an existing want at your product. The idea is laid out in the opening of Eugene Schwartz's Breakthrough Advertising.

Directing existing desire is what gives ads leverage. Trying to manufacture desire turns an ad into a costly lesson that, at best, breaks even. Nor can you push against the direction the market wants to go: when car buyers moved toward compact, economical models, campaigns that kept pushing size lost despite huge budgets.

Every product is really two products:
- The **physical product**: what it is made of, its parts and specs.
- The **functional product**: the result it delivers, which is what the buyer is really paying for.

People buy the function. Physical facts never lead; they serve as evidence for the performance claim.

## When to use it
- Choosing what your main headline or hook is about. No decision in an ad matters more; the wrong desire makes everything else irrelevant.
- Explaining why the same product sells in one season and not another.
- Deciding how scalable a product or angle can be. The amount of desire you channel caps your growth.

## How (steps)
1. **Spot the desires.** Look for complaint patterns, wording in reviews and forums such as "I've been searching for" or "if only I had", successful competitors, cultural trends and where people already spend money.
2. **List the performances.** Write every result your product delivers (for a car: getting around, reliability, economy, power, status, value, novelty) and sort them under the desire each one meets.
3. **Rate each desire three ways:**
   - *Urgency/intensity*: how badly and how soon relief is needed (a migraine versus a mild headache).
   - *Staying power*: does the want keep renewing, like hunger or status?
   - *Scope*: the size of the group that feels it. This matters most for scale.
   Beginners can focus on intensity and scope first.
4. **Pick one.** Lead with the single performance that scores highest right now. A headline has room for one thought.
5. **Set the starting point by awareness.** If they know and want your product, start with it; if all they have is the desire, open on the desire; if all they feel is a fuzzy problem, open on that problem and turn it into a clear need.
6. **Use everything else as support.** Other performances and physical facts justify the price, document quality, reassure on durability, make the workings easier to picture and, most importantly, supply a new reason to believe once rivals have already used the same promise.
7. **Keep watching.** Desires rise and fall; revisit the inventory regularly and pivot when it shifts.

## Example
Desire strength works like wind. A strong desire acts as a tailwind pushing an ad along; a weak one is a headwind; stack several headwinds and nothing moves. Scope varies hugely inside one theme: toned calves is a tiny niche, gaining muscle is larger, a six pack larger again and "be healthy" is the largest of all. Bigger desires mean bigger markets but more competitors, so starting narrower can be smart.

Events can move desire overnight. Say a widely reported sports injury makes headlines: demand for protective gear can leap overnight even though nobody changed their ads, because both the scope and the intensity of the desire rose at once.

Permanent desires (health, wealth, relationships) drive long-term results; every smaller want traces back to one of them. Trending desires (a speculative investing craze, a holiday rush, a burst of new technology) bring short-term gains and fade, like a crypto-themed shirt that sells at the peak and stalls after. A smartphone serves health, wealth and relationships at the same time, which helps explain its mass demand.

## Common mistakes
- Making the factory, materials or build quality the main selling point.
- Teaching people to want something rather than locating a want they already have.
- Opening on a lesser benefit simply because it is easier to prove.
- Swimming against a shift the market has already made.
- Talking about the technology instead of the outcome; a massage gun sells relief and loose muscles, not its motor.
- Confusing desire with angle. An angle is the broader framing built on top of a desire.
