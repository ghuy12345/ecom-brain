# Staged Growth for a New Brand - Offer, Ads, Then Journey

## What it is
The three big growth levers are product, brand and marketing to a specific avatar. Product and brand are slow and hard; most small brands get further, faster, by getting better at speaking to one avatar. Plenty of unremarkable products can hit a healthy monthly revenue with strong marketing. Very large scale usually needs real product work later.

The build order for a small brand is:
1. **One strong offer** - gets you from zero to your first meaningful revenue (roughly the first $10k a month).
2. **One scalable ad** - takes you to the next level (roughly $50k a month).
3. **One optimised customer journey** - a dedicated page and flow that lifts you further (roughly $100k a month).

The figures are rough estimates, not benchmarks or promises. None of the stages depends on finding a "winning product". What to prioritise at larger revenue levels is in [[Growth Priorities by Revenue Stage]].

## When to use it
- Starting a new brand or relaunching a stalled one.
- Deciding whether to spend money on an agency, a media buyer, content or a new page.
- Diagnosing why good ads are not producing profit.

## How (steps)
**Stage 1: the offer**
1. Work out your full cost of goods. As a rule of thumb, aim for gross margin above about 60%; higher order values can tolerate a little less.
2. Rework the offer around perceived value. A higher unit price can pay for a larger bundle or a bonus; discount only if it actually drives conversion.
3. Make success feel more likely, rather than only making the product cheaper.
4. Strip friction from the page: an obvious product name and description, ingredient or nutrition labels for consumables, FAQs, shipping and returns, visible reviews, more photos and video, and a money-back guarantee.
Plain product-image ads are enough here when the offer is compelling.

**Stage 2: one ad**
1. Put money into content before any media buyer. Without content you have nothing to advertise.
2. Source creators through product seeding or modest per-video fees; move the best to a retainer. Filming content yourself is a perfectly good zero-cost start.
3. Give creators the product and ideas rather than rigid scripts at this stage.
4. Hire a creator first, then an editor. Interview many candidates, run a short test with a real brief, and make their brief about producing winning ads, not simply cutting footage.
5. Run two to four concepts a week with a few variations each, review each ad after about a week and compare it with genuinely good ads.
6. A simple shape that often works: clear avatar call-out, a curiosity hook, a demonstration, then the ideal result. Keep narrowing the avatar (women, then mothers, then mothers starting strength training) until one responds.

**Stage 3: the customer journey**
1. Stop sending ad traffic to the homepage or a generic product page.
2. Build the offer first, research as you would for ads, and diagnose what fails on the current page.
3. Find the ads and angles in your account with scale potential.
4. Build a page that matches the avatar and angle those ads call out, with hand-picked reviews and claims backed by data. How fast you can clone pages matters more than which builder you use.
5. Below roughly $100k a month, formal split tests rarely reach significance. Instead send your top ads to the new page in the same campaign and let spend decide; see [[Testing Landing Pages Through Ad Spend]].

## Example
Say a snack brand sells a trial pack around $55 and its ads return under 1x. Without touching the creative, it switches to a bundle (buy two, get two free) at a higher list price but a lower effective cost per pack, adds clearer labels, reviews and a guarantee. Return on ad spend improves several times over. The offer, not the ad, was the bottleneck. Later, an ad that refuses to scale finally breaks through when it gets a dedicated page built around the avatar's number-one pain point.

## Common mistakes
- Hiring an agency or media buyer before you have content or an offer.
- Calling an ad a winner at small daily spend; a real winner keeps working as you push spend far higher.
- Changing pages on the strength of brief split tests with too little data.
- Cloning a viral creative exactly, which teaches you nothing you can repeat.
- Assuming a failing ad means a bad strategy. Early on, poor execution is much more often the culprit.
- Skipping consistency and correction: check what works, do more of it, stop what does not.
