# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 1 - Mass Desire

## Core Argument & Structure

The first chapter sets the law the rest of the book obeys: the urge to buy originates in the market rather than in the writing. Copy cannot create desire and cannot fight it. It can only point it somewhere, and an ad that runs against it fails. Schwartz defines mass desire, in Breakthrough Advertising, as "the public spread of a private want." A market exists once enough people share a private want that selling to them pays.

Because social, economic and technical forces build desire up over years, advertising that rides an existing desire gets huge leverage: a dollar of spend can return many dollars of sales. Trying to create a desire first is education, not advertising, and returns at best what you put in. Educating an entire population is beyond any one advertiser's budget.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

**1. The forces behind mass desire.**
- *Permanent forces* (timing doesn't matter, differentiation does):
  - *Mass instincts* such as wanting to be attractive, strong or healthy. They never fade, so your job is a fresh appeal, stronger believability, and moving desire from a rival's product to yours.
  - *Mass technical problems* that stay unsolved, so people keep buying and trying. Same claim, new presentation.
- *Forces of change* (here timing decides everything):
  - *Trends* that start, peak and reverse. You need sensitivity and foresight to arrive first.
  - *Mass education* (schools, media, peer pressure, all advertising). The question is when enough people have shifted.

**2. Standing order.** Detect, inventory and chart these forces daily, then hitch products onto them.

**3. The three-stage process.**
1. *Pick the strongest desire your product can serve.* Score each candidate on urgency (intensity of demand), staying power (how often it recurs, how hard it is to satisfy) and scope (how many people share it). Every product touches two to four desires, but only one can lead. This is the most important decision in the ad.
2. *Acknowledge, reinforce or offer to satisfy that desire in one headline statement.* Meet the prospect at their current awareness: product-aware prospects get the product first, desire-aware prospects get the desire first, and people who only feel a vague problem get the problem sharpened into a specific need. Naming the product in the headline is optional.
3. *Demonstrate what the product does that fulfils that desire.*

**4. Two products in one.** The physical product (materials) and the functional product (what it does, the stream of benefits). The physical product doesn't sell, and no physical part should ever be the headline.

**5. Five jobs for physical facts,** used downstream to support the performance: justify the price; document quality; reassure that performance will last; sharpen the mental picture; and, above all, give a familiar claim a fresh reason to be believed. That last job is the point of difference, often suggested by the writer and then built in.

**6. Performance inventory.** List every performance, group them by the desire each satisfies, and feature the one with the most selling power right now. For a car: transport, reliability, economy, power, recognition, value, novelty, and hidden motives uncovered by research.

**7. One desire per ad.** Space and attention are limited; you get one glance. Every other performance only reinforces the main one. The book's image: a product offers dozens of keys, but "only one will fit the lock."

## Examples & Evidence

- Four car-industry cases: a compact car launched while buyers wanted ever longer, lower, wider cars failed despite a lavish campaign; brands riding the 1950s horsepower trend won big; a heavy safety campaign met pure indifference; and the Edsel, a decent car with good ads, died in the swing to cheap compacts. Against the tide, creative quality can't save you.
- Rolls-Royce engine isolation shows a physical fact painting the performance picture.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** creative testing on Meta discovers desires; it doesn't make them. Category creation rarely pays for small brands; demand capture does. Urgency, staying power and scope make a ready-made angle-scoring sheet. One desire per ad is truer in the feed than in print: the scroll decision takes under a second.

**Needs translating:** desire waves now rise and fall in weeks; a dying account is often a fading wave, not a creative problem. Small markets are reachable profitably, and scope shows up as audience size. "Motivation research" is now review mining plus social listening.

Desires also migrate: safety later became one.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **Mirror proof:** describe how thoroughly your process does X, then promise the product does X for the customer, with the same verb.
- **Sensory status:** never say "status"; describe three sensory moments of being admired.
