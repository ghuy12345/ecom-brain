# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 13 - Camouflage

## Core Argument & Structure

The seventh belief technique flips the direction. Earlier techniques built belief within the copy itself; this one borrows it from places in society where trust is already stored.

It rests on a learned reflex. People buy a publication because they trust it, and they assume it wouldn't run an ad that wasn't true. Repeated enough, a medium's typical phrasing begins to feel true in itself, whatever it wraps. So a medium's credibility matters more than its circulation.

Working against you is the reader's reflex for spotting ads: copy that looks like advertising triggers pushback on sight. The book's guiding idea is that an ad should fit its surroundings so smoothly that the reader never feels a jolt on the way in.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

**1. Three ways to borrow trust.**
1. *Format:* take on the host medium's look and typesetting.
2. *Phrasing:* take on the stock phrases of the medium or media type.
3. *Mood:* use an idiom that steps away from hard sell regardless of medium, either understatement or deadly sincerity.

**2. Let the medium set your ad.** Minimise the reader's gear-shift from editorial to ad by copying the medium's headline style, illustrations, subheads and spacing. One format change can lift results by half.

**3. The five newspaper adaptations.**
1. Headline in the paper's own typeface; a bolder one would flag it as an ad.
2. Two stacked subheads, an old convention the paper still used.
3. Rules around the first subhead, a small identity touch.
4. Subheads pushed hard left, like the editorial pages.
5. A line drawing instead of a photo, because the paper used no photos.
The corny, old-fashioned, plain version roughly doubled believability, response and staying power.

**4. News signals for borrowed phrasing.** A date, a place line and a byline as the copy's opener. These first lines frame the rest as a report rather than a pitch.

**5. Direct mail.** Find the mail that always gets opened immediately (refund cheques, government letters, dividend notices, confidential reports, expensive newsletters) and borrow its appearance.

**6. Radio and TV.** Borrow the news format, phrasing and sound: the newsreader's clipped delivery, an insider-analyst voice, documentary style, shaky handheld footage.

**7. Understatement.** Simple language, almost no colourful words, adjectives used more sparingly than readers expect, short sentences whose tone drops at the close. Its rarity makes it more effective, especially in long-running campaigns. Study Claude Hopkins.

**8. Deadly sincerity.** Go out of your way to point out the offer's flaws, so the benefits are believed more deeply afterwards. Powerful in crowded categories, because ordinary copy never criticises its product. It also disqualifies the wrong buyers.

## Examples & Evidence

1. **A book ad on making people like you, run two ways.** In the advertiser's own house style across a dozen-plus magazines it was mildly successful. Rebuilt to match the newspaper's format feature for feature, the same copy was hugely successful and ran for well over a year without losing pull. In effect an A/B test of format alone.
2. **A blackhead-treatment news ad** with a specialist headline, a byline and a city dateline: three news signals frame it as a report before any selling.
3. **A Volkswagen ad** about the air-cooled engine: nouns instead of adjectives, gentle self-mockery, a quiet point, then it stops.
4. **A career book ad** addressed to the reader who wants nothing less than to run the company, using phrasing that doesn't sound like advertising and openly calling itself an advertisement.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** trust is stored outside the ad and can be tapped by matching format and language. That's the principle behind native ads, feed-native creator content, deliberately rough-looking ads, text-only emails and advertorial pages. Automatic ad scepticism is stronger now, amplified by AI content; understatement and deadly sincerity stand out against polished filler. Admitted weaknesses and "don't buy this if..." hooks are established Meta patterns against fatigue. The same-copy, different-format test is as modern as ever.

**Needs translating:** the trust store has moved away from publications toward creators, tight-knit communities and each platform's native look; the "medium" to blend into is the Meta feed itself. Format conventions change monthly, so keep recalibrating. Plain-text, transaction-style email beats designed newsletters for trust and deliverability.

**Risky:** making ads truly indistinguishable from editorial is hidden advertising and breaches labelling rules and Meta's branded-content policies. Fake bylines and deceptive subject lines are misleading. Specialist authority for cosmetics must stay within claims rules. Deadly sincerity is the one variant fully compatible with disclosure rules, because it never hides that it's an ad; admitting it won't work overnight even helps with cosmetic claims.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **Report-style opener:** headline, byline-style label and dateline for an advertorial or newsletter.
- **Negation cascade:** list the problems that simply don't exist with your product, one short line each.
- **Disqualifying opener:** this isn't for most people, and that's the point.
