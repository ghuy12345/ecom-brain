# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 8 - Identification - Part 1

Part 2 covers primary images, believability bridges, examples and modern use: [[Breakthrough Advertising Ch 8 - Identification - Part 2]].

## Core Argument & Structure

The desire craft described earlier, from patent-medicine copywriters through Claude Hopkins to the mid-1950s, works only the obvious side of wanting: getting satisfaction or relief. Schwartz argues there is a second, subtler and partly unconscious dimension: the longing to express yourself. Its rise as a buying motive is, in his view, the big merchandising shift of his era.

Identification has two meanings: the customer's wish to play roles and define themselves to the world, and the active work the copywriter does to build that fulfilment into the product through copy. It's an effect you produce, not a side effect. The role is the product's beyond-functional value, added by marketing rather than engineering, and Schwartz estimates that half or more of purchases can't be explained by function alone. When products are at parity, the role offered decides the sale.

The method mirrors desire work (discover, then magnify) with one big difference: the product already carries an accepted primary image. You can't demolish it; it acts like prison walls. You have to use it as a bridge.

The chapter moves from the two-dimensions thesis, through role theory (two uses, two reasons to buy), character roles and achievement roles, the discovery-and-magnification process and its limits, then primary images, image rebuilding, believability limits, and finally where identification can be found in the physical product.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

**1. Two dimensions of longing.** Desire is open, physical and responds directly to mechanisms. Identification is quiet and partly unconscious. Identification adds to desire-building; it doesn't replace it.

**2. Two ways to use identification in copy.** Position the product as the tool for reaching a role, or make it the acknowledgement that the role has already been achieved.

**3. Two reasons behind every purchase.** Satisfying a physical want or need, and choosing a particular way of satisfying it that tells the world what kind of person you are.

**4. Two kinds of role.**
- *Character roles,* expressed as adjectives (progressive, chic, charming, well-read). The prospect creates them, they're hinted at rather than claimed, can't be tested, are ambiguous and open to fantasy.
- *Achievement roles,* expressed as nouns or titles that society confers.

**5. Three ways a product serves a character role.** It helps the buyer achieve mastery of the role (a philosophy book makes you well-read); it simplifies or speeds up that mastery (a speed-reading course); and, most importantly, it acts as a visible symbol of mastery that wins recognition (the full bookshelf). All three are beyond-functional and add reasons to buy.

**6. Why character roles go down easily.** The ad makes no explicit claim, no statement for the rational mind to vet, and no situation where the role must be proven. Because they comfort without costing anything, character-role symbolism should back up what every ad says in words.

**7. Achievement roles.** The book lists the 1966 status titles for men (executive, homeowner, high earner, good golfer, man on the way up, community chair) and women (wife first, then trendsetter, career woman, good mother, civic leader, the power behind the throne, arts patron). The mechanics still apply even though the list is badly dated: achievements must be won, kept and above all displayed. Because they're invisible, they get translated into physical symbols, and together these form a material personality that tells strangers instantly who you are.

**8. Two-stage process.**
1. *Discovery:* Which roles will the prospect accept from this product? Which do they reject for it? Which accepted role is most compelling?
2. *Magnification:* show the selected roles with such vividness and force that resisting them is hard.

**9. Limits set by the accepted image.** You can't force the market to accept an unrealistic identity. Existing attributions are rock-hard and decisive; contradicting them head-on fails. Build on accepted traits and use them as a stepping stone toward prestige. A few products have built-in prestige (sports cars, pools, diamonds); most don't, and prestige has to be constructed.

**10. The strongest role clusters** (for the 1960s American market): masculine or feminine; exciting, unique, adventurous, fun; friendly and liked; important, influential, correct; modern and up to date; and above all, successful.
