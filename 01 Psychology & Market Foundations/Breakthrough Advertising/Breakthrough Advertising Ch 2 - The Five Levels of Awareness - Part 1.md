# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 2 - The Five Levels of Awareness - Part 1

Part 2 covers the Unaware stage, examples and modern use: [[Breakthrough Advertising Ch 2 - The Five Levels of Awareness - Part 2]].

## Core Argument & Structure

Where does a headline's content come from? Not from inspiration, but from locating the market precisely. Once you know the mass desire and the product's performance, the ad is the meeting point: it starts with the market and leads it to the product.

Three questions come before any headline:

1. What mass desire is driving this market?
2. What does this audience already know about how your product meets that desire? (awareness)
3. How many comparable products have already been sold to them? (sophistication)

The first question gives you the force; the other two pin down where the market sits relative to your product. These are two independent axes.

The headline's job is narrow: stop the prospect and get them to read the next sentence. Each sentence then sells the next. A headline only needs to sell outright when the reader is already hunting for your branded product and you add a price cut.

Five stages follow, separated by walls of indifference. A headline that works at one stage fails at another, and goes stale as the market moves.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

### Naming the stages (use names, not numbers)

Always refer to a stage by its name. The book counts from the top (Most Aware = 1), while many later sources count from the bottom, so a number alone is a reliable source of confusion.

| Stage name | Book's numbering | Common reversed numbering |
|---|---|---|
| Most Aware | 1 | 5 |
| Product Aware | 2 | 4 |
| Solution Aware | 3 | 3 |
| Problem Aware | 4 | 2 |
| Unaware | 5 | 1 |

### Most Aware

The buyer knows the product, knows what it does and already wants it. The more a market knows, the fewer words you need. Mechanics: product name plus a bargain price, a few short selling points, where to buy, and a close. The "news" is price, a free bonus, fast delivery or convenience. Modern equivalent: retargeting, dynamic product ads and win-back offers.

### Product Aware

The prospect knows the product but doesn't fully know what it does, isn't convinced it works well, or hasn't heard how much better it is now. Most advertising lives here. The headline takes on one of seven tasks:

a) Reinforce desire for the product, through association with admired people or values, sharpened sensory detail, or a striking illustration.
b) Sharpen the picture of how it satisfies the desire (the classic Rolls-Royce line about the loudest noise inside the car).
c) Extend where and when it satisfies the desire (a soft drink framed as right for every season).
d) Add new proof, detail or documentation.
e) Introduce a new mechanism that meets the desire more effectively.
f) Announce a new mechanism that removes old limitations.
g) Change the product's image or mechanism entirely to escape competitors.

Task (g) is where the problem shifts from awareness to sophistication: how much exposure the market has had to similar products. In every case here, the product name appears in the headline or the logo, and everything else argues superiority; the body elaborates, documents and explains how it works. Modern equivalent: social-proof and reformulation ads.

### Solution Aware (introducing new products)

The prospect knows, or instantly recognises, that they want the result, but doesn't know a product exists that delivers it. Three steps:

1. Name the desire or its solution in the headline.
2. Prove the result can actually be reached.
3. Show your product contains the way to reach it.

From here downward, execution matters more than mechanics. Three writer qualities matter: analysis (locating the market), intuition (sensing a trend early and timing when it becomes profitable), and verbal creativity (giving emotion a focus and a goal). Verbal intensity can be dialled up: plain statement, then fresh proof, then mystery, then wonder.

### Problem Aware (products that solve needs)

The prospect has a need rather than a desire, recognises it immediately, but hasn't connected it to your product. The steps: state the need or its fix, make the need vivid, then present the product as the obvious answer. Headline types climb a ladder:

- the bare problem as a one-word question ("Corns?")
- problem plus solution
- removing a limitation
- all three combined (result without the feared method)
- a cheap substitute for an expensive or unpleasant job
- prevention
- stressing "you" when the problem is vague
- a verbal twist when the obvious solution is worn out

Modern equivalent: call-out hooks and problem-agitate-solve scripts.
