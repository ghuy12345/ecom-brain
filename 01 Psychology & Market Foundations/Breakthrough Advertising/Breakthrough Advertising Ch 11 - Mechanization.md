# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 11 - Mechanization

## Core Argument & Structure

This chapter teaches how to prove in words that the product does what you claim. Good copy lives in two places at once, on paper and inside the reader's mind, and reading is a silent conversation in which the prospect keeps pushing back with demands. If you don't answer them, the copy fails. The third demand is "how does it work?", and the answer is the mechanism: a reasoned walkthrough, in words, of how the result comes about. Claude Hopkins mastered this reason-why copy.

The question is never whether to include mechanism but how much, and awareness sets the dose. Mechanism either sits inside the ad to prove the main claim or, in saturated markets, on top as the main claim.

Schwartz then extends the idea from product to offer: price cuts need a reason too, a mechanism behind them, shown through a classic Robert Collier letter.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

**1. Three kinds of demand from the prospect.**
1. *More:* more information, more imagery, more desire ("tell me more").
2. *Proof:* they want it; now they want to know it's true ("says who?").
3. *Mechanism:* they want the result; now they want to know how it happens ("how does it work?").
Leave any of them unanswered and the copy fails.

**2. Play both roles.** You have to be writer and prospect at once, knowing exactly where each demand arises and turning the copy there. Good ads break at these points.

**3. The dose.** The variable is awareness: when the market already understands the mechanism, competitors have already paid to teach it.

**4. Three levels of mechanization.**
1. *Name it.* When the market knows and accepts the mechanism, just name it, in bold, and beat competitors on price or features. Most catalogue and retail copy only needs this.
2. *Describe it.* Needed for two reasons: the prospect doesn't understand the mechanism, or everyone shares the same mechanism, promise and price and the market is tiring. For the first case the classic shape is promise, then reason why: a strong, fast promise followed by the explanation. It's never a dull lecture; load it with promise and emotion. Structurally, the explanation sells a supporting claim, and that supporting claim proves the main one.
3. *Feature it.* When the market is highly sophisticated, promises sound identical and a price war would be suicidal, put the mechanism in the headline so it becomes the main claim.

**5. Decision rule.**
1. Mechanism known, or the claim so new it doesn't matter: a word or phrase is enough.
2. Prospects unsure how it works: explain it, in persuasive terms, until they have grounds to believe.
3. Unusually strong or dramatic mechanism, or you're out to prove superiority: sell the mechanism hard.

**6. Mechanizing a price cut.** Treat a discount as a claim: it needs its own reason. Collier's techniques:
1. A causal chain rather than a single reason (unseasonal weather led to overstock led to a markdown), building belief on belief.
2. Keep quality running alongside, so "cheap" doesn't stick to the product.
3. Reverse the order: open with the mechanism and hold the bargain claims until several paragraphs in. This gamble multiplies believability.

## Examples & Evidence

- **Rinso soap (1926)** is the model for level two: a promise headline about whiter washing without hard work, three paragraphs building desire from different angles, then, exactly where readers would start doubting it, a turn into a persuasive explanation of why the soap works.
- **Mechanism headlines** (a car running without spark plugs, a treatment without surgery) show level three.
- **A Robert Collier shirt letter** shows a fully worked justification for a price cut: a story lead about prices rising, the causal chain, and the offer late.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** the three demands, the "how much mechanism?" question, the decision rule, giving every discount a reason, and the belief-upon-belief chain. The silent conversation is now partly public: Meta comment sections spell out the demands ("does this actually work?", "source?") and work as a free diagnostic.

**Needs translating:** the turning points are measurable in hook-retention curves, and in video the "how?" moment often arrives within 15 seconds. In the feed, compress causal chains to two links. Level one lives on in shopping feeds and retargeting. Same mechanism, promise and price is the normal state of direct-to-consumer Meta, so mechanism headlines are standard.

**Risky:** the book's suggestion to invent a miracle ingredient is the riskiest advice here. Find and dramatise a real mechanism instead. Discount reasons and reference prices must be genuine.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **"Who wouldn't love [result] without all the [effort]?"** a promise that carries social proof inside it.
- **"All it takes is [product], which is a [plain category] that [mechanism verb]."**
- **"[Result] without [the usual means]"** as a mechanism claim through negation.
