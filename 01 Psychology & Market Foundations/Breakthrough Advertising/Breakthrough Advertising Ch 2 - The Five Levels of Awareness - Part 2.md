# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 2 - The Five Levels of Awareness - Part 2

Continues [[Breakthrough Advertising Ch 2 - The Five Levels of Awareness - Part 1]].

## Frameworks & Mechanics (continued)

### Unaware (the completely unaware market)

The prospect either doesn't know the desire or need, won't admit it, or can't put it into words. This is the hardest stage.

**Decay checklist: when has your market slipped toward Unaware?** Check in order:
1. Sales fall even as budgets rise.
2. The name by itself has stopped selling.
3. Saying plainly what it does has stopped working.
4. Naming the desire outright has stopped working.

**Three negative rules:**
1. Keep price out of the headline and main visual; price means nothing to someone who doesn't know the product.
2. Keep the product name out of the headline, and be careful with a big logo, which can even hurt if it feels dated.
3. Don't state outright what the product does, what desire it meets or what problem it solves. The obstacle isn't sophistication; it's indifference or the topic being unacceptable.

**All that remains is the market: the identification headline.** You call your market together and tell them who they are, defining them to themselves. The body then moves through a steady chain of images: identification, awareness of the problem or desire, the news that a solution exists, and finally a narrowing onto your product.

**The repulsion rule.** A headline has to select the product's natural buyers and turn away about as many as it draws in. A headline nobody objects to is too blunt to hit anyone. Empty shock, humour or pretty-picture headlines waste money.

**Style versus strategy.** Styles age; once overused, people see the advertising and miss the message. Strategy doesn't age: old winning ads rewritten in today's idiom could still sell.

## Examples & Evidence

Eight classic patterns for the Unaware stage:

1. **Give words to a hidden dream.** A home-study school positioned itself as a "university of the night" for ambitious workers, naming the school only in the closing lines.
2. **Exploit a hidden fear.** A coffee substitute, after health and taste angles were exhausted, wrote about why strong men break down.
3. **Lead into an unmentionable problem through an accepted image.** A women's deodorant opened on a graceful image of a woman's arm, then turned gently to the problem, relief and the mechanism.
4. **Project a desire that can't be stated.** The Marlboro tattoo campaign conveyed virility through image alone.
5. **Use a common resentment.** A TV-repair manual broadened its market by asking why TV owners hadn't been told certain facts, then reframed what a repairman really charges for (knowledge, not labour).
6. **Project an ultimate triumph.** The famous piano-course ad: the narrator is laughed at, then plays brilliantly.
7. **Show what a problem leads to.** Listerine dramatised bad breath through a third person who never marries, with the warning that friends won't tell you.
8. **Project the result of an achievement.** A correspondence school showed a husband handing his wife extra money, steering attention from effort to reward.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** the five stages work as a diagnostic grid for Meta creative strategy. "Each sentence sells the next" is now measurable through hook rate, retention curves and scroll depth. The decay checklist describes creative fatigue precisely: when cost per purchase rises with budget, check the awareness stage (does the name still sell? the function? the desire?) instead of just rotating more creatives of the same stage. The repulsion rule is how broad targeting should be run: the creative does the targeting and the algorithm follows the signals. Identification headlines live on as logo-free top-of-funnel hooks, resentment as an "enemy" angle.

**Needs translating:** Automation now handles much Most Aware work, so human effort shifts to the lower stages. Walls between stages move in weeks. Styles wear out faster than ever. The melodramatic tone of the classics is dead; copy the structure, which the book itself permits. Third-person framing of problems is not just smart but required by Meta's personal-attributes policy.

**Risky:** tobacco examples are illegal to advertise. Healing promises, fear appeals for remedies and unsupported "nine out of ten" lines break claims rules. Strike-through prices need a genuine reference price, and fully disguised ads breach labelling rules.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **One-word self-selection:** "[Symptom]?" as the entire hook.
- **Revelation frame:** "Why haven't [group] been told [this]?"
- **Triumph reversal:** they doubted or mocked, then the result silenced them.
- **Shift the consequence onto a third party** and add the "nobody will tell you" clause.
- **Knowledge reframe:** you're not paying for the work, you're paying for the know-how.
- **Hyper-specific detail as proof** instead of an adjective.
- **Accepted image, then a gentle turn** toward the taboo.
