# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 10 - Redefinition

## Core Argument & Structure

No product is perfect. Paying alone creates resistance, and drawbacks can raise it enough to block the sale. Redefinition gives the product a new definition: it says the product is this, not that. The aim is to remove a roadblock, ideally before the prospect notices it exists. Refuting would validate it; reframing makes it irrelevant.

Schwartz sorts every purchase obstacle into three types: the product seems too complicated or hard to use; it seems not important enough (its appeal is too narrow for a mass market); or it seems too expensive. Gradualization works through arranging facts, redefinition through shifting perspective; both build belief unnoticed.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

**1. Answers:** too complicated → simplification; not important enough → escalation; too expensive → price reduction.

**2. The flip (concept judo).** Recast the drawback as proof of the core benefit: a soap's medicinal smell became proof it beat body odour. Check this route first.

**3. Simplification, step by step.**
1. *Order matters.* Never mention the difficult job before what makes it easy. The first TV-repair ad put "do your own repairs" in the headline, and readers simply turned the page.
2. *The turn.* The move from the problem to the product is delicate and must happen without a jolt.
3. *Voice the fear as a promise:* you don't have to be a technician to do this.
4. *Keep the scary word* (repair) out of sight until the reframe is in place.
5. *Redefine terms:* map onto the familiar (faults as warning signs, like a runny nose), rename consistently (adjustments, not repairs), and downplay the action.
6. *Fence off the hard cases with numbers:* real breakdowns are the rare few percent.
7. *Prove ease rather than claim it,* or it reads as a copywriter getting carried away. Redefine first, then show the mechanism of the new simplicity. In Breakthrough Advertising: "innovation without acceptance is valueless."
8. *Redefine the whole field* before introducing the product.
9. *Shrink the feared field to a countable core.* The Sherwin Cody English ad reduced English to a few dozen key words, a dozen punctuation rules and a couple of dozen common mistakes, so fifteen minutes a day felt logical.

**4. Escalation, three uses.**
1. *Benefit shift:* move the benefit from a weak area to a stronger one, so the product becomes a means to a bigger end (an English course recast as a tool for persuasion).
2. *Importance hinge:* a big, valued outcome depends on your small product (a cheap part that robs an expensive car of its power).
3. *Frequency:* show the buyer needs it nearly all day, every day, opening with a truth the reader can't deny.

**5. Price reduction.** A product seems too expensive because it's compared with others in its category. Switch the comparison to a more expensive standard. Set a high-value anchor, explain the lower price as a reasoned decision, reframe as payback, and add a condition that justifies it. Never use the defensive "costs a bit more but worth it" line.

**6. The bargain vocabulary.** One spark-injector ad's price section stacks value signals: a hand-made anchor, a hypothetical unit price, an earned "low", a payback multiple, a justifying condition, favour wording ("you can have") and "only" before the price. The reader simply feels a bargain.

## Examples & Evidence

- **Lifebuoy's body-odour campaign:** the classic flip; the smell you notice when opening the wrapper becomes proof of strength.
- **The TV-repair manual:** the turn, fear-as-promise and the repairs-to-adjustments redefinition.
- **Sherwin Cody's English course:** Schwartz calls it a masterpiece worth memorising, aimed at people who had tried and failed before.
- **A spark-plug ad, a speed-maths ad and a people-skills course** show the importance hinge, future importance and frequency.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** pre-emptive reframing beats rebuttal; in the feed an objection left unanswered quietly loses you the click. The turn is the ad-to-advertorial-to-product-page handoff, visible in drop-off and scroll depth. Anchoring is well supported by behavioural economics. Small word choices are precisely what bland AI copy misses. Quantified shrinking is the ancestor of quiz funnels and "just three steps". Hidden structural techniques age slowest.

**Needs translating:** "you smell" framing breaches Meta's personal-attributes policy; route it through a creator story instead. "Symptoms" is medical framing; use "signs" for cosmetics. Claims of ease need visible proof such as screen recordings, demos and creator tests. "AI-powered" wears out like "machine" once did.

**Risky:** made-up reference prices are misleading; strike-through prices need a genuine recent lowest price. Changing what you compare against (price per use, a salon visit, savings) stays legal and effective. Discounts in exchange for reviews must be disclosed. Percentage splits are factual claims that need backing.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **"You don't have to be a [expert] to [result]."**
- **"Only [minutes] a day. And there isn't much to learn."**
- **Hinge line:** you paid [big amount] for this, and one [cheap part] robs you of it.
- **"You can have it for..."** as a favour, not a sale.
