# Breakthrough Advertising - Preface

## Core Argument & Structure

Eighteen years after the first edition, Schwartz rereads his own book and corrects himself: he thought he had written about advertising, but it is really about creating markets. His evidence is that the readers who made fortunes from it were mostly not copywriters at all. The promise he restates is that a new market can be opened for a product, whether new or long established, through a defined set of steps, and the book lays out every one of them.

From there he widens the audience to every business owner, because everyone who sells is either building or tapping a market for a product. The book sums up that common role with one term: "Market-Makers."

The third pillar is the desire axiom. People's wants do not change; only where those wants point changes. You can't make anyone want something, and you don't need to. The book puts it as: human beings stay the same, and what shifts is merely the direction their desires take.

The preface moves from publication history and the rereading confession, to market-making and the life cycle of markets (including reviving old products), to the timelessness of the principles with proof cases, and finally to a close written as a miniature sales letter. It teaches by doing: it sells the book with the techniques the book contains.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

Eight rules can be drawn from it:

1. **The market comes before the product.** Most of the time demand already exists and you simply tap it. Copy focuses a force that is already there.
2. **Channel, don't create.** Your task is to point existing wants at products that genuinely satisfy them. Steering energy that already exists costs less and fails less often than producing new energy.
3. **Implicit three-step market-making process:** sense a market forming, test and size it, then focus people, money and desire onto your product.
4. **Market-making has three parts:** the ad, the product, and the channel through which the product reaches buyers.
5. **Intensify the problem, not the product.** Schwartz's image is about making the problem bigger and more vivid in the customer's mind rather than building a marginally better product, then presenting yours as the exclusive answer.
6. **Leverage asymmetry.** A market may be millions strong, yet one piece of copy (or the script a sales team repeats) can steer them. Ad copy and a sales script are really one asset.
7. **Rejuvenate mature products.** Growth often comes from finding a new market for an old product rather than inventing something new.
8. **The demonstrated close:** reframe the book as its end result (more customers for you), ask an inclusion question the reader can only agree with, project the reader into the next success story, and finish with a personal request that triggers reciprocity.

## Examples & Evidence

No ad reproductions. The evidence is a set of results with deliberately specific, lopsided numbers:

- A financier who multiplied his net worth roughly a hundredfold within a year of reading it, used to prove the non-copywriter thesis.
- An opening admission that first-edition sales were modest, a few thousand, which makes the later claims easier to believe.
- A health publisher that sold tens of millions of dollars of a single encyclopedia-style book.
- Boardroom Reports, started on a few thousand dollars and projected to reach tens of millions in annual sales.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** the desire axiom is the best filter against mass-produced AI content. Writing is now cheap; diagnosing the right desire is not, so market diagnosis gains value. "Market before product" is today's product-market fit. Odd, specific numbers still persuade.

**Needs translating:** sensing a market is now data work: search volume, trend speed, and on Meta, under broad targeting, the creative itself picks who sees it, so one piece of copy becomes one creative in the auction. The distribution channel is now the funnel and store. "Write and tell me" calls to action have become replies, community and user-generated content loops. Income-style testimonials like the financier's would be hard to defend under modern advertising rules and Meta policy; keep the lesson (concrete numbers) and use provable, typical results.

**Risky:** pushing fear as the intensification lever collides with Meta's policies on negative self-perception, so use measured problem agitation. Promising "a book full of customers" literally could read as a guarantee of results; as a positioning line it still works.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **Product as its end result:** present not a [product] but [the outcome, counted in what the buyer values].
- **Inclusion question before the close:** a short "that's what we all want, right?" line as a hook or subhead.
- **Future pacing plus a request for help:** put the reader in the next success story, then ask for their help.
- **Asymmetric number pairs:** a small, odd starting figure against a large end figure, never round pairs.
