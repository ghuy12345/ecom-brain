# Breakthrough Advertising Ch 12 - Concentration

## Core Argument & Structure

The chapter opens on a principle from Breakthrough Advertising: "no successful copy ever sells a product." What it sells is a route to a satisfied desire, and because profitable desires attract many sellers, competition is the normal state of business.

Schwartz first lists the weapons against competition and notes that four of them act as if no rival exists. The sixth technique, concentration (direct attack), is the opposite: deliberately dismantling the other routes to satisfaction. It's a challenger's tool. A dominant brand shouldn't attack and lend the rival prestige. A brand with a smaller budget, whose prospects already buy from the competitor, has to dent the rival's image and loosen that loyalty before it can win the desire.

The cardinal rule: never point out a weakness unless you supply the fix in the same breath. A bare attack is discounted as bias. It reuses almost every earlier technique, aimed at the rival's product.

## Frameworks & Mechanics

**1. Five weapons against competition.**
1. *A better product.* If your copy beats the product, get the product improved.
2. *A better promise:* stronger (more desire), broader (more buyers), more believable (reaches sceptics).
3. *Product role:* personality, prestige and excitement.
4. *Response and reaction:* escalating claims, shifting mechanisms, invading new markets.
5. *Direct attack:* concentration.

**2. When to attack.**
- Don't: if you dominate the market, if you're defending loyalty, or if your own story is so strong and fresh that rivals have no answer.
- Do: if your budget is smaller and most of your prospects already buy from the rival.
- Order: dent the rival's image first, then loosen loyalty, then steer the desire to you.

**3. Neutralise the bias.** Every weakness you name comes with its solution, and the attack is framed as being for the reader's benefit, because prospects automatically discount self-serving attacks.

**4. Structure one: interwoven contrast.**
1. The rhythm alternates weakness and strength, pair after pair. Each pair is a weakness, its cause ("because..."), and your mirrored benefit.
2. Mirror the wording: a weak category word against a strong product word (a thin spark versus a powerful flame).
3. Define and redefine: the rival's way is the most wasteful; yours the most efficient, sharpened by parallel sentences.
4. Redefine by analogy: the product "breaks in" rather than wears out.
5. Give nearly every benefit its own proof mechanism.
6. Close with an authority climax that retroactively validates the whole list.

**5. Structure two: time sequence.** Use it when there are no neat points of contrast, only a familiar, recurring bad experience.
1. Ask two questions: what happens now with what you use, and what happens when you switch?
2. Devalue the whole category with one word (passive), hinting at the active counter-mechanism.
3. Use causal logic ("so what happened?") to make the failure feel inevitable.
4. Build a stream of recognisable experiences, each one agreed with.
5. Shift the blame implicitly: it was the old product that made the attempt fail. Never assert it; make it the only conclusion.
6. Come back to the promise, now told as one customer's story and far stronger, backed by eliminating alternatives and documenting the mechanism.

**6. Compression.** The same effect fits in two or three sentences or a single phrase. "[Result] without [the feared method]" carries contrast, an implied weakness in alternatives and your promise all at once.

**7. What counts.** Only the reaction: doubting a habit, shifting loyalty, risking a purchase.

## Examples & Evidence

- **A spark-plug versus fire-injector ad:** six bad-good pairs with specific numbers, a lifetime guarantee, an air-force endorsement and a headline aimed at mechanics. A warning comes with it: the product was pseudo-technology, so the structure can sell junk too.
- **A reducing-pill ad:** the failure timeline (passive appetite suppressants, punishing diets, willpower finally snapping), then authority stacking and a vivid image of a tiny grey pill.

## Modern Validity 2026

**Still works as-is:** the challenger logic holds. Attacking in the reader's service is the logic of advertorials and educational content. Bad-good pairs live on as comparison tables; the time sequence is the "I tried everything" advertorial. "X without Y" is one of the sturdiest hook formulas,. Implied conclusions beat stated ones with sceptical audiences.

**Needs translating:** naming competitors is legally constrained (objective, verifiable, not disparaging), so attacking the category is the safer default; bidding on competitor brand terms in search is the modern structured attack. Numbers and endorsements need evidence. Use third-person framing under Meta's personal-attributes policy.

Keep the structure of the reducing ad; use specific, provable claims.

## Swipe-Worthy Language Patterns

- **"[Result] without [feared method]."**
- **Weak category word versus strong product word** in parallel sentences.
- **"So what happened?"** as the causal hinge.
- **"[Specialists]: read this carefully"** as an audience-selecting hook.
