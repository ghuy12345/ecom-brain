# Emotional Valence and Intensity

## What it is
Purchases are emotional decisions. Plenty of ads hold attention well and still do not sell, simply because they leave the viewer feeling nothing. That is an issue of tone rather than format. The feeling a reader is left with outweighs the exact wording.

Two dimensions describe that feeling:
- **Valence**: the direction, from positive to negative.
- **Intensity**: the strength, from calm to charged.

Together they form four zones:

| Zone | Direction / strength | Feeling | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | positive / low | calm, reassured, "this will work out" | exhausted audiences who have tried it all |
| 2 | positive / high | joy, thrill, identity pride | audiences with strong identity attachment and passionate communities |
| 3 | negative / low | annoyed, let down, irritated | recurring everyday frustrations |
| 4 | negative / high | alarm, fear, survival instinct | the subconscious "watch out" response |

An excitable promise aimed at someone sitting in zone 1 falls flat. The scene your words place the reader in also shifts the emotion, and it depends on the person: a golf tee box thrills a confident golfer and stresses a nervous one.

As a wider reference ladder, David Hawkins' Map of Consciousness orders emotional states on a ladder: shame, guilt and apathy near the bottom; grief, fear, desire, anger and pride above them; courage as the turning point; and states such as willingness, acceptance, reason, love, joy and peace higher up. Most problem-driven buying starts in the lower states, while the outcome you promise is framed in the higher ones. Use it to meet prospects where they are, not where you wish they were.

## When to use it
- An ad gets attention but not sales.
- A "better" iteration of a winner unexpectedly flopped; often it drifted to a different zone without anyone noticing.
- Reviving old winners or planning variations.
- Looking for a gap competitors leave open.

## How (steps)
1. **Classify your winners.** Give an AI assistant your hooks and your page headlines and have it place each one on the direction and strength grid. Over time, learn to judge it yourself.
2. **Make more in the proven zone,** saying the same angle differently.
3. **Test the angle in other zones.** A single concept carrying four ads, one for each zone, is a reasonable format even if it is more ads than usual.
4. **Keep an anchor.** At least one in every three variations should stay in the proven zone.
5. **Revive old winners** by rewriting them in the zone that currently works.
6. **Plan zone transitions.** Most of the emotional work happens in the hook and the bridge. Moving straight from a low, negative zone to an excited hard sell is jarring; pass through reassurance. Direct-response ads should end positive. A curiosity ad that only needs the click can stay negative if the next page resolves it.
7. **Compare competitors by zone.** If direct competitors all live in one zone, the opposite zone may be open to you.

## Example
The same claim can live in different zones: "check your shampoo label for these ingredients now" (zone 4) versus "the best ingredients for healthier hair" (zone 2). A children's-vitamin brand might use "signs your child may be low on key vitamins", which flags a problem without promising harm. Say a hair-care brand's winning ad is zone 3 frustration; a new iteration written in upbeat zone 2 language fails. Before blaming the angle, rewrite it back in zone 3 and retest.

## Common mistakes
- **Fear-mongering.** Fear often motivates more than desire, which is exactly why it needs limits. Untrue threats are unethical and can bring ad bans, a damaged reputation and lost payment processing. Acceptable: facts you can verify, linked clearly to the product ("early warning signs", "left untreated, this can cause lasting damage"), that you can back up. Unacceptable: threats you cannot back, such as implied death. If people stop reading because the ad frightens them, it has gone too far.
- **Assuming negative always wins.** Many winning ads are positive and high intensity, and that can scale too.
- **Ignoring the ending.** An ad that leaves people in a negative state with no resolution only educates.
- **Inventing intensity.** The strongest ethical source of intensity is genuine customer stories; add a survey question asking customers to tell theirs.
- Treating this as a replacement for your process. It is one more lens on top of what already works.
