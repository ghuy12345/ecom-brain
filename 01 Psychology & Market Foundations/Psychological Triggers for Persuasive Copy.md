# Psychological Triggers for Persuasive Copy

## What it is
A small set of well-documented mental shortcuts shapes how people judge offers. Used honestly, in service of a product that genuinely delivers, they help the right buyers decide faster, and they also reduce returns because expectations are set clearly. Used dishonestly, they destroy trust and can break advertising rules.

The ten covered here are confirmation bias, anchoring, scarcity, social proof, loss aversion, authority, framing, the halo effect, the curiosity gap and reciprocity. Their effect compounds when several are layered in one passage. How proof should be ordered and placed on a page is covered in [[Escalating Levels of Proof]] and [[Proof Types and Placement on Pages]].

## When to use it
- Writing or reviewing landing pages, pre-sale pages and long ad copy.
- Auditing a page that explains the product well but still does not persuade.
- Choosing how to present price and offer.

## How (steps)
1. **Confirmation bias.** Begin with statements the reader already accepts, so they are nodding along before you reach your bigger claims. On a skincare page, the first items might simply validate that hydration matters.
2. **Anchoring.** Whatever number appears first sets the benchmark. Options: the yearly cost of alternatives, the price of reaching the result another way, the running cost of doing nothing, and a crossed-out original price. Showing the single-purchase price next to a subscription can make the subscription feel like the smarter deal.
3. **Scarcity.** Use it only if it is real: genuine deadlines, small or seasonal batches, limited stock, capped early-adopter pricing. Genuine scarcity signals were reported to cut conversion losses by 37% against pages without them.
4. **Social proof.** Precise numbers persuade more than vague praise; an exact customer count beats "thousands of happy customers", by about 73% in reported tests. Use testimonials from people who resemble the avatar, plus usage figures and media coverage.
5. **Loss aversion.** A loss stings about twice as much as an equal gain pleases. Frame copy around what the reader is giving up, such as money thrown at products that fail, rather than what they could save. Loss-framed statements reportedly outperform gain-framed ones by 40-60%.
6. **Authority.** Genuine, checkable experts and credentials that fit what the audience already trusts, not a vague "research says". Readers expect a brand to be partial and an independent expert to be fair. Supplement pages have reportedly seen authority lift conversion by as much as 300%, and dermatologist endorsements reportedly lifted a skincare page 34% overnight.
7. **Framing.** Position the product as *the* answer rather than one option, speak with certainty, show alternatives as the weaker choice and address the reader as someone with good judgement, not someone who needs persuading.
8. **Halo effect.** Open with your strongest claim, number or testimonial; a strong first impression lifts everything after it.
9. **Curiosity gap.** Subheads that hint without revealing ("the overlooked step", "the one thing missing"), with loops opened early and resolved further down.
10. **Reciprocity.** Offer three or four tips that genuinely help before the product appears, then present the product as the last tip, the one that makes the others work.

**Layer, do not overload.** Aim for several triggers in one passage, for example: an exact customer number (social proof and specificity), a comparison built on how the product works (anchoring and framing), a statistic (authority), what each week of delay costs (loss aversion) and an opening premise the reader already accepts (confirmation). Then move to the next section rather than piling every trigger into one paragraph.

## Example
Say a sleep-supplement page opens with "You already know a bad night ruins the next day" (confirmation). The next block notes that the average person spends far more per year on coffee and energy drinks to cope than this costs (anchoring and loss aversion). Then comes a pharmacist explaining the ingredient (authority), followed by an exact review count from people with similar sleep complaints (social proof). A sub-headline reads "the one thing most sleep aids leave out" (curiosity gap). Each section stacks two or three triggers and hands off to the next.

## Common mistakes
- **Fabricated experts or scarcity.** Fake countdown timers and invented "limited stock" erode trust and may break platform policies.
- **Vague proof** such as "customers love it".
- **Disguised pitches.** "Value" that is obviously a setup for the sale backfires into skepticism.
- **Stacking everything everywhere,** which reads as pushy and dilutes the strongest trigger.
