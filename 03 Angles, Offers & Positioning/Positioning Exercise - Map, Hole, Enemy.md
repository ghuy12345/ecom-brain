# Positioning Exercise - Map, Hole, Enemy

## What it is
The practical companion to [[Brand Positioning - Different, Not Better]]. The key question is not "what position do we want?" but **"what position can we own?"** Plenty of brands chase a spot a rival already occupies. And the position you claim is capped by what buyers will accept as believable, not just by what you would like to say.

Buyers can accept surprising framings. A cold medicine whose side effect was sleepiness turned that weakness into a selling point by becoming the remedy you take at bedtime, and built a large category on it, because buyers found the framing sensible.

## When to use it
- Launching a brand or a new product line.
- When a competitor copies you, or your ads sound like everyone else's.
- When you are stuck at a plateau and suspect the problem is "why us?", not the ads themselves.
- When designing a new product or a version two.

## How (steps)
**1. Map the landscape.** Write down what category you are in, your direct rivals, the market leader and the idea it is known for, and every position that is already taken in the buyer's mind.

**2. Fill in four tables.**
1. *Rival brands* next to *what sets you apart from each.* Only the difference, not a claim of superiority.
2. *Rival features* next to *your contrast.* List what they offer that you don't, and what you offer that they don't.
3. *Common complaints about products in this category* next to *how you fix or avoid each one.* Mine competitor reviews for big and small issues.
4. *Synthesis:* take each difference from tables 1 and 2 and pair it with a complaint from table 3. **Any difference that fixes a real complaint is a candidate position.**

**3. Choose a path.**
- *Path A, be first:* can you narrow down to a sub-category you would lead from day one, with nobody directly competing?
- *Path B, find the hole:* if being first is off the table, change the intended buyer or the lens people use on the category. Ask: What will rivals not do, admit or put on camera? Which opposite stance could you take (classic rather than trendy, a single size rather than many)? Which glaring problem is everyone ignoring?

**4. Make it concrete.**
- Name the **strategic enemy**: a rival, an outdated method, or a belief buyers hold.
- Decide what you will **refuse** to make or do.
- Make the difference **visible**: packaging, format, product design. Visual difference may need product work, so treat it as a longer-term play.

**5. Set the breadth.** Balance uniqueness against a market big enough to scale. Smaller brands (roughly under six figures a month) are better off narrow: own a sub-category, work through avatars and narrower sub-avatars, and widen only after that foothold is secure. Larger brands need more judgement about breadth.

**6. Design positioning into the product.** The easiest positions to market are built in from the start: the best macros per serving, a gummy made for children, a format nobody else offers. Ask how the product can make the outcome feel easier, faster and more likely to the buyer. A gummy is not a mechanism in itself; people care what it does for them (easier for kids, nicer to take).

**7. Decide where your advantage lies.** A strong mechanism means building angles off the mechanism. A weaker or saturated product means niching the avatar harder. Ideally have both.

**8. Test it.** Write the position into ads and a page, and let buyers vote. Nobody can grade it on paper.

## Example
A hypothetical baby-carrier brand fills the tables. Competitors lead on "ergonomic" and "premium fabric", both already owned. Reviews across the category complain about overheating in summer and fiddly buckles. The brand's differences: a mesh panel and a one-clip fastening. Mapping differences to problems gives two candidates: "the carrier for hot climates" and "on in ten seconds". It picks hot climates (a real subcategory, visible in the product), names the enemy as thick padded carriers built for cold countries, refuses to make winter fabrics at all, and puts a visible mesh window on the box. The next product version adds a cooling insert, deepening the position rather than adding unrelated features.

## Common mistakes
- Choosing a position a competitor already owns.
- Listing reasons you are "better" instead of how you are different.
- Going so narrow the market cannot support your spend goals, or so broad you stand for nothing.
- Leaving the position in the copy only, with nothing visible in the product or packaging.
- Treating the exercise as a one-off; revisit it as competitors move.
