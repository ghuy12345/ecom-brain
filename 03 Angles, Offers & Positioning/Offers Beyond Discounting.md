# Offers Beyond Discounting

## What it is
Plenty of brands blame their ads when the real weakness is the offer, and very often the habit of discounting. A discount is only one way to structure price. Used constantly, it re-anchors your market price: a brand launched at a healthy margin can become "the discount brand" and live permanently on a much thinner one. A year-round promotional calendar teaches customers to wait.

A full offer has five components:
1. **Positioning:** what problem it solves, for whom, and why now.
2. **Price architecture:** percentage off, thresholds, multi-unit deals, bundles.
3. **Value mechanic:** the difference between what an extra costs the brand and how much it feels worth to the buyer (gift, sample, free shipping, subscription perk).
4. **Risk reversal:** guarantee, trial, easy returns, a sample first.
5. **Urgency:** a real reason to act now (seasonality, limited runs).

## When to use it
- Margins are eroding because the brand discounts constantly.
- You are a premium brand and want to protect price perception.
- Early in the year, when sentiment is low and offer tests are cheap.

## How (steps)
**1. Answer four questions first.** What is your gross-margin floor? Do your order values cluster around one level or two? Are you a single hero product or many products? Do customers repeat (build subscription in) or buy once (protect first-order margin)?

**2. Pick from five mechanics.**
- **Reposition the same product.** Same SKU, new persona, problem and headline, each with its own offer. Margin impact is zero or positive. A generic daily-greens powder can become a gut-health, hair or focus product. For slow-to-show results, add a free extra unit on the second delivery instead of a bigger first-order discount.
- **Bundles.** Raise units per order and pass on shipping and fulfilment savings (not product cost). Suit multi-product brands and same-product multi-packs.
- **Gift with purchase.** Buy two, get a low-cost accessory free. Contribution per order can more than double while breakeven barely moves. The only way it fails is a gift nobody values, so sell the gift separately on-site at a real price and show the saving. Better still, ask customers what nearly stopped them buying and build the answer into the offer, for instance a longer guarantee reserved for the bigger tier.
- **Second-buyer add-ons.** Put a product for another person in the same order (a kids' supplement plus a parent version).
- **Tiered thresholds.** Look at the actual distribution of order values, not the mean. If there are two peaks, build one offer per peak. A threshold pegged to the average hands a gift to shoppers who were already spending more and does nothing for those well under it.

**3. Use straight discounts with discipline.** Reserve them for a handful of products with unusually fat margins or for turning old stock into cash, once or twice a year, and never to rescue top-line revenue.

**4. Use risk reversal in sceptical categories.** A strong guarantee can be the whole offer for products like acne care where buyers have been burned before.

**5. Model the trade-off.** Breakeven ROAS rises roughly in line with the discount, but the ROAS needed to hold your *contribution per order* rises much faster. Very small discounts (5-10%) often lose money because behaviour doesn't change; contribution peaks somewhere in the middle, then collapses past roughly 30-40%. The full math is in [[Checking Whether a Discount Is Profitable]].

**6. Offer differently by visitor stage.** Browsing exits can get a more generous, time-limited incentive; cart exits a small one; checkout exits only risk reversal or shipping help. Post-purchase add-ons are covered in [[Post-Purchase Upsells and AOV Levers]].

**7. Review after three to four months.** Ask buyers which part of the offer tipped them over; cut the parts nobody mentions, and make sure any digital extra isn't making the product feel cheaper. Test new offers on separate pages, because offers change both first-order profit and retention.

## Example
Hypothetical numbers for a consumable: AOV $100, product cost $40, breakeven ROAS about 1.67. A flat 25% off lifts units per order, but product cost now takes a bigger share of each sale, breakeven rises to about 2.2, and the ad efficiency needed to keep the same profit per order jumps by around half. Revenue looks healthier even as each order earns less.

Instead the brand offers a 90-day supply at a moderate saving, a free digital guide, a cheap mystery sample and a lasting subscriber discount. More buyers choose the large pack, AOV more than doubles, shipping per unit falls, and the ROAS needed for the same profit per order drops sharply. Because it is evergreen, it lowers the efficiency the brand needs all year.

## Common mistakes
- Calling a blanket discount an "offer".
- Miscalculating margin by ignoring shipping and fulfilment.
- Same offer for new and returning customers; thresholds set on mean AOV; gifts nobody values.
- Running promotions 52 weeks a year.
- Writing off January to March as a slump instead of using it to test offers.
