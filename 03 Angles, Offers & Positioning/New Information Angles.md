# New Information Angles

## What it is
In a sophisticated market, buyers have heard every claim. One of the most accessible responses is **new information**: sharing a verifiable fact the buyer hasn't come across, typically one that explains why earlier fixes let them down or why yours succeeds. Unlike a new mechanism, it needs nothing unique about the product. It can be a research finding, a reframe of what the category already does, or a link between the product and a problem the buyer has not connected to it. Stack it with a mechanism and you get the biggest lift.

New information is one of three classic responses to high sophistication, alongside new mechanisms and new identity (see [[Responding to High Market Sophistication]]).

## When to use it
- Your product has no obvious unique feature, but the market is tired of direct claims.
- You can find a credible, verifiable fact that changes how the buyer sees the problem.
- You want a curiosity-driven hook that still leads somewhere believable.

## How (steps)
1. **Gather raw leads.** Use your desire research, product research and competitor review mining. An AI chat with your product details and sophistication analysis can suggest directions, but its output is a lead for research, not copy.
2. **Verify every lead.** Check primary sources. Hallucinated or overstated findings are common, and educated buyers will call them out in the comments.
3. **Check congruence.** The information must support your product. Do not claim "this form of the ingredient is destroyed by digestion" if that is the form you sell.
4. **Build a curiosity loop.** Hook with the finding plus a gap ("...and it switches on only in total darkness"). The audience wants to know what that means. Explain it simply and cite where it comes from. Then show how your product creates the condition the finding requires. This works in video and on landing pages.
5. **Limit jargon.** One unfamiliar term can create curiosity ("what's that?"); several make the ad unreadable. Describe benefits in plain terms rather than specs.
6. **Screen valence and intensity.** Valence is whether the feeling is positive or negative; intensity is how strong it is. A negative tone is allowed; a negative tone turned up to maximum becomes fear-mongering (see [[Emotional Valence and Intensity]]).
7. **Run the loved-one test.** Picture a parent or sibling seeing this ad. If it would frighten you, keep it away from strangers too. Fear-driven customers are poor customers.
8. **Rewrite rather than discard.** If a finding is framed too intensely, keep the defensible core and remove the alarm. When using AI, name the offending phrases, explain why, and show a sample line at the mildest tone you would accept; a general instruction to "be ethical" is not enough, so always review by hand.

## Example
Imagine a seasoning brand advertising "tastes good and is good for you" to little response. It could reframe the product around sensitive digestion ("sensitive stomach? this blend was made for you"), backed by qualities such buyers look for, then link to how quickly it is ready. Nothing about the product changes; the information alone could turn it into a scalable ad.

Imagine a blackout-blind brand finding two leads. One is a reported finding that a receptor involved in deep sleep works best in complete darkness, which becomes a curiosity hook about the discovery and its darkness condition (to be verified before use). The other linked light pollution to serious cognitive decline. That one fails the intensity screen and gets rewritten into a gentler line it could defend: a fully dark room may help protect memory over the long run.

Imagine a functional-mushroom pouch brand running a longer video of a couple of minutes: a hook that something is not being said, information that stomach acid can degrade certain compounds, and a bridge to taking it in through the lining of the mouth instead. Higher production, harder for beginners, but a clear new-information structure.

## Common mistakes
- Treating AI-generated "facts" as copy without checking them.
- Information that undermines your own product category.
- Fear-mongering: negative valence at high intensity.
- Stacking too many unfamiliar terms.
- Lecturing instead of telling a story; information lands better inside a narrative.
- Unverifiable statistics in health categories (see [[Product Integrity and Claims That Outgrow Reality]]).
