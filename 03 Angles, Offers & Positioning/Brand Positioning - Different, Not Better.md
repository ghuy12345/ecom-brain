# Brand Positioning - Different, Not Better

## What it is
Positioning is how customers see your brand compared with their other options. The tradition that runs from Al Ries and Jack Trout to April Dunford treats marketing as a battle fought inside the buyer's head, where perception decides the winner more than the product spec. A workable definition: for a clearly defined group of customers, your product is the world's best at one specific, distinctive kind of value.

The central idea is **different beats better**. Saying "better" puts buyers on guard: if you really were better, surely you would already lead the market? Saying "different" makes them curious, and as they work out *why* you are different, they often decide on their own that you are also better. Never assert superiority; let the buyer reach it.

A copycat brand without a position hands shoppers nothing to choose it for. Whatever it sells comes from convenience (someone happened to see an ad), and convenience does not compound into a brand.

## When to use it
- When your ads sound like every competitor's.
- When you plan a new product, sub-brand or category entry.
- When a teardown of a winning ad needs a "positioning" line: how does it frame the product for this avatar, and why would anyone care? Give positioning its own slot, separate from avatar and mechanism.
- Once copy and creative fundamentals are in place: positioning is deep brand work, and it is reasonable to fix ads first and return to it.

## How (steps)
1. **Start from what buyers already believe.** People's heads are full; planting a brand-new idea from scratch is expensive. Research competitors so you know where to stand apart, not what to imitate. Pretending they don't exist is a classic failure.
2. **Don't attack a position someone owns.** Taking the leader on at the leader's own strength rarely works.
3. **Be first.** If the main category is taken, claim the lead in a narrower sub-category, as long as buyers see it as a genuine category.
4. **Failing that, reposition by contrast.** Change the intended buyer, or change the frame buyers use to judge the category. "We beat X" is not repositioning. Tie the product to a familiar reference ("it's like this"), then demonstrate, rather than assert, where it departs.
5. **Pick a strategic enemy.** Contrasting your idea with something buyers already accept is far easier than overturning a firm belief. Your enemy might be a rival brand, an old way of doing things, or a widely held assumption. This is not about hate; it acknowledges the buyer faces a choice and makes choosing easier. A good enemy gives customers something to rally around, which drives word of mouth.
6. **Say no.** Selling to everybody sells to nobody. What you refuse to make or do signals what you stand for. Take a clear stance while staying respectful of every group.
7. **Look for the obvious hole.** The best open position is often hiding in plain sight, which is exactly why others overlook it. Frequently it is a fact rivals avoid admitting or showing.
8. **Make the difference visible.** Seeing is believing; packaging, format and product design carry positioning as much as copy.
9. **Test, don't seek approval.** Nobody can grade a differentiation idea in advance. Any idea can work if customers care; testing tells you.

## Example
Famous public patterns:
- **First in a category:** Liquid Death made water in a tall can a category of its own; the smartphone framed everything before it as a "dumb" phone.
- **Different, not better:** once Red Bull had defined the energy drink in a slim can, a challenger answered with a much larger can. Not better, just different.
- **Contrast:** Avis, the number-two rental company, said it tried harder *because* it was number two. A compact car made "small" its whole identity in a market that worshipped size.
- **Enemy:** electric cars against petrol, ride-hailing against taxis, fresh dog food against kibble.
- **Repositioning without a product change:** Old Spice realised women bought much of the category and spoke to them instead, with the same product.
- **The hole:** a low-calorie ice-cream brand only took off once its packaging printed the calorie count for the entire tub, a number rivals preferred to hide.

A protein-bar category shows how this repeats. An endurance bar was challenged by a high-protein bar for ordinary people, then by a bar that printed its few ingredients in large type on the wrapper, then by a whole-food bar that made processing the enemy. Each was arguably not better, just clearly and visibly different.

## Common mistakes
- Claiming "better" or "best" with nothing to make it visible.
- Attacking a leader on its own turf.
- Inventing a subcategory so narrow nobody recognises it.
- Assuming perception did all the work: many successful repositionings also changed the product. Product advantage is itself positioning.
- Trying to please everyone and standing for nothing.
