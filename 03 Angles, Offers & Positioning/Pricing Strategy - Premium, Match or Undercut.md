# Pricing Strategy - Premium, Match or Undercut

## What it is
Price is part of positioning. It decides who buys, what they expect, how much you can afford to pay for a customer, and how much room your offers have. There are three basic positions relative to competitors:

1. **Premium:** priced above the category. Needs a genuinely distinct product, a strong brand or a clear identity position.
2. **Match:** priced in line with competitors, winning on message and avatar.
3. **Undercut:** priced below. Wins price-sensitive buyers but leaves little margin for acquisition and invites price wars.

**The sensible default** for most brands is level with or a touch under the competition, with the real separation coming from which avatar you target and what you say to them.

## When to use it
- Launching a product or entering a new market.
- When CAC is rising and margin no longer covers it.
- When your ad account is maxed out on the current product.
- When you sell on marketplaces alongside your own store.

## How (steps)
1. **Map the price bands.** List competitor prices for the same and adjacent products. Note where the cheap, middle and premium clusters sit. Avoid anchoring against an implausible compare-at price.
2. **Pick a position on purpose.** Premium only if you can show why (a real mechanism, materials, brand, identity). Match if your edge is the message. Undercut only if your cost structure genuinely supports it.
3. **Use price as a signal.** A higher price can tell buyers the product is better quality. In some categories, cheap reads as suspicious, especially when paired with an extravagant free-extra offer.
4. **Raise prices before stacking discounts.** If you plan a subscribe-and-save discount (20-30% is common), consider raising the base price first so the discount does not eat the margin.
5. **Expect volatility after a price rise.** Sales are often inconsistent in the first days after a change. Judge on several days of averaged data, not single days, and don't read early noise as failure.
6. **Sell value on marketplaces.** On creator-led marketplaces, a pricier product has to win on value instead of cost, and creators' videos have to make that value case, because a listing alone can't.
7. **Scale through the product line.** When an account is maxed out, a higher-priced version or bundle (say at about three times the price) increases the most you can pay per new customer, which unlocks more spend at the same efficiency.
8. **Revisit if the economics don't close.** If breakeven ROAS stays unreachable after offers and pricing are exhausted, the issue may be the product itself (see [[Breakeven ROAS and KPI Tiers]]).

## Example
A hypothetical cookware brand sells a frying pan at $45 in a category where most pans sit between $35 and $60. It matches the market at first, winning on a specific avatar (people who cook at high heat and keep warping pans). Once the angle is proven, it raises the price to $55 and sees a choppy first week; its three-day averages settle at a similar conversion rate with better margin. When ad spend plateaus, it launches a $160 three-pan set. The higher order value lets it afford a higher CPA and push spend further without changing the core ads.

A different hypothetical: a skincare brand undercuts the category leader by a third. It wins early sales, but every competitor promotion forces a matching discount, and it never builds the margin to fund creative testing. Matching price and leading with a sharper avatar would have left room to grow.

## Common mistakes
- Pricing premium with nothing visible to justify it.
- Undercutting by default and starving the acquisition budget.
- Panicking at early volatility after a price change and reversing it.
- Stacking subscription and promotional discounts on an already thin price.
- Competing on price in a marketplace when the product's value story is the real advantage.
