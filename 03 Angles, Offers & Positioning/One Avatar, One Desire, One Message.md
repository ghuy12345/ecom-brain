# One Avatar, One Desire, One Message

## What it is
Keep each ad, and the page it sends traffic to, to **one** person, **one** desire and **one** main message. A product can support many angles, but each ad should pick one. You want one particular person to feel the product was built for them. Specificity creates relatability, relatability creates belief, and belief is what converts: more belief, more sales.

Trying to reach everyone in a single ad creates confusion and weak relevance. A tight, single angle regularly beats an ad that lists every feature.

## When to use it
- Writing any new ad or brief.
- Diagnosing an ad that gets attention but no sales: it often speaks to several people at once.
- Building a landing page for a proven ad.
- After a win, when deciding what to test next.

## How (steps)
1. **Define a real avatar, not a demographic.** "Women over 35 with joint pain" describes an enormous population, and a crowded shelf of products already chases them. A real avatar pins down four things: what hurts emotionally, what they already tried that failed, what exactly they want, and what their days look like. Steer between too vague and pointlessly precise (an exact age, how many kids). Look for traits that keep repeating in your research.
2. **Ladder the desire.** Take the surface want ("look better") and keep asking "why?" until you reach something fundamental: health, money, relationships, or feeling wanted. Then show that end state in the ad (the partner noticing), not the surface one (looking good in the mirror).
3. **Pick one angle.** A useful shape: angle = the dominant emotion + either the daily struggle or the ideal outcome. Problem-solving products lean on the daily struggle (what makes the current situation miserable); aspirational products such as fashion lean on the ideal outcome. Often, picking the avatar hands you the angle.
4. **Write a hook that names one thing only:** who it is for, what is wrong, or what fixes it.
5. **Put the remaining benefits in a supporting role.** They can appear in body copy, but they do not compete with the main reason to buy.
6. **Match the landing page.** Same avatar, same desire, same angle, in copy and visuals. If the ad speaks to one person and the page to another, belief breaks, and that journey rarely gets a second chance (see [[Ad-to-Page Congruency]]).
7. **Once proven, hold avatar and desire fixed** and test several angles against that same desire. A large share of winners turn up this way.

Also remember that few people purchase while feeling guilt, shame or grief. Offer something (a fix, a fresh mechanism) that lifts them toward hope and belief, the emotional zone where buying happens (see [[Emotional Valence and Intensity]]).

## Example
A greens-gummy brand has three possible angles: children like the taste, it's the simplest route to daily greens, and it supports digestion. Asking "who is this ad for?" and answering "parents of fussy eaters" turns "easiest way to get greens" into "your child will actually ask for their greens". One ad, one person, one reason.

Imagine a children's shoe brand running a broad video positioned as easy for the whole family. A quick static aimed visibly at parents of children who wear a supportive brace, with the desire laddered to "my child can dress themselves", might beat it: suppose it does, then a narrower avatar plus a deeper desire has beaten a broad one.

A canned-water brand shows how one product holds different angle types: a functional one ("no microplastics"), an identity one (joining a stand against plastic), and a status one (looking cool holding it). Each deserves its own ad.

## Common mistakes
- Calling a demographic an avatar and a surface want a desire.
- Listing generic benefits ("nourishing", "smells nice") with no comparison to what the buyer used before.
- A hook that names a problem while the person on screen visibly does not have it.
- Vague taglines that leave the buyer guessing whether it's meant for them.
- Treating two neighbouring avatars (different habits, goals, failed attempts) as one across consecutive ads, which muddles learnings.
- Treating formats such as UGC or video as angles.

A side benefit of the rule: when avatar, desire and angle are fixed up front, a losing ad tells you exactly which single variable to change next.
