# Ecommerce Offer Types and How to Choose

## What it is
A practical menu of the offers most ecommerce brands use, with rules for picking one. Every offer has a job, and there are only two: push **conversion rate up hard**, or push **average order value up hard while conversion stays where it is**. If you can't name which of those an offer is supposed to do, rethink it.

## When to use it
- Choosing a first offer, designing an offer for a winning ad's page, or replacing a stale offer.

## How (steps)
### The menu
- **Bundle and save (the strongest default).** Lifts conversion and order value together while your headline price stays intact. Extra small units often ship in the same box at little extra cost, so margin improves. Give the bundle a reason and a name, group items that naturally go together (several of a consumable makes sense; several of the same appliance usually doesn't), and make the saving obvious at a glance.
- **Buy X, get X free.** Suits products people actually use more than one of. Require a minimum quantity so a single unit isn't the path of least resistance. With healthy margins, more generous free-unit deals can increase profit, since each added unit costs almost nothing extra to ship.
- **Buy X, get a percentage off the next.** For thinner margins; it can also layer on top of a sitewide discount as a second, conditional deal.
- **Percentage off.** Better on low-priced items, where a dollar figure looks trivial ("10% off" reads better than "$3.75 off").
- **Dollar off.** Better on high-priced items. Rule of thumb: show whichever number is bigger. Never mix units across tiers.
- **Free gift.** A relevant guide, a complementary item, a mystery gift or holiday wrapping. An add-on; buyers mainly want the advertised product.
- **Store credit.** For single-product stores, a voucher added to the order. A good share of it never gets spent, which keeps the real cost low, and it can be stacked onto a genuine bundle as well.
- **Free shipping.** An add-on or a bundle incentive, not the headline offer. Try a minimum-spend threshold; when your shipping cost is only a few dollars, charging for it can actually win. Avoid promising faster delivery before you control fulfilment.
- **Guarantees and risk reversal.** The offer in sceptical categories (see [[Offers Beyond Discounting]]).

### Choosing
1. **Start from data.** For the ad you are supporting, pull its conversion rate, its order value and the actual mix of items new customers buy. Note constraints (pack sizes, margin).
2. **Match to price point and margin.** Low price: percentage or multi-unit offers. High price: dollar off, gifts, guarantees. Thin margin: a percentage off the next unit rather than free units.
3. **Match to the product.** Consumables suit multiples and subscriptions. For taste-driven products, let buyers choose their own mix; a fixed bundle can push away shoppers who wanted other flavours or styles.
4. **Draft tiers.** Let the larger tiers do the persuading; a 10% saving on a small starter tier rarely moves anyone. Pick a default tier, usually the middle, and tie it to the ad's problem. What you highlight most is what most people buy.
5. **Estimate realistic AOV.** Average the tiers, then shade the estimate lower, since most real orders land on the default or entry tier.
6. **Check breakeven** with the discounted margin before launch, including stacked offers (see [[Checking Whether a Discount Is Profitable]]). Don't anchor against an implausible compare-at price; check competitor price bands first.
7. **Remember subscription** as a separate lever (a deeper first order for subscribers), not only one-time offers.

### Ad-only offers
A deal better than the one in your regular store, shown only to people who click a particular ad, is a powerful lever. Two page types carry it: a pre-sell page that tells a story and then hands the visitor to the store (simplest), or a full offer page where the offer lives on the page (see [[Sales and Offer Page Structure]]).

### Cheap offer tests
- Put the new offer into the headline of your existing top ads and keep the creative constant.
- Treat a price test as an offer test, and consider showing your email welcome discount on the page instead.

## Example
A hypothetical soup brand designs an ad-only page for its "gut-friendly lunch" ad: tier one, six cans at 10% off; tier two (default, highlighted), twelve cans plus a free recipe guide at 20% off; tier three, twenty-four cans at 25% off with free shipping. Real orders cluster on tier two, AOV rises by about half, and the recalculated breakeven still clears comfortably.

## Common mistakes
- Making free shipping the core offer.
- Rewarding repeat orders with a discount while offering nothing for a bigger basket today.
- Unchecked tier arithmetic, or offers never tested end to end at checkout.
- Losing money on first orders by accident rather than as a deliberate, LTV-backed choice.
