# Unique Mechanism - Problem Cause and Solution

## What it is
A mechanism is the particular route by which a product produces its result, the reason it works differently from the alternatives. The idea goes back to Eugene Schwartz in *Breakthrough Advertising*. In practice a mechanism does one job: it proves the angle. For every claim you make, ask "what makes this true?" The answer is the mechanism.

A mechanism you can actually use comes in two parts:
1. **The problem mechanism:** the real, usually hidden cause of the problem. It explains why the prospect has struggled, why standard approaches missed it, and why it was not their fault.
2. **The solution mechanism:** what your product does about that exact cause, and why that lets it succeed where others treated only symptoms.

Put together, the message is: *you were nearly right, you were just missing one piece, and that missing piece is why this works.* Sceptical buyers have tried other things; showing them a gap in their knowledge turns your product into the logical next step.

## When to use it
- Mainly for sceptical, well-informed, or "tried everything" audiences in sophisticated markets. In young, hopeful markets a confident, direct claim frequently does the job alone.
- Match the half you lead with to awareness: **problem-aware** prospects need the problem mechanism first; **solution-aware** prospects need the solution mechanism (why yours is different); **product-aware** prospects need little mechanism and more proof and offer.
- Write the mechanism for the awareness level of the segment you are mainly targeting, not some other one.

## How (steps)
1. **Finish research first.** Failed existing solutions in reviews and forums reveal what was missing (the problem mechanism). Competitor gaps reveal what nobody claims (solution opportunities).
2. **State the central problem** your product solves.
3. **Find the root cause** your audience has never been told about, from credible sources. It must be real.
4. **Find how your product, ingredient or design acts on that exact cause.**
5. **Write it as one sentence:** the hidden cause, then why your solution works where other things didn't ("The real reason is X; that is why Y works").
6. **Open with the payoff and let the mechanism arrive as the explanation.** Advertise a hoodie that wraps you up like an embrace and let the viewer discover it is weighted; they think "that explains it".
7. **Stress-test it:**
   - *Simple:* a twelve-year-old could grasp it in a couple of seconds.
   - *Visual:* the viewer can imagine it in action. When nothing can be shown, the words have to do that work.
   - *Sounds new:* not something the market already repeats.
   - *Root-cause, not feature:* it explains failure history, and could not be pasted into a competitor's ad.
   - *Believable over clever:* if it sounds invented, it destroys trust.
8. **Weave it in, briefly.** One or two lines in a video script, not a science lecture. On pages: a listicle gets one item on why past fixes failed plus one on the mechanism; an advertorial puts the problem cause in the agitation section and the solution in the product reveal; a sales page needs only a short reminder.
9. **Name it, if it helps.** A specific, descriptive name (a descriptive modifier plus a process word such as "method", "protocol" or "effect") can make the solution feel categorically different, as long as it labels something real.

AI can draft candidate mechanisms from your research; see [[Finding New Mechanisms With AI]].

## Example
Hypothetical patterns across categories:
- *Oral care:* a film of bacteria builds up where brushing cannot reach and wears enamel; a rinse removes it.
- *Shaving:* standard blades may shed microscopic bits of metal that inflame skin, which a ceramic edge avoids.
- *Language learning:* most apps train recognition rather than speaking; this one makes you say the words out loud.
- *Kitchenware:* plastic boards shed particles into food; a wooden board avoids that.
- *Water:* "no microplastics" is the claim; the aluminium can is the mechanism.

Each one passes the simple, visual and new tests.

## Common mistakes
- Saying "mechanism" as a fashionable word when no real cause sits behind it.
- Explaining the science at such length that the ad turns into a lecture.
- Describing a product feature and calling it a mechanism.
- Opening with a mechanism when the audience would buy on a simple claim.
- Recycling a mechanism the market has heard over and over.
- Inventing a cause. A fictional mechanism might lift one ad, then erodes trust and invites refunds and policy problems.
