# Types of Unique Mechanism

## What it is
Once you understand what a mechanism does (see [[Unique Mechanism - Problem Cause and Solution]]), the next question is where to get one. Mechanisms differ by how much invention they require. You do not need a patent to have one; many winning mechanisms are things every competitor also does but nobody talks about.

Why it matters: people tune out anything that sounds like more of the same. They still want the outcome, but a pitch that echoes every other brand slides straight past them. A mechanism breaks that filter by giving the prospect **hope**, which grows into belief that this will work where other things did not. If you have no mechanism, a competitor will claim one and own that belief.

Novelty is the lever. Something can grab attention by being new without being superior, so "nothing makes us different" becomes a messaging challenge rather than a product one. Still, novelty only sells when it helps. A feature that makes nothing simpler, safer or more effective is mere ornament. Over time, genuinely improving the product is still the strongest play, because better tends to create new.

## When to use it
- When your claims sound identical to competitors'.
- When the market is sophisticated and direct claims no longer land.
- When you are entering a new avatar and your proven mechanism does not transfer.
- When competitors have copied your mechanism.

## How (steps)
Work through the three types in order of invention:

1. **New mechanism (most invention).** Something truly yours alone: a material, an ingredient, a part, a formula or a design. Example: a stainless-steel humidifier that is easier to clean, avoids mould in crevices and blows no air over plastic. Counter-example: bolting a colour-changing light onto that humidifier is new but useless for the buyer's problem.
2. **Unspoken mechanism.** Nothing unique, but no competitor mentions it. The classic case is a cigarette brand advertising that its tobacco was "toasted" when every rival toasted theirs too; it worked because it explained a better taste. Audit your manufacturing, materials and quality steps and list everything nobody in your category talks about.
3. **Renamed or reframed mechanism (least invention).** A familiar component given a fresh name or framing so it feels new: chicken stock sold as bone broth, a known technology given a descriptive proprietary name, an established formula repurposed for a fresh job. People assume newer is better. Whatever you call it, it has to describe something that genuinely exists.

Then run every candidate through three checks: Is it **useful**? Does it address a **painful problem**? Is it **believable**?

### Mechanisms are per avatar
A mechanism that persuades one avatar can mean nothing to another. A material you can see that makes cleaning easy wins over the buyer who hates scrubbing, yet leaves the style-led buyer cold; that person needs a mechanism about design. If a proven mechanism won't carry over, work out which problem you have:
- The new avatar **doubts it**: add authority (see [[Authority as Proof in the Claim Stack]]) or rename it.
- The new avatar is **more sophisticated** and has already seen it: find a different mechanism.

### Repositioning a crowded mechanism
If your claim stack matches everyone else's ("boosts X, reduces cravings, keeps you full"), change the verb or the category. Positioning a product as *protecting* something the category claims to *boost* can open a mechanism slot nobody occupies. A one-word shift can be enough.

When testing a new mechanism inside a funnel that already works, leave the working funnel untouched as your baseline and launch ads that change only the mechanism, so you can read the effect cleanly.

## Example
A water-bottle brand in a crowded market lists what it has: a ceramic inner coating (new to the category), a standard leak test every unit already goes through (unspoken: nobody advertises it), and a common double-wall vacuum (worn out). It leads with the coating for health-focused buyers ("nothing metallic in your water"), uses the leak test as an unspoken mechanism for commuters ("tested upside down before it ships"), and gives the vacuum layer a fresh descriptive name for a gift-buyer avatar that has never heard of it. Each avatar gets the mechanism that fits its problem.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming you need a patent or new technology to have a mechanism.
- Adding novelty that does not help the buyer.
- Renaming something that does not exist, which is fabrication, not positioning.
- Using one mechanism for every avatar.
- Repeating the same claim stack as competitors and expecting a different result.
