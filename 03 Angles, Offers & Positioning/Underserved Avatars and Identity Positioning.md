# Underserved Avatars and Identity Positioning

## What it is
In the most sophisticated markets, buyers resist claims. They look for emotional connection, a sense of who they become, and a brand experience. An **identity** position lets the price reflect something bigger than the item itself, supports a premium price, and, unlike a mechanism or an information claim, does not have to be defended line by line.

Genuine identity brands, the kind customers sign up to because of who it makes them, demand serious experience. Well-known identity brands are usually built by teams with deep brand-building experience. Do not make a category-defining identity brand your first project.

The practical version for most operators: **find an underserved identity**, a clearly defined kind of buyer that no competitor addresses, and speak directly to them. That is the same work as developing a winning avatar. Getting much sharper about your buyer is, on its own, a valid answer to a sophisticated market, and it still builds a brand over time.

## When to use it
- Claims and mechanisms in your category have all been heard.
- Competitors target the same obvious buyer.
- You cannot make a hard, sophisticated avatar convert despite decent execution.
- You want pricing power that does not rely on discounts.

## How (steps)
1. **List who the category currently speaks to.** Read competitor ads and pages and write down the avatar each one targets.
2. **Look for who is missing.** Use reviews, forums and survey data to find buyers who use the product but never see themselves in its marketing: a different gender, life stage, profession, hobby or problem context.
3. **Check the size.** The underserved group must be big enough to scale ads into; a niche that is too small stalls.
4. **Make it feel made for them.** Casting, language, use cases and visual cues that only this person recognises. A deliberately niche signal strengthens resonance with the target.
5. **Express the identity, not just the benefit.** Who do they become, or avoid becoming, by choosing this?
6. **If a hard avatar fails, move to an easier one.** Shifting to a less sophisticated avatar that your execution can reach is a legitimate strategy, not a cop-out. You only learn how sophisticated an avatar is by testing. No magic ad count tells you when to switch; judge by whether the product truly helps this person and whether your execution was sound. Your own skill is uneven across niches too, so consider which people you can speak to well.
7. **Remember the funnel level.** You don't need a quota of ads at each awareness level. Smaller accounts often do best on product-aware ads in a large market; if those stall, move up to problem-aware or unaware.

## Example
A men's body-wash brand was losing ground to a rival aimed at younger men. Digging into the data revealed that women did much of the category's buying for the men in their lives. The brand repositioned around that buyer: a confident, humorous pitch to the partner rather than the user, the famous Old Spice approach. Same product, new identity, and it moved from struggling to leading on marketing alone.

A hypothetical example at smaller scale: a posture-support brand is lost in a market full of generic "fix your back" ads. Its survey shows a cluster of dental hygienists and surgeons who stand bent over patients all day. Ads cast real clinicians, use their vocabulary and show the exact working posture. The product does not change; the buyer finally sees themselves in it.

## Common mistakes
- Trying to build a lifestyle brand from day one without the experience to execute it.
- Confusing a demographic with an identity.
- Picking an underserved group too small to scale.
- Persisting with a sophisticated avatar long after the evidence says your execution cannot reach it, or abandoning one after a couple of weak executions.
- Claiming an identity the product cannot actually serve.
